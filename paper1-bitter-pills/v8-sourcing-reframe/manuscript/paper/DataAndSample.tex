\section{Data and Sample}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data Sources}

We combine three administrative datasets covering January \BPsampleStartYear{} through December \BPsampleEndYear: (i) bid-level records from the BEC electronic procurement platform for all SES/SP purchases of medical supplies (BEC Group~\BPbecGroup), where each observation is a purchase-offer-item (POI) recording reference prices, bid prices, number of bidding firms, quantities, and tender outcomes; (ii) purchase-type classification, assigning each POI as ordinary, administrative, or litigated using a regular-expression algorithm applied to tender notices, which by law must reference any underlying court order;\footnote{Online Appendix \S A.7 documents the classifier's design and a stratified hand-labeling protocol over \BPregexNotices{} notices (\(\sim\)\BPregexPerClass{} per predicted class). The regex relies on literal court-order references that are present or absent independently of the price outcomes we estimate, so any residual misclassification attenuates rather than biases our estimates.} and (iii) health litigation records from S-CODES, the SES/SP database of health-related lawsuits, from which we derive SUS formulary status and injunction enforcement data. The binary outcome \emph{tender success} equals one if the POI closes with an accepted winning bid within its procurement order and zero otherwise; re-tendered items enter the data as new POIs and are not counted retroactively as successes. All continuous variables are winsorized at the \BPwinsorLow{}st and \BPwinsorHigh{}th percentiles; robustness to alternative winsorization is in the Appendix.

\subsection{Sample Construction}

The raw dataset comprises \BPnPOIfull{} POI observations across \BPnItemsFull{} distinct items, \BPnOrdersFull{} purchase orders, and \BPnPBUsObserved{} PBUs (descriptive statistics in Online Appendix, Table~A.1).

The analysis sample is restricted to items for which at least one ordinary and at least one litigated purchase is observed, ensuring within-item comparability. This yields \BPnAnalysisSample{} observations covering \BPnItemsAnalysis{} items.\footnote{Sample sizes vary slightly across tables due to missing values in specific dependent variables and the restriction to winners for price regressions.} For regressions using bid prices, we further restrict to winning bids (\BPnWinners{} observations).

Several features of the data are noteworthy. In levels, ordinary purchases have substantially higher mean reference prices (\BPmeanRefPriceOrd) compared to administrative (\BPmeanRefPriceAdm) and litigated (\BPmeanRefPriceLit) purchases, reflecting the mix of items across procurement types. However, the composition effect is absorbed by our item fixed effects. In logs---which form the basis of our regressions---litigated purchases show higher mean prices (\BPmeanLogRefLit{} for reference price, \BPmeanLogNegLit{} for negotiated price) than ordinary purchases (\BPmeanLogRefOrd{} and \BPmeanLogNegOrd), consistent with a price premium conditional on the item. Quantities are markedly lower for litigated purchases (mean of \BPmeanQtyLit{} units vs.\ \BPmeanQtyOrd{} for ordinary), reflecting the inability to aggregate demand under compressed timelines. The number of bidding firms is also lower for urgent purchases (\BPmeanFirmsLit{} for litigated vs.\ \BPmeanFirmsOrd{} for ordinary), suggesting reduced competition in the external phase.

The Online Appendix presents complementary geographic evidence on the spatial distribution of administrative demand, which mirrors the patterns documented for litigation in Section~\ref{sec:institutional}.

For the ``under the gun'' analysis, we construct a subsample of urgent purchases (administrative and litigated) for items with both types present, yielding \BPnUTG{} observations (balance statistics in Online Appendix, Table~A.2). The tightest specifications use item$\times$year-month cells: of \BPutgCellsItemYM{} such cells, \BPutgCellsBoth{} (\BPutgCellsBothPct) carry within-cell variation in regime; the remaining cells are absorbed as singletons under the fixed-effects design. Both-types cells are not a representative subsample of urgent items---they tilt toward higher-volume, more-reordered medications. Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:both_types_cells} compares both-types cells with singletons along reference price, modality, SUS-formulary status, and supplier base, and we discuss how this representativeness restriction interacts with the bounded UTG estimate in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}.
