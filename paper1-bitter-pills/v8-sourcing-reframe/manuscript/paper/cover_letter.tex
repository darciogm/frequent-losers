\opening{Dear Editors,}

We submit \emph{``Sanctions without Markups: Sourcing as the Margin of Bureaucratic Inefficiency''} for consideration as a \textbf{Short Paper}. Standard models of bureaucratic accountability predict that sanction-exposed officials face higher per-unit prices because suppliers extract a delivery-risk premium; passive-waste accounts predict that distortion operates through sourcing rather than pricing. The two predictions are observationally equivalent in cross-buyer data. Using bid-level data on \BPnPOIfull{} pharmaceutical purchases by the S\~ao Paulo State Department of Health (\BPsampleStartYear--\BPsampleEndYear), we provide the first within firm-buyer-item test of these competing predictions. The within-firm prediction fails; the sourcing prediction holds.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Findings.}
Within the same item, urgent purchases are \BPnegPctHeadline{} more expensive and attract \BPfirmsPctHeadline{} fewer bidders, yet succeed \BPsuccessPP{} more often. An institutional contrast unique to S\~ao Paulo since~\BPsampleStartYear---a parallel administrative-request channel that shares all planning constraints but carries no penalty for officials---separates the sanction effect from urgency. Manski-Lee bounds, correcting for SES/SP committee selection on cost-effectiveness, place the under-the-gun gap at $[\BPutgBoundLow, \BPutgBoundHigh]$. Within firm-buyer-item triples observed under both regimes, the same firm prices the same item to the same buyer at a statistically indistinguishable level ($\BPutgTripleCoefUTG$, SE~$\BPutgTripleSEUTG$, NULL): the cost margin operates entirely through demand fragmentation (admin orders are roughly \BPutgQtyAdminFoldPref$\times$ larger, mechanically delivering bulk discounts) and through equilibrium changes in \emph{which} firm wins. Wild cluster bootstrap confirms inference ($p_{\mathrm{boot}}=\BPutgBoottestPval$); items never litigated deliver placebo zeros. Welfare bound: \BPwelfareBound{} per year on \BPwelfareBoundAnnualSpend{} of annual litigated spending. The policy lever is demand aggregation, not contract design.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{JPubE fit.}
The paper engages procurement design and frictions (Bandiera, Prat \& Valletti 2009; Bosio et al.\ 2022; Best, Hjort \& Szakonyi 2023), accountability-induced bureaucratic distortion (Prendergast 2007; Bandiera et al.\ 2021), and judicial enforcement of public contracts (Coviello et al.\ 2018). It directly engages two recent JPubE contributions: Lin and Wang (2025), whose 4.8\% supply-cost reductions from group purchasing in U.S.\ hospitals identify the same demand-aggregation lever we find compromised by court-mandated fragmentation; and Coviello, Guglielmo, Lotti and Spagnolo (2026), whose evidence on threshold manipulation identifies sourcing distortion via a different institutional mechanism (discretionary procedures) than the one we study (compressed timelines under sanctions).
