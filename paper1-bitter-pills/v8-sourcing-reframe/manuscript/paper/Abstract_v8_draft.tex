\begin{abstract}
Judicial enforcement is essential for the rule of law, yet the
\emph{design} of enforcement can generate economic inefficiency.
We provide bid-level evidence on the procurement cost of court-mandated
pharmaceutical purchases in S\~ao Paulo, Brazil
(\BPsampleStartYear--\BPsampleEndYear). With item, time, and buyer fixed
effects, urgent purchases carry negotiated prices \BPnegPctHeadline{}
higher and attract \BPfirmsPctHeadline{} fewer bidders than ordinary
purchases of the same item; they also succeed \BPsuccessPP{} more often.
Within urgent purchases, a parallel administrative-request channel that
shares all planning constraints but carries no penalty for officials when
delivery fails offers an institutional contrast: an ``under the gun''
(UTG) comparison between litigated and administrative purchases of the
same item. Naively, the UTG gap is large, but it is contaminated by
selection on cost-effectiveness criteria into the administrative channel.
Selection-bias bounds (Manski-Lee, with a Heckman cross-check on the
committee acceptance rule) put the UTG gap at \BPutgBoundLow--\BPutgBoundHigh,
with a preferred bounded estimate of \BPutgPointBounded.
The economically interesting finding is what produces the gap.
Within firm-buyer-item triples observed in both regimes, the same firm
sells the same item to the same buyer at a slightly \emph{lower} per-unit
price under urgency (\BPtripleFirmBuyerItemPct, $p<0.01$): there is
\emph{no} within-firm markup. The entire price margin operates through
equilibrium changes in \emph{which} firm wins (supplier composition shift
under compressed timelines) and through demand fragmentation (smaller
orders forgoing bulk discounts). Accountability-driven sourcing
constraints, not price discrimination, are what make compliance expensive.
The findings speak to a general design problem: when non-completion is
punished more severely than overpayment, sourcing efficiency is what
gives way.
\end{abstract}
