\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In Brazil, courts compel state governments to purchase specific
medications when patients win right-to-health lawsuits. Compliance
is enforced through personal fines, civil liability, and asset
seizure on procurement officials who fail to deliver---but no
analogous penalty attaches to officials who deliver at high prices.
The result, in S\~ao Paulo alone, is roughly
\BPwelfareBoundAnnualSpend{} of pharmaceutical purchases per year
subject to a one-sided accountability regime. Two theoretical
predictions about how this regime distorts procurement diverge.
Standard accountability models \citep{prendergast2007bureaucrats}
predict suppliers extract delivery-risk premia from sanctioned
officials; passive-waste accounts \citep{bandiera2009active}
predict the buyer's sourcing pattern shifts. The two are
observationally equivalent in cross-buyer data, where
buyer-supplier match heterogeneity confounds within-firm and
between-firm margins. We provide a within firm-buyer-item test
that resolves the horse-race in a setting where accountability
pressure bites hardest.

We use the universe of pharmaceutical procurement by the S\~ao Paulo
State Department of Health (SES/SP) from \BPsampleStartYear{} through
\BPsampleEndYear: \BPnPOIfull{} purchase-offer-item observations on
the state's electronic procurement platform (BEC), mandatory for
common goods since \BPbecMandatorySinceYear. The identification asset
is institutional. Since \BPsampleStartYear{} the SES/SP scientific
committee has run an administrative-request channel parallel to the
courts, with the same compressed timelines
(\BPurgentPlanLow--\BPurgentPlanHigh{} days), the same small
quantities, the same diverted budget, and the same auction procedures
on BEC. The two channels differ only in penalty exposure: failure to
deliver under a court order triggers personal fines, liability, and
fund seizure for the procurement officer; failure to deliver under
the administrative channel triggers nothing. To our knowledge, no
other Brazilian state operates a comparable dual channel for urgent
pharmaceutical demand.

Three findings emerge. There is no within-firm markup in deep
markets: within firm-buyer-item triples observed under both regimes
(\BPutgTripleCountUTG{} triples, \BPutgTripleNUTG{} observations),
the Admin coefficient is $\hat\beta=\BPutgTripleCoefUTG$
(SE $\BPutgTripleSEUTG$), statistically indistinguishable from zero.
The null is robust on above-median-quantity items, SUS-formulary
items, and the later period; it breaks on thin-supplier subsamples
(below-median quantity, earlier period), where the incumbent does
extract a sanction premium consistent with
\citet{prendergast2007bureaucrats}. The deep-market within-firm null
is the central finding; the thin-market deviation locates where the
delivery-risk-premium prediction does bite. The bounded under-the-gun
gap is modest: under Manski-Lee monotone restrictions on committee
selection, the gap lies in
$[\BPutgBoundLow, \BPutgBoundHigh]$, at the lower end of
within-buyer procurement-cost dispersion documented in comparable
settings \citep[e.g.,][]{best2023procurement, bosio2022public}.
And the gap operates through demand fragmentation and a sourcing
shift: administrative orders are roughly
\BPutgQtyAdminFoldPref$\times$ larger than litigated orders; the
bulk-discount channel mechanically delivers $\BPutgMechanicalCone$ in
admin-minus-litigated log price. Among item-buyer pairs observed
under both regimes, the modal winning firm differs across regimes in
\BPwinnerSwitchDiffModalPct{} of pairs; mean Jaccard similarity of
the winner sets is \BPwinnerSwitchJaccardMean. Sanctions reorganize
the supplier set far more than they reorganize the incumbent's price
(Figure~\ref{fig:cascade}, Table~\ref{tab:winner_switch}).

These findings depend on bounded admin-channel selection. The
scientific committee admits on cost-effectiveness criteria, and a
pre-period probit confirms that admin over-represents items that
would have been cheaper under any regime: the coefficient on
pre-period log reference price is \BPutgProbitRefPriceCoef{}
(\BPutgProbitRefPricePval), and on SUS-formulary status
\BPutgProbitSUSCoef{} (\BPutgProbitSUSPval). The Lee bound corrects
for this wedge.\footnote{A parametric Heckman correction is
non-identified in this design (Online
Appendix~\ref{tab:utg_heckman}).} Wild cluster bootstrap on the
preferred-FE Admin coefficient gives
$p_{\mathrm{boot}}=\BPutgBoottestPval$ (\BPutgBoottestB{} Rademacher
replicates; 95\% log-point CI
$[\BPutgBoottestCIlow, \BPutgBoottestCIhigh]$). A
Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess event study with a Rambachan-Roth
\citep{rambachan2023more} honest-sensitivity overlay is supportive
in the Appendix; the cross-sectional fixed-effects design is
primary. A placebo on items never subject to litigation returns
economically and statistically zero coefficients
($\BPplaceboNegCoef$, SE $\BPplaceboNegSE$ for negotiated prices):
the urgency premium exists only inside the litigation pool.

The central contribution is conceptual: identifying which margin
of public procurement efficiency cedes under accountability that
punishes non-delivery one-sidedly. The sourcing-vs-pricing
distinction is the empirical lever---the cross-buyer literature
cannot separate within-firm pricing from equilibrium supplier
selection; the within firm-buyer-item triple does, and isolates
sourcing under fragmentation as the empirical signature of
accountability-induced inefficiency. Two supporting contributions
follow. Whereas \citet{coviello2018court}
document timeline \emph{extension} as a procurement cost driver, we
identify timeline \emph{compression} under sanctions as an alternative
channel, with the cost concentrated where demand aggregation matters
most. The finding directly engages recent JPubE evidence on group
purchasing in health \citep{lin2025value}, which identifies the same
demand-aggregation lever we find compromised by court mandates. The
paper engages procurement design and frictions \citep{bandiera2009active,
best2023procurement, bosio2022public, decarolis2018buyer,
carril2026competition}, accountability-induced bureaucratic distortion
\citep{prendergast2007bureaucrats, bandiera2021allocation}, and the
judicialization of health in Latin America \citep{wang2015right,
ferraz2009right, biehl2009will}. Methodologically, the design honors
post-treatment-bias \citep{acharya2016explaining}: quantity- and
firm-fixed-effect controls are read as channel-isolating rather than
direct-effect estimators. Applied to the \BPwelfareBoundAnnualSpend{}
of annual litigated spending in S\~ao Paulo with an admissibility
calibration of \BPwelfareBoundAdmissibility, the bounded UTG implies a
per-unit-price welfare cost of \BPwelfareBound{} per year (Lee range
$[\BPwelfareBoundCIlow, \BPwelfareBoundCIhigh]$). The policy lever is
demand aggregation, not contract design.
