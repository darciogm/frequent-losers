\begin{abstract}
Judicial enforcement is essential for the rule of law, yet the
\emph{design} of enforcement can generate economic inefficiency.
Using bid-level administrative data on \BPnPOIfull{} pharmaceutical
procurement orders by the S\~ao Paulo State Department of Health
(\BPsampleStartYear--\BPsampleEndYear), we ask how court-mandated
procurement raises prices and through which mechanism. Within the
same item, urgent purchases carry negotiated prices
\BPnegPctHeadline{} higher and attract \BPfirmsPctHeadlineAbs{}
fewer bidders, yet succeed \BPsuccessPP{} more often than ordinary
purchases. Within urgent procurement, a parallel
administrative-request channel that shares all planning constraints
but carries no penalty for officials offers an institutional
contrast. The administrative subsample is selected by an SES/SP
scientific committee on cost-effectiveness criteria; Manski-Lee
bounds put the selection-corrected ``under the gun'' gap at
$[\BPutgBoundLow, \BPutgBoundHigh]$, broadly consistent with
within-buyer procurement-cost dispersion documented in comparable
settings \citep[e.g.,][]{best2023procurement,bosio2022public}.
\emph{The mechanism is sourcing, not pricing.} Within firm-buyer-item
triples observed under both regimes, the same firm prices the same
item to the same buyer at a statistically indistinguishable level
($\hat\beta=\BPutgTripleCoefUTG$, $\mathrm{SE}=\BPutgTripleSEUTG$):
there is no within-firm markup. The price margin operates through
demand fragmentation---administrative orders are roughly
\BPutgQtyAdminFoldPref$\times$ larger than litigated orders,
mechanically delivering bulk discounts---and through equilibrium
changes in \emph{which} firm wins each contract. The per-unit-price
cost of the litigated regime is bounded at \BPwelfareBound{} per
year (Lee range $[\BPwelfareBoundCIlow, \BPwelfareBoundCIhigh]$) on
the \BPwelfareBoundAnnualSpend{} of annual litigated spending in
S\~ao Paulo. The policy lever is demand aggregation, not contract
design.
\end{abstract}
