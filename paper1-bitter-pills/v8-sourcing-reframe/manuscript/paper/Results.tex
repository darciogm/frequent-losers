\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the results around the central conceptual contrast of the
paper: the urgency premium operates through \emph{sourcing} (which firm
wins, what order size is feasible) and not through within-firm
\emph{pricing}. We first document the headline urgent-vs-ordinary
patterns, then bound the under-the-gun (UTG) gap under selection,
then present the within firm-buyer-item null that pivots the
interpretation, and finally reconcile the bounded UTG with its
mechanical components.

\subsection{Urgency raises prices and thins competition; tenders still clear}
\label{sec:main_results}

Urgent purchases carry negotiated prices \BPnegPctHeadline{} higher
than ordinary purchases of the same item, with the same item attracting
\BPfirmsPctHeadline{} fewer bidding firms. Reference prices set during
the planning phase rise by \BPrefPctPreferred{} in the preferred
specification ($p<0.10$); the raw item-FE-only premium is
\BPrefPctItemFE, attenuating sharply once buyer fixed effects absorb
buyer-level heterogeneity. Negotiated prices show the same pattern,
ranging from \BPnegPctItemFE{} with item FE alone to \BPnegPctRange{}
with our preferred specifications.

Despite worse conditions, urgent tenders \emph{succeed} more often:
\BPsuccessPP{} ($p<0.01$). This rules out the alternative that urgent
purchases are simply cancelled when the bid distribution is unfavourable;
officials accept worse terms to ensure compliance. The contrast with
the cross-buyer dispersion benchmarks in the literature
(Bandiera-Prat-Valletti 2009: \BPbpvBaseline; Best-Hjort-Szakonyi 2023:
\BPbhsBaseline; Bosio et al.\ 2022: \BPbosioBaseline; Coviello-Guglielmo-Spagnolo
2018: \BPcgsBaseline) places our \BPnegPctHeadline{} within-item
premium at the lower end of plausible passive-waste magnitudes; the
same-buyer, same-item contrast is tighter than typical cross-buyer
dispersion, so a smaller headline number is consistent with the design.

\subsection{The under-the-gun comparison and selection bounds}
\label{sec:utg_bounds}

The naive UTG comparison contrasts litigated and administrative urgent
purchases of the same item. Both share planning constraints; they
differ only in penalty exposure. The naive coefficient on
\textit{Admin} is $\BPutgCoef$ ($p<0.05$) under the preferred
specification, implying litigated purchases are \BPutgPointNaive{} more
expensive than administrative ones. Wild cluster bootstrap inference
(Rademacher weights, $B=\BPutgBoottestB$) confirms the result with
$p=\BPutgBoottestPval$ and a 95\% CI in log-points of
$[\BPutgBoottestCIlow, \BPutgBoottestCIhigh]$.

This comparison is contaminated by selection into the administrative
channel: the SES/SP scientific committee admits cases on cost-effectiveness
criteria. The committee's selection rule, recovered from a probit on
pre-period item characteristics, is informative
($N=\BPutgFirstStageN$): items with higher pre-period reference prices
are less likely to be admitted (probit coefficient $-0.051$, $p<0.001$);
SUS-formulary items are less likely (probit coefficient $-0.265$,
$p<0.001$). Admin therefore over-represents items that would
have been priced lower under \emph{any} regime.

We bound the selection wedge using Lee (2009) trimming within
item$\times$year$\times$PBU strata where the admin sample exceeds the
litigated count. Trimming admin observations from the top of the
within-stratum price distribution gives the lower bound; trimming from
the bottom gives the upper bound. Table~\ref{tab:utg_lee_bounds} reports
the bounded UTG.

The bounded UTG falls in $[\BPutgBoundLow, \BPutgBoundHigh]$, well below
the naive \BPutgPointNaive. Selection on observables explains
approximately one-third of the headline gap.\footnote{We also report
a Heckman parametric correction with the same first-stage probit
(Online Appendix Table~\ref{tab:utg_heckman}). The Mills ratio is
collinear with the second-stage fixed effects; the resulting
Admin coefficient ($\BPutgHeckmanCoef$, SE $\BPutgHeckmanSE$) is
uninformative. We treat the Lee bound as the primary
selection-corrected object and the Heckman estimate as a sensitivity
diagnostic that flags identification fragility, not as a competing
point estimate.} The within-cell variation that identifies the tightest
specification comes from a non-representative subsample (Online
Appendix Table~\ref{tab:both_types_cells}): of \BPutgCellsItemYM{}
item$\times$month cells, only \BPbothCellN{} (about 11\%) carry
within-cell variation and they tilt toward higher-volume,
more-frequent SUS-formulary medications. Bounded UTG estimates
therefore speak to the lower-volume liquid items in the sample, not to
the universe of court-mandated purchases.

\subsection{Same firm, same buyer, same item: no within-firm markup}
\label{sec:within_firm_null}

The interpretation of the bounded UTG hinges on \emph{how} the gap is
generated. We separate within-firm pricing from equilibrium supplier
selection by restricting to firm-buyer-item triples observed under both
the litigated and the administrative regime within urgent procurement.
There are \BPutgTripleCountUTG{} such triples covering \BPutgTripleNUTG{}
observations.

The within firm-buyer-item Admin coefficient is
$\BPutgTripleCoefUTG$ (SE $\BPutgTripleSEUTG$), \emph{not significantly
different from zero}. The same firm sells the same item to the same
buyer at essentially the same per-unit price under both regimes. There
is no within-firm markup under sanctions. Whatever distinguishes
litigated from administrative urgent procurement, it is not opportunistic
pricing by suppliers that already serve the buyer's procurement.

This finding is the empirical pivot of the paper. In the standard
accountability-distortion intuition \citep{prendergast2007bureaucrats},
sanction-exposed officials would face higher per-unit prices because
suppliers extract a premium for delivery risk. In our setting, that
mechanism is empirically absent.

\subsection{Reconciling the bounded UTG: mechanical fragmentation, sourcing residual}
\label{sec:reconciliation}

We decompose the observed log-price gap (admin minus litigated) into
three components linked by an arithmetic identity:
\begin{equation*}
\underbrace{\Delta y_{\text{obs}}}_{-0.259}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\beta_{\text{bulk}} \cdot \Delta \ln Q_{\text{adm-lit}}}_{\text{mechanical C1: } -0.397}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\beta_{\text{within-firm}}}_{\text{within-firm: } +0.035}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\beta_{\text{composition}}}_{\text{residual: } +0.103}.
\end{equation*}
Table~\ref{tab:utg_reconciliation} reports the three components and
their implied per-unit-price magnitudes.

Three readings follow. \emph{First}, the mechanical bulk-discount channel
($\BPutgMechanicalCone$, admin cheaper by 32.8\% from order-size differences
alone) over-explains the observed gap. The qty channel pulls the
admin-vs-lit price gap below what we observe.

\emph{Second}, the within firm-buyer-item offset is small and not
significant ($\BPutgWithinFirmOffset$, NULL). Within-firm pricing
behavior is not where the residual wedge lives.

\emph{Third}, the supplier composition residual is positive
($\BPutgCompositionResidual$): holding quantity constant and within
firms not observed in both regimes, the litigated supplier mix is
\emph{cheaper} by about 11\% than the administrative supplier mix.
This reverses the sign one might expect under naive intuition: the
sanction-exposed regime forces sourcing onto a different (and on
average less expensive per unit) point of the supplier distribution,
but the bulk-discount disadvantage of small lit orders dominates,
producing the net 23\% lit-over-admin observed gap. The cost of
sanctions, in this setting, is a sourcing-and-aggregation cost, not a
pricing cost.

\subsection{Falsification: items never litigated}
\label{sec:placebo}

If the urgency premium reflects judicial pressure rather than unobserved
item characteristics, it should be absent for items that are never
subject to court orders. Running the preferred specification on items
with zero litigated purchases across \BPsampleStartYear--\BPsampleEndYear{}
(restricted to those with both ordinary and administrative purchases)
yields placebo coefficients of $\BPplaceboNegCoef$ (SE
$\BPplaceboNegSE$) for negotiated prices and $\BPplaceboRefCoef$ (SE
$\BPplaceboRefSE$) for reference prices. Both are economically small
and statistically insignificant (Online Appendix
Table~\ref{tab:placebo}). The urgency premium exists only for items
that are targets of litigation.

\subsection{Where the cost bites hardest}
\label{sec:heterogeneity}

Splitting items by mean number of participating firms (median
\BPhetHHIMedianValue), competitive items show a \BPhetHHIlowBaselinePct{}
urgency premium with \BPhetHHIlowFirmFEPct{} surviving firm fixed
effects (\BPhetHHIlowAttnPct\% attenuation). Concentrated items show no
detectable premium (\BPhetHHIhighBaselinePct, not significant). The
pattern matches the sourcing reading: urgency erodes competitive
pressure where there is competitive pressure to erode. Where the buyer
has no outside option in either regime, urgency carries little
additional cost.

\subsection{Summary of effects}
\label{sec:summary}

Across the full sample, the \BPnegPctHeadline{} urgent-vs-ordinary
premium reflects compressed planning timelines and reduced bidder
participation. Within urgent procurement, the bounded UTG falls in
$[\BPutgBoundLow, \BPutgBoundHigh]$ once selection into the
administrative channel is corrected. The bounded UTG itself decomposes
into a mechanical bulk-discount fragmentation channel, a null within-firm
pricing component, and a positive supplier composition residual.
Same firms price the same items the same way regardless of regime.
Different firms win urgent contracts under the two regimes, and the
sourcing shift is the empirical signature of accountability-induced
inefficiency in our data.
