Referee Report

"Bitter Pills to Swallow: The Enforcement Costs of Health Litigation"
Authors: D. Genicolo-Martins & P. Furquim de Azevedo Institution: INSPER Date: February 2026
Recommendation: Revise and Resubmit (Major Revision)

1. Summary

This paper investigates the fiscal costs imposed by judicial enforcement of health litigation on public procurement in São Paulo, Brazil (2009–2019). Court orders compel the government to make urgent purchases of medicines under adverse conditions: tight deadlines, small quantities, and threat of sanctions against public officials. The authors compare urgent (litigated and administrative) vs. ordinary purchases and find that urgent purchases result in prices 30–44% higher, 32% fewer participating firms, and 39–49% lower success rates.

A novel contribution is the "under the gun" effect: comparing litigated vs. administrative urgent purchases (both urgent, but only the former carries penalties), the authors estimate an additional 9–10% price premium attributable to the sanction channel.

The paper addresses an important and policy-relevant question at the intersection of law and economics, public procurement, and health policy. The institutional setting is rich and well-described. However, the paper has significant weaknesses in identification, methodology, and presentation that must be addressed before publication.

2. Strengths

Strength A — Important and novel research question

The paper fills a genuine gap in the literature. While there is extensive work on procurement inefficiency (Bandiera et al., 2009) and health litigation in Brazil (Wang, 2015), no prior study quantifies the procurement cost channel with this level of granularity. The decomposition into urgency costs vs. sanction costs ("under the gun") is a creative and valuable contribution.

Strength B — Rich institutional setting

The Brazilian context is well-suited for this analysis. The SES/SP operates 99 PBUs under a single e-procurement platform (BEC), creating a natural laboratory where identical institutional rules apply to both ordinary and urgent purchases. The description of the institutional environment is thorough and informative.

Strength C — Unique and comprehensive dataset

Matching procurement-level data from BEC with litigation records from S-CODES over 11 years is a significant data contribution. The scale (~3.9M transactions, ~19K firms) provides ample statistical power.

Strength D — Clear empirical findings

The results are striking, consistent across specifications, and economically significant. The magnitude of the inefficiencies documented is large enough to warrant policy attention.

3. Major Concerns

3.1. Identification Strategy

Concern A — Exogeneity claim needs strengthening

The paper argues that judicial orders are exogenous shocks to procurement, invoking the "principle of impersonality." This is the central identifying assumption, but the argument is largely institutional and lacks formal testing. Key concerns:

  • Selection on items: Even with item fixed effects, there may be time-varying selection. Items that are litigated at a particular moment might differ systematically from the same items purchased ordinarily (e.g., during supply shortages, epidemics, or regulatory changes). The paper should provide evidence that the timing of litigation is uncorrelated with market conditions for specific drugs.
  • Selection on PBUs: PBUs facing more litigation may be systematically different — larger, more urban, or facing different local health conditions. PBU fixed effects help, but time-varying PBU characteristics could still confound.
  • Formal falsification tests are absent. The paper provides no placebo tests, pre-trend analysis, or event-study design around the timing of court orders. An event study showing that prices for a given item at a given PBU spike precisely when a court order arrives (and not before) would be extremely valuable for establishing causality.
Concern B — Endogenous control variable

Log quantity is included as a control in the price regressions (Tables 6–7, 10). However, quantity is a choice variable determined in the internal phase — precisely the phase the paper argues is disrupted by urgency. This creates a classic "bad controls" problem (Angrist and Pischke, 2009). Urgency affects quantities, and quantities affect prices. By controlling for quantities, the coefficient on "urgent" captures only the residual price effect after netting out the quantity channel, which is one of the main mechanisms. The paper should:

  • Present specifications with and without quantity controls, clearly labeling the former as capturing the "direct" effect and the latter as the "total" effect.
  • Consider an instrumental variables approach for quantities if the goal is to isolate the price effect conditional on quantities.
Concern C — "Under the gun" identification has selection concerns

Administrative requests go through a scientific committee and represent only 6.5% of urgent purchases. This raises concerns:

  • Drugs approved through the administrative channel may be systematically different (more standard, better evidence base, potentially more competitive markets).
  • The choice between judicial and administrative channels is endogenous — individuals choose to litigate vs. request administratively based on unobservable characteristics.
  • The paper should provide evidence that the item composition and market characteristics are comparable between the two groups. A balance table would be essential.

3.2. Econometric Issues

Concern D — Standard errors

The paper does not discuss clustering. Given the panel structure (repeated purchases within PBUs, within items, over time), standard errors should be clustered at least at the PBU level or PBU-item level. Without clustering, the reported standard errors are likely severely underestimated, and statistical significance may be overstated. This is a critical omission for any empirical microeconomics paper.

Concern E — Missing descriptive statistics

There is no summary statistics table. The tables jump from Table 1 (types of purchases, which is actually a figure) to Table 4. The paper needs at minimum:

  • A table of means and standard deviations for all key variables, separately for ordinary, administrative, and litigated purchases.
  • A balance table showing observable characteristics across groups.
  • Information on the distribution of observations across PBUs, items, and time.
Concern F — R-squared variation

The R-squared drops dramatically across specifications (e.g., from 0.879 to 0.339 in Table 6 when moving from OLS to FE). This suggests that item-level variation explains the vast majority of price variation. The paper should discuss what this means for the interpretation of results and whether within-item variation is sufficient to estimate the effects of interest reliably.

3.3. Missing Analyses

Concern G — No heterogeneity analysis

A top journal would expect exploration of how effects vary by:

  • Drug type (SUS-list vs. non-SUS, high-cost vs. low-cost, chronic vs. acute)
  • PBU size and experience with litigation
  • Time period (has the effect changed as PBUs gain experience with litigation?)
  • Market concentration (items with many vs. few potential suppliers)
  • Procurement modality (pregão vs. convite)
Concern H — No aggregate welfare/fiscal cost calculation

The paper has all the ingredients to compute the total fiscal cost of health litigation (price premium × quantity × number of litigated purchases), yet never does this calculation. A back-of-the-envelope estimate would dramatically strengthen the policy relevance.

Concern I — No dynamic analysis

Has the "under the gun" effect changed over time? Did the introduction of administrative requests in 2009 reduce costs? Are PBUs learning to cope with litigation?

Concern J — Machine learning analysis mentioned but not presented

Section 4 mentions using "supervised machine learning" to analyze court decision texts, but no results are shown. Either present the results or remove the reference.

3.4. Theoretical Model

Concern K — Model lacks depth

The commented-out theoretical model is too mechanical to add value. The "proofs" are definitional — they follow directly from the assumed price structure. The model assumes the penalty markup enters as π · γ without micro-founding this result. A more careful model would:

  • Derive supplier bidding strategies as a function of the known urgency/penalty structure.
  • Generate predictions about how the "under the gun" effect varies with market characteristics.
  • Explain the mechanism through which public knowledge of litigation translates into higher prices (supplier strategic behavior vs. PBU reduced bargaining effort).

I would recommend either substantially developing the model or keeping it out and focusing on the empirical contribution.

4. Minor Issues

Minor A — Literature review

The literature review is too long and too generic. Paragraphs on strategic management (Barney, 1991; Eisenhardt, 1989; Grant, 1996) feel out of place in an economics paper. The review should be tightened to focus on: (i) judicial enforcement and procurement, (ii) procurement efficiency/waste, and (iii) health litigation. Consider integrating the review into the introduction, which is standard in economics journals.

Minor B — Introduction previews too much

The introduction presents all numerical results in detail, making the empirical section feel repetitive. Consider presenting only the qualitative direction of results in the introduction and leaving magnitudes for the results section.

Minor C — Conclusion is too brief

Seven lines is insufficient for any journal. The conclusion should discuss: limitations of the study, specific policy recommendations, agenda for future research, and how findings relate to the broader literature on judicial intervention in policy.

Minor D — Table numbering gap

Tables are numbered 4–10, but Tables 1–3 are absent. This suggests missing descriptive statistics tables.

Minor E — REGEX methodology

The paper relies on regular expressions to classify purchases as litigated. Misclassification could bias results. The paper should report the validation of this approach (precision, recall, manual audit of a subsample).

Minor F — Data section ends abruptly

The Data and Sample Definition section (40 lines) feels incomplete relative to the richness of the data. More detail on sample construction, exclusion criteria, and variable definitions would help replicability.

Minor G — Version date

The paper states "April 2025" as current version date, which should be updated.

5. Specific Suggestions for Revision

  1. Add a comprehensive descriptive statistics table with means, standard deviations, and observation counts for all key variables, split by purchase type (ordinary / administrative / litigated).
  2. Add a balance table comparing observable characteristics of litigated vs. administrative purchases.
  3. Cluster standard errors at the PBU level (or PBU-item level) and report robustness to alternative clustering schemes.
  4. Present an event-study design around court orders to validate the exogeneity assumption.
  5. Show results with and without quantity controls, discussing the "total" vs. "direct" effect interpretation.
  6. Add heterogeneity analyses by drug type, PBU characteristics, time period, and market structure.
  7. Compute aggregate fiscal costs of health litigation using the estimated price premiums.
  8. Substantially expand the conclusion with limitations, policy implications, and future research.
  9. Tighten the literature review and consider merging it with the introduction.
  10. Either develop the theoretical model rigorously or remove it, focusing on the empirical contribution.

7. Final Assessment

The paper asks an important question, exploits a compelling institutional setting, and presents economically significant results. However, the identification strategy needs formal validation, the econometric implementation has gaps (standard errors, bad controls), and the paper needs substantial additions (descriptive statistics, heterogeneity, welfare calculations) to meet the standards of a top field journal.

With these revisions, the paper has strong potential.
