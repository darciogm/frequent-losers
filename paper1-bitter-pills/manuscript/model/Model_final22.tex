\begin{abstract}
This appendix develops a theoretical model to examine the welfare impact of judicial mandates on public procurement in the health sector. Judicial mandates requiring urgent procurement often disrupt standard procurement, reduce supplier competition, and increase costs. By incorporating probabilistic supplier entry and stochastic judicial penalties, we analyze welfare losses across procurement types—ordinary, urgent, and litigated urgent. Our findings suggest that judiciary-executive coordination could reduce procurement inefficiencies and highlight the significant role that judicial mandates play in shaping public procurement costs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Judicial mandates have a growing influence on public procurement, particularly in the health sector, where courts often compel governments to procure medical supplies urgently to meet critical needs. In Brazil, such mandates frequently bypass standard procurement processes, forcing acquisitions that are conducted under time constraints, with fewer participants and often at higher costs. This paper presents a theoretical model that formalizes these disruptions by modeling judicially mandated urgent procurement as an auction with probabilistic supplier entry and stochastic judicial penalties.

Our primary contribution is to develop a framework that quantifies welfare losses across different procurement types—ordinary, urgent, and litigated urgent. Using auction theory, welfare analysis, and comparative statics, we demonstrate how judicial mandates increase procurement costs and reduce welfare by altering standard procurement processes. The policy implications of our findings highlight the need for coordinated actions between judicial and executive branches to mitigate procurement inefficiencies.

\section{Model Framework}

\subsection{Assumptions}
The model relies on the following assumptions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Public Buyer (Government):} The government seeks to minimize the cost of procuring health-related goods while meeting urgent public health demands. The buyer faces two procurement regimes: \textit{Ordinary Procurement (O)} and \textit{Urgent Procurement (U)}.
    \item \textbf{Suppliers:} Suppliers are assumed to operate under constant marginal costs. Each supplier \( i \) has a marginal cost \( c_i \), and participation in procurement auctions is probabilistic. The entry probability is \( p_O \) in ordinary procurement and \( p_U < p_O \) in urgent procurement, reflecting higher operational constraints and risks in urgent scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Judiciary:} The judiciary acts as a stochastic enforcer, intervening with probability \( \pi \), and imposing penalties \( \gamma \) if procurement conditions are not met. Judicial mandates introduce additional urgency in the procurement process, raising the equilibrium price through reduced competition and penalty-driven markups.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Procurement Types and Price Dynamics}
The model identifies three procurement types that determine the level of competition, expected prices, and welfare impacts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ordinary Procurement (O):} This scenario involves planned procurement with ample time for competitive bidding. The expected number of suppliers participating is \( E[N_O] = N_O \cdot p_O \). The large quantity procured, denoted by \( Q_O \), and competitive environment yield a lower equilibrium price \( P_O \) due to economies of scale.
    
    \item \textbf{Urgent Procurement (U):} Judicial mandates may require urgent procurement, which occurs with smaller quantities \( Q_U < Q_O \). The probability of supplier entry is lower, \( p_U < p_O \), reducing expected competition \( E[N_U] = N_U \cdot p_U \). As a result, the urgency-driven equilibrium price is given by:
    \[
    P_U = c + \delta,
    \]
    where \( \delta > 0 \) represents the markup due to urgency and reduced competition.
    
    \item \textbf{Litigated Urgent Procurement (Litigated U):} When judicial penalties \( \gamma \) are imposed on the government for failing to meet procurement deadlines, suppliers perceive additional urgency. The penalty markup increases the equilibrium price to:
    \[
    P_{Litigated} = c + \delta + \pi \cdot \gamma,
    \]
    reflecting both the urgency and penalty effects.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theoretical Results and Equilibrium Analysis}

\subsection{Expected Price Derivation Across Procurement Types}
We derive the expected prices for each procurement type using auction theory and probabilistic supplier entry:

\subsubsection{Ordinary Procurement Equilibrium Price}
With high supplier entry probability \( p_O \) and expected participation \( N_O \cdot p_O \), we assume a competitive equilibrium that approaches marginal cost \( c \):
\[
P_O = \min(b_i^{O}) = c + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \to 0 \text{ as } N_O \cdot p_O \to \infty.
\]

\subsubsection{Urgent Procurement Equilibrium Price}
In urgent procurement, reduced competition due to lower \( p_U \) and expected participation \( N_U \cdot p_U \) results in a higher equilibrium price with an urgency markup:
\[
P_U = \min(b_i^{U}) = c + \delta, \quad \delta > \epsilon.
\]

\subsubsection{Litigated Urgent Procurement Equilibrium Price}
Judicially imposed penalties \( \gamma \), applied with probability \( \pi \), further increase the price due to perceived urgency and the risk of non-compliance penalties:
\[
P_{Litigated} = \min(b_i^{Litigated}) = c + \delta + \pi \cdot \gamma.
\]

\subsection{Theorem 1: Expected Price Hierarchy Across Procurement Types}

\textbf{Statement:} Judicially mandated urgent procurement yields the highest equilibrium price, establishing the following hierarchy:
\[
\mathbb{E}[P_{Litigated}] > \mathbb{E}[P_U] > \mathbb{E}[P_O].
\]

\textbf{Proof:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Ordinary Procurement:} Under ordinary conditions, high supplier participation leads to an equilibrium price close to the marginal cost, \( P_O \approx c \).
    
    \item \textit{Urgent Procurement:} Fewer suppliers and urgency create a markup \( \delta \), yielding \( P_U = c + \delta \).
    
    \item \textit{Litigated Procurement:} The presence of judicial penalties introduces an additional markup, giving:
    \[
    P_{Litigated} = c + \delta + \pi \cdot \gamma.
    \]
    Therefore, the expected hierarchy is:
    \[
    \mathbb{E}[P_{Litigated}] > \mathbb{E}[P_U] > \mathbb{E}[P_O].
    \]
\end{enumerate}

\section{Welfare Analysis and Expected Welfare Loss}
Social welfare \( SW \) is defined as the net benefit from procurement, accounting for the difference between the public valuation and procurement costs. We evaluate welfare under each procurement type.

\subsection{Welfare in Ordinary Procurement}
\[
EW_O = Q_O \cdot (V - P_O),
\]
where \( V \) is the public valuation of health goods. Welfare is maximized under ordinary procurement due to high competition and lower costs.

\subsection{Welfare in Urgent Procurement}
Urgent procurement results in lower welfare due to urgency-driven costs:
\[
EW_U = Q_U \cdot (V - P_U).
\]
The welfare loss from ordinary to urgent procurement is:
\[
\Delta W_U = EW_O - EW_U = Q_U \cdot (P_U - P_O).
\]

\subsection{Welfare in Litigated Urgent Procurement}
Judicial penalties further reduce welfare due to increased costs:
\[
EW_{Litigated} = Q_U \cdot (V - P_{Litigated}).
\]
The welfare loss under judicial mandates is given by:
\[
\Delta W_{Litigated} = EW_O - EW_{Litigated} = Q_U \cdot ((P_{Litigated} - P_O) + \pi \cdot \gamma).
\]

\subsection{Theorem 2: Expected Welfare Loss from Judicial Mandates}

\textbf{Statement:} Judicial mandates impose welfare losses due to urgency and penalties:
\[
\mathbb{E}[W_{Litigated}] = Q_U \cdot \left((P_{Litigated} - P_O) + \pi \cdot \gamma \right).
\]

\textbf{Proof:} By substituting the expected prices into the welfare function, we have:
\[
\mathbb{E}[W_{Litigated}] = Q_U \cdot ((P_{Litigated} - P_O) + \pi \cdot \gamma).
\]
