\begin{abstract}
Courts can force governments to deliver medicines, but legal urgency changes how the state buys them. We study \BPnPOIfull{} pharmaceutical purchase-offer-item observations from S\~ao Paulo's electronic procurement platform during \BPsampleStartYear--\BPsampleEndYear. Urgent procurement is \BPnegPctHeadline{} costlier than ordinary procurement, attracts \BPfirmsPctHeadlineAbs{} fewer bidders, and succeeds \BPsuccessPP{} more often. To separate pricing from sourcing, we compare court-mandated urgent purchases with an administrative urgent channel that lacks judicial sanctions. Because administrative cases are selected and larger, we combine selection bounds, within firm-buyer-item comparisons, and sourcing tests. Manski-Lee bounds place the litigated-over-administrative price gap at $[\BPutgBoundLow,\BPutgBoundHigh]$, compared with a naive \BPutgPointNaive{} gap. Yet the same firm selling the same item to the same buyer shows no broad markup in deep urgent markets ($\hat\beta=\BPutgTripleCoefUTG$, SE \BPutgTripleSEUTG). The markup channel reappears in thinner and earlier subsamples. The main margin is sourcing: administrative orders are \BPutgQtyAdminFoldPref{} times larger, winner sets overlap weakly (mean Jaccard \BPwinnerSwitchJaccardMean), and modal winners differ in \BPwinnerSwitchDiffModalPct{} of item-buyer pairs. One-sided sanctions secure delivery, but they impair demand aggregation and supplier matching.
\end{abstract}
