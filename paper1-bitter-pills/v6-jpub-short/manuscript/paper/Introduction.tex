\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When courts order governments to purchase specific goods, they override standard procurement procedures and compress the time available for planning and competitive bidding. This paper estimates the fiscal costs of such judicial enforcement in the context of health litigation in Brazil, where courts routinely compel state agencies to procure prescription drugs under tight deadlines.

The interaction between the judiciary and public administration has long attracted scholarly attention. Courts and regulatory agencies can be viewed as alternative instruments of social control, each with distinct comparative advantages \citep{glaeser2003rise}. While courts protect individual rights, their interventions in procurement can be costly: inefficient judicial processes may induce public buyers to accept suboptimal outcomes \citep{coviello2018bidding}. In health care, judicial mandates are particularly consequential, as they directly determine how---and at what price---the government acquires essential medicines \citep{wang2015right, ferraz2009right}.

Brazil provides an ideal setting to study these costs. The 1988 Constitution enshrines health as a ``right of all and a duty of the state,'' and courts have adopted an expansive interpretation under which virtually any medication can be obtained through litigation. Court orders are enforced as preliminary injunctions requiring purchase within days, compared to the weeks or months available for ordinary procurement. The volume of health litigation has grown dramatically: first-instance health claims reached approximately 96,000 nationally in 2017, a 130\% increase over 2008 \citep{cnj2019judicializacao}. In S\~ao Paulo state alone, health lawsuits grew by 913\% between 2008 and 2017.

We construct a unique bid-level dataset covering all pharmaceutical procurement by the S\~ao Paulo State Department of Health (SES/SP) from 2009 through 2019, comprising over 479,000 purchase-offer-item observations for medical supplies conducted through the state's electronic procurement platform (BEC).

Our empirical strategy exploits the quasi-experimental variation generated by court orders and administrative requests. These shocks partition purchases into \textit{ordinary} (standard timeline) and \textit{urgent} (compressed timeline). Within urgent purchases, we further distinguish \textit{administrative} requests---which carry no penalty for procurement failure---from \textit{litigated} purchases, where officials face fines, personal liability, and fund seizure. This distinction isolates what we term the ``under the gun'' effect: the additional cost attributable to the threat of judicial punishment. We estimate high-dimensional fixed effects regressions with item, year (or year-month), and public buyer unit (PBU) fixed effects, clustering standard errors at the PBU level.

The main findings are as follows. Urgent purchases carry reference prices 2.7\% higher and negotiated prices 5.4\% higher (total effect), with a direct effect of 3.1\% after controlling for quantity differences. Urgent tenders attract 5.5\% fewer bidding firms, yet the probability of tender success is 2.1 percentage points \textit{higher}---consistent with officials accepting higher prices to ensure compliance. The ``under the gun'' analysis reveals that comparing litigated and administrative urgent purchases for the same items, litigated tenders carry prices 23--30\% higher, a difference attributable to the threat of judicial punishment.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we provide the first estimates of the causal effect of judicial enforcement on public procurement outcomes using comprehensive administrative data and high-dimensional fixed effects, contributing to the literature on government spending efficiency \citep{bandiera2009active, best2023procurement, decarolis2018buyer}. Second, we identify and quantify the ``under the gun'' effect by exploiting the institutional distinction between administrative and litigated urgent purchases, contributing to the literature on how accountability mechanisms generate unintended costs \citep{rasul2018management, duflo2018obligations}. Third, we document how judicial mandates distort the planning phase of procurement, adding to the evidence on ex ante design in auction performance \citep{coviello2018bidding, baltrunaite2021discretion}. Heterogeneity analyses by SUS component, time period, competition, and PBU size---reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_het}---confirm that enforcement costs are concentrated where competitive pressure is weakest.
