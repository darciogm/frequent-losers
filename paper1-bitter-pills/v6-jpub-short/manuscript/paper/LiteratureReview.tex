\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

This paper connects several strands of the economics literature. The foundational distinction between \textit{active waste} (corruption) and \textit{passive waste} (inefficiency) in public spending \citep{bandiera2009active} motivates our focus on a previously unstudied source of passive waste: the compression of planning time induced by judicial mandates. Subsequent work has identified specific channels of passive waste, including bureaucratic capacity \citep{best2023procurement}, management practices \citep{rasul2018management}, and procurement design \citep{lewisfaupel2016can, szucs2023competition}. On the design side, \citet{coviello2018bidding} show that court-imposed delays reduce bidder participation and increase costs in Italian public works, while \citet{decarolis2018buyer} demonstrate that buyer competence is a key determinant of procurement outcomes, and \citet{baltrunaite2021discretion} find that reducing discretion can improve outcomes when corruption is prevalent but may harm outcomes otherwise. Our setting differs in that judicial intervention compresses rather than extends the procurement timeline, and the primary mechanism operates through planning disruption rather than direct manipulation of award criteria. Our ``under the gun'' effect is a specific instance of the broader tension identified in the accountability literature: oversight mechanisms intended to reduce waste can themselves generate costs when they distort rather than align incentives \citep{duflo2018obligations, williams2021bureaucratic}. Finally, the legal and public health literatures have documented the growth of health litigation in Latin America \citep{wang2015right, ferraz2009right, biehl2009will}, but have focused on the legal and health dimensions with limited attention to fiscal costs transmitted through the procurement channel. We fill this gap by providing granular estimates of how judicial enforcement affects prices, quantities, competition, and tender success.
