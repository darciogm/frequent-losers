\begin{abstract}
When public officials face personal sanctions for procurement
failure, do they buy at any price? We study this question using
court-mandated pharmaceutical purchases in S\~ao Paulo, Brazil
(2009--2019), where judges compel agencies to procure specific
medicines within days---and officials face fines, liability, and
fund seizure if tenders fail. With item, time, and buyer fixed
effects, court-mandated purchases raise prices by 5.4\% and reduce
bidder participation by 5.5\%. We exploit an institutional
distinction between two types of urgent purchases that share
planning constraints but differ in penalty exposure to isolate the
``under the gun'' effect: the threat of judicial punishment alone
raises procurement costs by 23--30\%, operating entirely through
demand fragmentation---officials cannot aggregate orders---rather
than through direct price inflation. The mechanism is general:
whenever accountability regimes penalize non-completion more than
overpayment, procurement officials will prioritize compliance over
cost-efficiency.
\end{abstract}
