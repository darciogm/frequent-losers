\section{Mechanisms and Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

We consider three mechanism tests and two alternative explanations for the FL-price association.

\subsection{M1: Strategic Selection vs. Competitive Displacement}

If FL firms are cover bidders, does their presence \textit{displace} genuine competitors? This is a critical question for welfare analysis: if cover bidders crowd out legitimate firms, the welfare cost may exceed the direct price markup. We regress $\log(n_{\text{genuine}} + 1)$---the number of non-FL participants---on the cover bidder indicator. The coefficient is positive (0.14, SE = 0.006), indicating that FL-present tenders have \textit{more} genuine competitors, not fewer.

We interpret this as evidence of \textbf{strategic selection}: cartels deploy cover bidders in tenders where legitimate competition is already strong, precisely because more cover bids are needed to secure the designated winner's contract when facing more genuine competitors. This is consistent with the cartel coordination problem analyzed by \citet{marshall2012economics}: the value of cover bidding is highest when competitive pressure threatens the cartel's designated winner. A cartel facing only one genuine competitor may not need cover bidders at all, while a cartel facing four or five genuine competitors needs cover bids to dilute the competitive signal and meet minimum bidder requirements without relying on genuine entrants.

The strategic selection interpretation has important implications: it means the FL-price association is not driven by FL firms reducing competition (they do not), but rather by cover bidders being deployed precisely in the tenders where the cartel is extracting rents---tenders where legitimate competition would otherwise drive prices down. The matching estimators reported in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} (Table~\ref{tab:matching}) provide additional support: after matching on item, year, and procedure type, the FL-price association remains positive and significant.

\subsection{M2: Reference Price Calibration}

We test whether the price effect operates through distortion of the winning-to-reference price ratio. The coefficient of the cover bidder indicator on the price ratio is $-0.041$ (SE = 0.003), indicating that winners in FL-present tenders obtain prices \textit{closer} to the reference price---consistent with the cartel's designated winner calibrating their bid to appear reasonable relative to the reference, while cover bidders absorb the ``competitive'' space above. This pattern makes economic sense: a cartel-designated winner bids just below the reference price (appearing to offer a good deal to the purchasing body), while cover bidders bid above the reference price (appearing to be less competitive). The result is a winning price that is higher than what genuine competition would produce, but still below the reference price---avoiding the red flag of a winning bid exceeding the reference.

\subsection{M3: Reverse Causality}

A natural concern is reverse causality: do FL firms enter markets \textit{because} prices are already high, rather than \textit{causing} high prices? High-price markets may be more attractive to opportunistic bidders, or firms that cannot compete on cost may gravitate toward markets where prices are inflated by other factors. We construct a market-level panel (item$\times$PBU$\times$year) and regress FL entry on lagged log prices. The coefficient is positive and statistically significant ($\beta = 0.002$, $p = 0.012$), suggesting that FL firms are more likely to enter higher-price markets.

However, the magnitude of this reverse causality channel is small. A one-standard-deviation increase in lagged log prices predicts only a 0.2 percentage point increase in the probability of FL entry, compared with the unconditional FL entry rate of 4.8\%. We interpret this as consistent with both the cover bidding hypothesis and selection: cover bidders may be deployed where the cartel's stakes are highest (high-value tenders), and the reverse causality channel, while statistically present, is too small to explain the 6.4\% FL-price association documented in our main results. The staggered DiD analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:did} provides additional perspective on the temporal relationship between FL entry and prices, though with important limitations regarding parallel trends.
