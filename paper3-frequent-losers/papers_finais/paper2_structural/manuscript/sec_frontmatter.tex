\begin{abstract}
\noindent We develop a structural model of cover-bidder deployment and
estimate it on 40~million bids from Brazilian public procurement
(2009--2019). The model reveals a \emph{dispersion paradox}:
coordinated cover bidding---the empirically dominant regime
($\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$)---produces \emph{lower}, not higher,
within-tender bid dispersion ($\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$).
This overturns the high-dispersion assumption embedded in mainstream
detection screens. The calibrated complementarity elasticity
($\hat{\gamma} = 0.694$) shows that cartels scale cover-bidder
deployment proportionally to genuine competition, concentrating in
competitive rather than concentrated markets. Five out-of-sample
specification tests---including a novel Bajari--Ye tender-FE reversal
where the bid-coordination signal flips sign once the focal-point
component is absorbed---confirm the model's predictions. The
structural mechanism explains why a participation-based screen
(AUC~$= 0.94$ against CADE convictions) dominates variance-based
alternatives (AUC~$= 0.79$), and why Brazil's minimum-bidder rules
generated institutional demand for cover bidders until the 2021
procurement reform abolished them.
\end{abstract}
