\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cover bidding---submitting bids designed to lose---is a prevalent
operational mechanism of bid-rigging cartels
\citep{oecd2012fighting}, yet its internal
structure remains poorly understood. How do cartels calibrate cover
bids? Does coordination tighten or widen the bid distribution? And
what does the answer imply for the screens that competition authorities
use to detect collusion? This paper develops and estimates a structural
model of cover-bidder deployment that answers these questions and
uncovers a \emph{dispersion paradox}: coordinated cover bidding
produces \emph{lower}, not higher, within-tender bid dispersion---the
opposite of what the dominant detection screens assume.

The model specifies two regimes that differ in coordination capacity.
Under \emph{complementary} cover bidding (Regime~1), cover bidders
receive loose instructions (``bid above the winner'') and spread bids
across a wide range, producing high dispersion. Under
\emph{coordinated} cover bidding (Regime~2), cover bidders calibrate
bids near a focal point just above the winning price, producing low
dispersion. The cartel chooses the number of cover bidders, their bid
distribution, and which markets to target as a function of genuine
competition, detection probability, and the institutional rules of the
procurement system. The model generates five testable predictions that
we take to data from 40~million bids in Brazilian public procurement
(2009--2019).

Maximum likelihood estimation on bid-level data selects Regime~2:
cover-bid dispersion is 28\% below genuine-bid dispersion
($\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$;
$\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$). Cover bids cluster 0.83 log points above the winning price
($\hat{\epsilon} = 0.83$, approximately $e^{0.83} - 1 \approx 129\%$
in levels), high enough to guarantee losing yet calibrated to
simulate competitive bidding. The
structural estimates are identified from the distribution of 189,381
losing bids submitted by 2,735 \emph{frequent losers}
(FL)---firms that never win a single tender yet participate in
abnormally many procurement processes---benchmarked against 23.2
million genuine losing bids. The FL classification, validated against
Brazil's competition authority (CADE) convictions with
AUC~$= 0.94$, is developed in a companion paper
\citep{genicolomartins2026screening}; here we take it as given and
focus on the structural mechanism it reveals.

\subsection{The dispersion paradox and its implications}

The central finding---that coordinated cover bidding reduces
within-tender bid dispersion---has three implications for our
understanding of how cartels operate and how they can be detected.

\emph{First}, it provides a structural explanation for a puzzle in
the detection literature. Variance-based screens
\citep{imhof2019detecting} achieve AUC~$= 0.79$ against CADE
convictions, while the participation-based FL screen achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$. The model explains the gap: variance screens are
designed for Regime~1 (complementary) cartels but are structurally
misaligned with Regime~2 (coordinated) cartels, whose bid patterns
resemble competitive tenders. The participation footprint of cover
bidding---never winning yet bidding repeatedly---is invariant to the
bid distribution, making it robust to both regimes.

\emph{Second}, it provides a structural interpretation for the
\citet{bajari2003deciding} exchangeability test. FL bid residuals
violate exchangeability and exhibit excess pairwise correlation,
but these violations \emph{reverse} under tender fixed effects---the
FL pairwise product drops below the non-FL product. The model
predicts exactly this pattern: cover bids share a focal-point
component ($b^* + \epsilon$) that tender fixed effects absorb,
leaving only residual dispersion---which, under Regime~2, is low.
The reversal is a novel directional prediction that only the
structural model generates (Section~\ref{sec:spec_tests}).

\emph{Third}, it yields testable market-selection predictions.
Because cover bidding is most valuable in competitive markets (low
HHI), the model predicts that FL activity concentrates in
competitive rather than concentrated markets. We confirm this
empirically, and the calibrated complementarity elasticity
$\hat{\gamma} = 0.694$ shows that cartels scale cover-bidder
deployment nearly proportionally to genuine competition---a
relationship that holds both across and within PBUs
(Section~\ref{sec:results_calibration}).

\subsection{Contributions}

We make three contributions to the understanding of how bid-rigging
cartels operate.

\emph{First}, we develop and estimate a structural model of
cover-bidder deployment that distinguishes complementary from
coordinated cover bidding. The model formalizes the cartel's
trade-off between detection risk, cover-bidding cost, and the
institutional constraint of minimum-bidder rules, and generates
closed-form predictions for the number, distribution, and market
selection of cover bids. Regime~2 (coordinated) is selected
overwhelmingly ($\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$), establishing the
dispersion paradox as an equilibrium outcome: \emph{low} dispersion,
not high, is the empirical signature of cover bidding in this setting.

\emph{Second}, we validate the model through five out-of-sample
specification tests on moments not targeted in estimation:
(i)~the corner-vs-interior solution for cover-bidder counts;
(ii)~within-tender bid dispersion (FL bids less dispersed in
82.6\% of mixed tenders); (iii)~the $m$--$\theta$ comparative
static across PBU size quartiles; (iv)~the Bajari--Ye
exchangeability and conditional-independence violations; and
(v)~the market-selection gradient across winner HHI. All five go
in the direction predicted by the model. The Bajari--Ye tender-FE
reversal---where the FL correlation flips sign once the shared
focal-point component is absorbed---is a novel prediction that only
the structural model generates.

\paragraph{What the model does and does not deliver.}
The structural framework delivers three things that reduced-form
analysis alone cannot: (i)~the two-regime taxonomy that gives the
dispersion paradox its equilibrium interpretation; (ii)~the
Bajari--Ye tender-FE reversal, a novel directional prediction that
only the focal-point model generates; and (iii)~the calibrated
complementarity elasticity ($\hat{\gamma} = 0.694$) that quantifies
how cartels scale cover bidding with genuine competition. What the
model does \emph{not} deliver is causal identification of the
FL--price association: the reduced-form coefficient $\hat{\beta}$
reflects both treatment and selection, and the welfare counterfactuals
inherit this limitation (Section~\ref{sec:counterfactual}).

\emph{Third}, we derive policy implications from the model's
mechanism. The minimum-bidder rule for convite procedures creates a
corner solution that forces cover-bidder deployment in 39.2\% of
FL-present tenders; removing it would eliminate the institutional
demand for cover bidding. Brazil's 2021 procurement reform
(Lei~14.133) has since abolished convite entirely, effectively
implementing this counterfactual. We embed the detection ROC curve
in a social welfare function and show that the welfare-maximizing threshold
($1.6\times$IQR) coincides with the statistical optimum, providing
a coherent basis for operational deployment. The model also explains
why participation-based screens (AUC~$= 0.94$) outperform
variance-based screens (AUC~$= 0.79$): the dispersion paradox
renders bid-level statistics structurally uninformative about
coordinated cartels.

\subsection{Related literature}

This paper connects to three strands. First, the structural auction
literature that estimates bid distributions to recover costs or detect
collusion \citep{guerre2000optimal, bajari2003deciding,
porter1993detection, pesendorfer2000study}. We contribute a mixture
model that separates cover bids from genuine bids using
participation-based labels rather than latent types, enabling direct
estimation of the cover-bid distribution without an EM algorithm.

Second, the proactive cartel screening literature
\citep{harrington2008detecting, imhof2019detecting,
huber2019machine}. Our structural model provides a mechanism-based
explanation for the documented performance gap between
participation-based and variance-based screens.

Third, the procurement design literature
\citep{szymanski2006impact, decarolis2018buyers}. The model's corner
solution (Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m}) shows that minimum-bidder
rules create institutional demand for cover bidders, converting a
competition-enhancing regulation into a cartel-facilitating one.
