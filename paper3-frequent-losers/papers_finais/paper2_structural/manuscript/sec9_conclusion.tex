\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

How do cartels calibrate cover bids, and what does this imply for
detection? This paper answers both questions through a structural
model estimated on 40~million bids in Brazilian public procurement
(2009--2019). The model generates five testable predictions; all
five are confirmed out of sample.

The central finding is the dispersion paradox: coordinated cover
bidding---the empirically dominant regime ($\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$)---
produces \emph{lower} within-tender bid dispersion than genuine
competition ($\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$). This
overturns a maintained assumption in the cartel detection literature
\citep{imhof2019detecting, huber2019machine} and explains a
practical puzzle: why participation-based screens (AUC~$= 0.94$)
dominate variance-based alternatives (AUC~$= 0.79$). Under
Regime~2, bid-level statistics are structurally uninformative; the
participation footprint---never winning yet bidding
repeatedly---is the only invariant signature of cover bidding.

The welfare counterfactuals illustrate the policy stakes. The
model's corner solution shows that convite minimum-bidder rules
forced cover-bidder deployment in 39.2\% of FL-present tenders;
Brazil's Lei~14.133/2021 has since abolished convite entirely
(Art.~28), effectively implementing this counterfactual. The
welfare-maximizing detection threshold ($1.6\times$IQR) coincides
with the statistical and detection-theoretic optima, providing a
coherent basis for operational deployment.

\paragraph{Scope and boundaries.}
The structural model identifies the cover-bid mechanism---the
distribution, deployment, and market selection of cover
bids---while the price-link in the welfare chain uses reduced-form
bounds (3.6--8.0\% across five estimators) that reflect both
treatment and selection. All results come from a single electronic
procurement platform (BEC/S\~ao Paulo); the dispersion paradox
depends on Regime~2, which requires real-time information
exchange---a feature of electronic auctions. In jurisdictions where
sealed-bid formats dominate, Regime~1 may prevail and
variance-based screens may perform adequately. The calibrated
detection-penalty $\hat{\phi}_0$ is indistinguishable from zero,
limiting enforcement counterfactuals; this may reflect limited
enforcement variation in the data rather than true insensitivity.
Replication on platforms with different institutional designs is the
natural next step.

\paragraph{Implications for detection methodology.}
The dispersion paradox has a practical corollary: any screen that
conditions on within-tender bid statistics will underperform when
facing coordinated cartels---precisely the cartels with the highest
welfare cost. Participation-based screens, which detect the
\emph{operational footprint} of cover bidding regardless of bid
calibration, fill this gap. The structural model's specification
tests show that the IQR rule captures the most discriminative
dimension of cover bidding; adding bid-level features or structural
scores does not improve detection.

\paragraph{Future research.}
Three directions merit investigation: (i)~replication in other
procurement systems to test external validity;
(ii)~dynamic models of cartel--authority interaction, where cartels
adapt to screening by rotating cover bidders or allowing occasional
wins; and (iii)~field experiments in which procurement agencies
deploy FL screening and measure deterrence effects.
