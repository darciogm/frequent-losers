\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% Results opening — screening/triage framing

This section reports four sets of results that play distinct roles
in evaluating the screen as a triage tool.
Classification diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:class_diagnostics})
assess whether the FL rule captures a real behavioral pattern.
Conditional price comparisons
(Section~\ref{sec:results_ols}) document the association between FL
presence and tender outcomes. Detection performance
(Section~\ref{sec:results_detection})---the paper's primary
validation exercise---evaluates the screen against
external enforcement data. Supporting diagnostics
(Sections~\ref{sec:results_network}--\ref{sec:results_structural})
ask whether the screen's observed behavior is coherent with
coordinated cover bidding. After absorbing item fixed effects, the
raw FL--non-FL gap compresses from 2.43 to 0.050 log points (overlap
0.978): within narrow product categories, FL-present and FL-absent
tenders look nearly identical on observables. The conditional price
association is 3.6--7.7\% across four estimation approaches.

% ── 7.0 Classification Diagnostics ──────────────────────────────

\subsection{Classification Diagnostics}
\label{sec:class_diagnostics}

Before turning to the price regressions, we assess whether the binary
FL classification captures something real or is an artifact of the
threshold. The zero-win condition is strict: relaxing it to include
firms with win rates below 1\% or 2\% attenuates the price coefficient
substantially (Table~\ref{tab:fl_lowwinrate},
Appendix~\ref{app:online_robustness}), so the bright line at zero is
doing real work. A continuous measure---the log of the most active
always-loser's participation count---yields a coefficient of 0.022
(SE = 0.005, $p < 0.01$) per log-point.%
\footnote{The continuous measure correlates moderately with the number
of always-losers in the tender ($r = 0.33$), so part of the
coefficient may capture always-loser count rather than individual-firm
intensity. Using the mean (not max) participation count reduces the
correlation to 0.15 and yields qualitatively similar results.}
At the FL threshold ($\log 14 \approx 2.64$), the continuous prediction
is $2.64 \times 0.022 \approx 5.8\%$---close to the binary OLS of
6.4\%, indicating that the binary coefficient reflects the underlying
continuous relationship evaluated at the threshold rather than an
artifact of the cutoff. A permutation placebo that randomly reassigns
the FL indicator across tenders (preserving the 4.8\% FL-presence rate)
produces coefficients centered at zero (mean 0.001, SD 0.003 over 20
replications), with no permutation reaching 0.064. The FL--price
association reflects the specific allocation of FL presence across
tenders, not chance.

% ── 7.1 Price Association ───────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{OLS and Matching Results}
\label{sec:results_ols}

Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports the baseline OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main} for log negotiated prices
(Diagnostic~\ref{pred:price_v6}). Across all four
specifications, FL presence is associated with significantly higher
prices: 6.8\% in the general specification (column~1), 6.4\% with PBU
fixed effects (column~2), 9.3\% for preg\~ao (column~3), and 3.8\% for
convite (column~4). Adding PBU fixed effects barely changes the
coefficient, indicating that FL presence does not proxy for high-cost
purchasing units. The unrestricted sample (including items that never
attract FL firms) yields a nearly identical coefficient, so the
item-type restriction does not inflate the baseline.

\input{./output/tables/tab_prices}

FL-present tenders also have 0.19 log points more non-FL firms
(Diagnostic~\ref{pred:selection_v6}; PBU FE specification),
contradicting crowding-out and consistent with selection
into competitive markets
(Table~\ref{tab:nfirms_excl}, Appendix~\ref{app:competition}).

\paragraph{Cross-fit estimate.}
FL status is defined on the same 2009--2019 window used for
estimation, potentially creating a mechanical link between
classification and outcomes. To break this link, we define FL using
only odd years and estimate on even years, and vice versa. The
cross-fit average is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{CF}} = 0.036$ (3.6\%),
attenuated relative to full-sample OLS. A decomposition
exercise---applying the odd-year FL list to the \emph{full}
sample---yields 0.043 (SE = 0.019), indicating that roughly
two-thirds of the attenuation reflects classification noise from
the smaller subsample (1,885--2,153 vs.\ 2,735 FL firms),
not a mechanical bias in the baseline.\footnote{The difference
$0.064 - 0.043 = 0.021$ has approximate SE
$\sqrt{0.019^2 + 0.020^2} \approx 0.028$, yielding $t \approx 0.75$
($p \approx 0.45$).}
The cross-fit is valuable as a conservative estimate that eliminates
any within-sample mechanical correlation; the OLS provides the
full-sample baseline. The robust range is 3.6--6.4\%.

\paragraph{Matching estimators.}
Coarsened exact matching (CEM: 0.077, $N = 969{,}751$) and inverse
probability weighting (IPW: 0.055, $N = 830{,}194$) bracket the OLS
and cross-fit estimates: the price association holds up under non-parametric
reweighting.

\paragraph{Measurement-error diagnostic (IV).}
The leave-one-out IV estimate (0.194, $p = 0.01$, $F = 396$) exceeds
OLS by a factor of three, as one would expect under measurement-error
attenuation in the binary FL indicator
(Section~\ref{sec:reduced_form}). However, if cartels operate across
PBUs within the same product market, the instrument may capture cartel
intensity rather than misclassification alone, biasing the IV upward.
We therefore treat the IV as a diagnostic suggesting that the OLS
may be attenuated, not as a primary estimator
(Table~\ref{tab:iv_balance}, Table~\ref{tab:iv_main} in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Summary.}
Four approaches that differ in weighting, functional form, and
information sets produce a consistent 3.6--7.7\% conditional price
association: cross-fit (3.6\%), IPW (5.5\%), OLS (6.4\%), CEM
(7.7\%). The estimates cluster rather than scatter, indicating a
stable conditional association across designs. The IV (19.4\%) is
directionally consistent with attenuation in the binary indicator
but is not interpretable as a preferred magnitude, given
exclusion-restriction concerns
(Section~\ref{sec:reduced_form}). An omitted confounder would need
to explain 17.5\% of residual variation in \emph{both} FL presence
and log prices---after absorbing item, year, and PBU fixed
effects---to nullify the coefficient \citep{cinelli2020making}.
None of these estimates has a causal interpretation; they document a
robust conditional association that motivates the screen's
deployment.

% ── 7.2 Detection Performance ────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Detection Performance}
\label{sec:results_detection}

This subsection evaluates the screen's triage performance---its
ability to flag suspicious environments using only participation
records. Figure~\ref{fig:roc} (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness})
presents the FL ROC curve against CADE cartel co-participation. The
screen achieves AUC~$= 0.94$ (bootstrapped 95\% CI: $[0.91, 0.94]$;
Table~\ref{tab:roc_detection}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).
The data-driven optimal threshold (Youden's $J = 0.84$) corresponds
to 1.45$\times$IQR---nearly identical to the 1.5$\times$ rule chosen
\emph{a priori} on economic grounds---achieving TPR~$= 1.00$ with
FPR~$= 0.16$. The convergence is notable: the participation intensity
at which profit-maximizing entry becomes difficult to rationalize is
also the intensity that best discriminates cartel-linked environments.
Higher participation counts mechanically increase co-participation
opportunities; the permutation test (Section~\ref{sec:cade}), which
stratifies on participation quartile, provides an independent check
that the association is real (3.5$\times$, $p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{Complementarity with bid-level screens.}
Against the same CADE ground truth, the FL screen achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$ versus 0.79 for a \citet{imhof2019detecting}-style
bid-level proxy (CV median split; DeLong $p < 0.001$;
Table~\ref{tab:imhof_comparison},
Figure~\ref{fig:roc_comparison} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).
The comparison uses a coarser proxy than the full Imhof
implementation (which combines CV, kurtosis, spread, and a trained
classifier), and the two screens operate at different levels (firm
vs.\ tender).%
\footnote{The CADE ground truth, based on co-participation, may
mechanically favor participation-based screens; the permutation test
(Section~\ref{sec:cade}) controls for this.}
What FL adds is reach: it operates where bid-level records are
unavailable---a common constraint in developing-country
procurement---and produces firm-level flags that triage
investigations before detailed bid analysis begins. For enforcement
purposes, the screen's main value is not to replace bid-level tools
but to prioritize where those tools should be deployed, particularly
in settings where investigative resources are scarce.

\paragraph{Horse-race regression.}
Including both FL presence and an Imhof-style CV flag as regressors
reveals that the two screens capture nearly orthogonal information
(correlation 0.060). The FL coefficient \emph{rises} from 0.064 to
0.084 ($p < 0.01$) when the CV flag is added---a suppression effect
consistent with the framework's logic that coordinated cover bidding
may produce low within-tender dispersion while maintaining FL
participation. The Imhof flag enters at 0.021 ($p < 0.01$). The two
screens complement each other: FL triages environments using
participation records alone, and bid-level tools can then be deployed
on the flagged subset (Table~\ref{tab:horse_race},
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Why combination degrades.}
Naively combining the FL and Imhof scores into a single index
degrades detection sharply (AUC~$= 0.61$ vs.\ 0.94 for FL alone;
Table~\ref{tab:imhof_comparison}). The degradation reflects the
framework's central insight: under coordinated cover bidding, FL
firms enter tenders where they \emph{raise} within-tender dispersion
(Table~\ref{tab:regime_test}, coefficient 0.47--0.55 on log bid SD),
causing Imhof-style features to classify those environments as
\emph{less} suspicious. The two screens point in opposite directions
for the same firms---precisely the reason the paper proposes
sequential deployment (screen $\to$ triage $\to$ investigate) rather
than score combination.

\medskip
\noindent\textit{The remaining subsections report supporting diagnostics.
These are secondary to the screen's detection performance above; their
role is to assess whether the screen's behavior is coherent with the
proposed cover-bidding interpretation.}
\medskip

\subsection{Network-Split Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:results_network}

Table~\ref{tab:network_split} splits FL firms into
concentrated-market and competitive-market subgroups based on
co-bidding network metrics (Section~\ref{sec:empirical_network}).\footnote{Markets
above the winner-HHI sample median are classified as concentrated;
firms sharing at least two tenders are classified as repeat
co-participants.}
Competitive-market FL firms---operating in markets with diversified
winner patterns (low HHI)---account for most of the observed price association: 0.126
($p < 0.01$, PBU FE). Concentrated-market FL firms show no significant
effect ($-0.018$, $p > 0.3$).

The pattern is consistent with the framework's market-selection
implication (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}): cover bidding is most
profitable in competitive
markets, where manufactured competition has the highest marginal
return. In the framework, concentrated markets already sustain high prices
through market power, making cover bidders redundant---consistent
with the near-zero FL coefficient in concentrated markets.%
\footnote{Noisier FL classification in concentrated markets or thin FE
cells absorbing the effect cannot be ruled out with cross-sectional
data; the monotonic pattern across HHI quartiles is suggestive but not
definitive.}
From a detection standpoint, the result is encouraging: the FL screen
bites hardest in competitive markets, especially where undetected collusion would be most costly to
taxpayers and where other detection tools have limited reach.

\subsection{Bajari--Ye Tests}
\label{sec:results_bajari}

The price association concentrates in competitive markets; the next
question is whether FL bids look like they come from a different
data-generating process. The \citet{bajari2003deciding} framework
provides two tests
(Diagnostic~\ref{pred:exchange_v6};
Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_corrected} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Exchangeability.}
The KS test rejects the null that FL and non-FL bid residuals share
the same distribution ($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$): FL bids appear to be drawn
from a different process.

\paragraph{Conditional independence.}
The mean pairwise product of FL residuals is 4.28 ($t = 81.0$,
$p < 0.001$), with the bootstrap FL--non-FL difference
excluding zero, indicating systematically different within-tender
correlation coherent with coordinated bidding. Enriching the
first stage with firm age and CNAE sector dummies alongside firm
size leaves R-squared virtually unchanged (0.770) and does not
alter the KS or pairwise-product results, reducing the concern
that the exchangeability violation is driven by omitted firm-level
cost shifters (Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_corrected}).

\paragraph{Tender FE.}
Under tender FE (Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_tender_fe},
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}), both products drop substantially
(FL: $5.16 \to 0.38$; non-FL: $2.21 \to 0.86$), with the FL product
falling \emph{below} non-FL---a reversal consistent with Regime~2.
Under the framework, cover bids would cluster near $b^* + \epsilon$;
removing the tender mean strips out any shared focal-point component, which is the sort of
variation the coordinated-regime framework emphasizes.

% ── 7.4 Structural Diagnostic ───────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Structural Diagnostic}
\label{sec:results_structural}

As a final supporting diagnostic, we report the structural
estimation results from the bid-level mixture model
(Appendix~\ref{app:structural_id}). Within the framework, BIC
strongly favors Regime~2 ($\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$;
Table~\ref{tab:structural_params}). The dispersion ratio
$\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$: FL bids are 28\%
\emph{less} dispersed than non-FL bids---coherent with coordinated
cover bidding, under which variance-based screens lose power.
The $n$-conditional markup (6.4\%) is close to the OLS baseline.
These results provide one structural interpretation for why a
participation-based screen may complement dispersion-based tools;
they do not independently validate the mechanism. Additional
specification tests are in Appendix~\ref{app:structural_id};
a companion paper \citep{genicolomartins2026structural} develops
the full structural model, calibration, and policy counterfactuals.
