\section{Conceptual Framework: Why Participation Anomalies May Signal Collusion}
\label{sec:structural}

Cover bidding is well documented in prosecuted cartels
\citep{porter1999ohio, pesendorfer2000study, asker2010leniency,
marshall2012economics}. We develop a simple framework that organizes
the screening intuition and generates diagnostic implications
(full assumptions, proofs, and structural likelihood in
Appendices~\ref{app:proofs} and~\ref{app:structural_id}).

A cartel controls a designated winner and deploys $m \geq 0$ cover
bidders (FL firms), each bidding above the cartel's winning bid
$b^*$. The optimal $m^*$ decreases in detection probability
$\theta_k$ and per-bidder cost $c_1$, and is weakly larger under
convite, where the minimum-bidder rule ($\underline{n} = 3$) forces
participation regardless of market conditions---diluting the FL--price
signal by mixing mandatory and voluntary deployments
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}).

Two information structures generate two cover-bidding regimes.
Under \textbf{Regime~1} (complementary), cover bids scatter uniformly
above $b^*$, raising within-tender dispersion. Under \textbf{Regime~2}
(coordinated), cover bids follow a truncated normal centered near
$b^*$, \emph{lowering} dispersion. The detection implication is sharp:
Regime~2 defeats dispersion-based screens precisely when coordination
is strongest, because cover bids cluster where genuine bids would
cluster. A participation-based screen sidesteps this vulnerability
entirely---it does not depend on how cover bids are distributed, only
on the fact that cover bidders must participate.

A \textbf{market-selection implication} follows from the cross-derivative
of cartel surplus: cover bidding is most profitable in competitive
markets (low HHI), where the marginal return to manufactured
competition is highest.
