\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The detection literature's most widely used tools share a
vulnerability: they rely on bid dispersion, and the best-coordinated
cartels produce exactly the dispersion patterns that evade them. We
formalized this blind spot in a model of cover bidding, distinguishing
complementary from coordinated regimes, and built a screen that
exploits the one trace coordinated cover bidding cannot
erase---participation.

A firm that bids on dozens of tenders over a decade without winning a
single one is not competing; it is performing. The frequent-loser
screen detects this performance using only win/loss records. Applied
to 4.5 million tender-items from Brazilian procurement, it achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$ against competition-authority convictions---outperforming
both machine-learning and structural classifiers---and flags
environments with 3.6--7.7\% higher prices. Five independent
diagnostics produce a joint pattern coherent with coordinated cover
bidding: the structural model selects the coordinated regime,
Bajari--Ye tests reject bid exchangeability, the price effect
concentrates in competitive markets, FL--winner pairs recur far
beyond chance, and the premium is largest where cover bidding is
voluntary.

For procurement authorities, we propose a three-stage pathway:
(i)~\emph{Screen}---apply the FL rule to participation records, a
computation that runs in minutes on any procurement database;
(ii)~\emph{Triage}---cross-reference FL-flagged markets with network
metrics to prioritize the highest-risk cases; and
(iii)~\emph{Investigate}---deploy bid-level forensic tools on the
triaged set. The first stage requires no bid microdata, no
enforcement priors, and no supervised training---only the information
that every procurement system already collects.

The screen's bite varies with institutional context: the FL price
gap is 12.5 times larger in the smallest purchasing units than in
the largest, tracking the model's prediction that cover bidding
thrives where oversight is weakest. This gradient suggests that
even modest investments in procurement monitoring can compress the
scope for coordinated entry.

The FL pattern is not an artifact of Brazilian institutions. Wherever
cartels face a trade-off between coordination precision and detection
risk, the model predicts that cover bidders will participate and
lose. Replication across procurement systems is the most pressing next
step; the screen's minimal data requirements make it immediately
portable.
