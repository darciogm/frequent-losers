\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Procurement authorities need triage tools that work with minimal data.
Existing bid-level screens require granular bid data and lose power
against coordinated cartels whose cover bids mimic genuine dispersion.
The frequent-loser screen exploits a simpler trace---participation---that
coordinated cover bidding is unlikely to erase.

A firm that bids on dozens of tenders over a decade without winning a
single one exhibits behavior difficult to reconcile with
profit-maximizing entry. The frequent-loser screen flags this anomaly
using only win/loss records. Applied
to 4.5 million tender-items from Brazilian procurement, it achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$ against competition-authority convictions and flags
environments with 3.6--7.7\% higher conditional prices. Five
diagnostics produce a joint pattern coherent with coordinated cover
bidding: within the structural framework BIC favors the coordinated regime,
Bajari--Ye tests reject bid exchangeability, the price association
concentrates in competitive markets, FL--winner pairs recur beyond
chance, and the premium is larger where cover bidding is voluntary.

For procurement authorities, we propose a three-stage pathway:
(i)~\emph{Screen}---apply the FL rule to participation records, a
computation that runs in minutes on any procurement database;
(ii)~\emph{Triage}---cross-reference FL-flagged markets with network
metrics to prioritize the highest-risk cases; and
(iii)~\emph{Investigate}---deploy bid-level forensic tools on the
triaged set. The first stage requires no bid microdata, no
enforcement priors, and no supervised training---only the information
that every procurement system already collects.

The screen's bite varies with institutional context: the FL price
association is 12.5 times larger in the smallest purchasing units than
in the largest, consistent with the framework's implication that cover
bidding thrives where oversight is weakest. This gradient suggests
that even modest investments in procurement monitoring can compress the
scope for anomalous entry.

The FL pattern need not be specific to Brazilian institutions.
Wherever procurement systems record participation and outcomes, the
screen is directly applicable; its economic rationale---that cover
bidders must participate to perform their role---is
institution-general, though its empirical performance in other
settings remains to be tested. Replication across procurement systems
is the most pressing next step; the screen's minimal data requirements
make it immediately portable.
