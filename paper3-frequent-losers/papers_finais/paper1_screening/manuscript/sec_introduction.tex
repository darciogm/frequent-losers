\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Procurement authorities investigating bid rigging face a practical
constraint: the most widely deployed detection tools require granular
bid data that many systems do not collect.
Existing screens---within-tender variance, kurtosis, and
coefficient of variation
\citep{imhof2019detecting, huber2019machine}---rely on bid
dispersion and require bid-level microdata. Moreover, when cover
bidders coordinate their bids tightly around the designated winner,
dispersion \emph{falls} and these screens lose power---a
vulnerability that a participation-based approach sidesteps.

We exploit a simpler footprint. Cover bidding requires firms to
show up and lose---repeatedly. A firm that bids on 50 tenders
over a decade without winning a single one is either
extraordinarily unlucky or engaged in behavior that warrants
scrutiny. We call these
firms \emph{frequent losers} (FL) and show that this participation
anomaly---observable from win/loss records alone, without any bid
microdata---flags procurement environments with systematically higher
conditional prices and strong proximity to known cartel environments.

A conceptual framework (Section~\ref{sec:structural}) organizes the
screening intuition by distinguishing two cover-bidding regimes: under
complementary cover bidding (Regime~1), cover bids scatter above the
winning bid; under coordinated cover bidding (Regime~2), they cluster
near a focal point. The framework suggests a clear detection
implication: under Regime~2, dispersion-based screens lose power when
coordination is strongest. A participation-based screen sidesteps
this problem because it does not depend on how cover bids are
distributed---only on the fact that cover bidders must participate.

We test this logic on S\~ao Paulo's Bolsa Eletr\^onica de Compras (BEC),
covering 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids over 2009--2019.
Among 41,000 firms, 16,843 never win a single tender; a simple
IQR-based outlier rule identifies 2,735 whose participation frequency
is difficult to reconcile with profit-maximizing entry---the frequent
losers. Against CADE ground truth, the rule achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$---higher than a CV-based proxy for
\citet{imhof2019detecting}-style screens (AUC~$= 0.79$), though the
comparison uses a coarser implementation than the full Imhof pipeline
(Section~\ref{sec:results_detection}).
The FL rule is a first-stage triage device that prioritizes
suspicious environments for investigation, not proof of collusion
or a label of cartel membership.

Validated against CADE (Brazil's competition authority), the screen
achieves AUC~$= 0.94$, with the data-driven optimal threshold nearly
identical to the \emph{a priori} rule. FL firms co-participate with
convicted cartelists at 3.5$\times$ the baseline rate ($p < 0.001$).
Three CADE-convicted cover bidders are themselves classified as FL
(Section~\ref{sec:cade}).

FL-flagged tenders show 3.6--7.7\% higher conditional prices in
within-item, within-year, within-purchasing-unit comparisons. The
range spans temporal cross-fitting (3.6\%), OLS with high-dimensional
fixed effects (6.4\%), and matching (CEM: 7.7\%; IPW: 5.5\%). After
absorbing item fixed effects the raw gap collapses from 2.43 to 0.050
log points (overlap 0.978; Table~\ref{tab:conditional_descstats},
Appendix~\ref{app:competition}), so the regressions compare
observationally similar tenders within tight product categories.
Sensitivity analysis (\citealt{cinelli2020making},
$RV = 17.5\%$) sets a high bar for omitted confounders.

Five diagnostics, each motivated by the framework, assess whether the
screen's behavior is coherent with coordinated cover bidding.
The price association concentrates in competitive markets, where the
framework suggests cover bidding pays the highest marginal return. Within the framework, BIC favors
Regime~2: FL-bid dispersion is 28\% \emph{below} non-FL bids
($\sigma_c/\sigma_g = 0.72$), coherent with coordinated cover
bidding and consistent with the rationale for a participation-based
screen.
Bajari--Ye tests reject exchangeability of FL and non-FL bid
residuals. FL--winner pairs recur far more often than chance
predicts. And the FL coefficient is larger in tenders where cover bidding
is voluntary (7.6\%) than where the minimum-bidder rule forces
participation. A horse-race regression shows FL captures price
information nearly orthogonal to \citet{imhof2019detecting}-style
bid-level features (correlation 0.06). No single diagnostic is
dispositive, but the joint pattern strengthens the plausibility of
the cover-bidding interpretation.

\subsection{Contributions}

\emph{First}, we build a firm-level screen that requires only
win/loss records---no bid microdata, no enforcement priors, no
supervised training labels. A conceptual framework distinguishing
complementary from coordinated cover-bidding regimes motivates the
screen and organizes the diagnostics.

\emph{Second}, the screen achieves AUC~$= 0.94$ against
competition-authority convictions and flags environments with
3.6--7.7\% higher conditional prices, with the CADE-optimal
threshold nearly identical to the \emph{a priori} economic rule.
It is deployable wherever procurement systems record who bid and
who won---including low-capacity enforcement settings where bid-level
forensics are infeasible.

\emph{Third}, five independent diagnostics---regime selection,
Bajari--Ye bid-coordination tests, network-split heterogeneity,
dyadic linkage, and invitation variation---are jointly coherent
with coordinated cover bidding, providing supporting evidence for
the screen's economic rationale without claiming mechanism
identification.

Three boundaries define what this paper does \emph{not} claim. The
price association is a conditional correlation, not a causal
estimate; the FL classification identifies a participation anomaly,
not true cartel membership; and the diagnostics are coherent with
coordinated cover bidding but do not uniquely identify the
mechanism. The paper's contribution is the screen itself---its
construction, external validation, and the coherent diagnostic
pattern that supports its deployment---not causal identification
of a price effect or proof of a mechanism
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
