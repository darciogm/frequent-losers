\section{Supporting Diagnostics and Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

A price association, by itself, does not tell an enforcement agency
what is generating it. This section asks a narrower question: does the
screen behave the way one would expect if coordinated cover bidding
were behind the association? Five diagnostics probe different facets
of this question. No single one is dispositive; the weight comes from
the joint pattern (Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms},
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{M1: Do FL firms crowd out genuine competitors?}
If FL firms displaced real bidders, we would see fewer genuine
participants in FL-present tenders. We see the opposite: $+0.19$
more non-FL firms under PBU fixed effects ($p < 0.01$). FL firms
add to participation rather than substitute for it, as the framework
predicts (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}).

\paragraph{M2: Are winning bids anchored to the reference price?}
BEC posts reference prices before bidding opens. In FL-present
tenders, winners bid 4\% closer to that anchor ($-0.041$,
$p < 0.01$)---a pattern one would expect if the winning bid and
the cover bids are calibrated around the same public price
\citep{marshall2012economics}.

\paragraph{M3: Do FL firms chase high prices?}
Within item markets, a 10\% lagged-price increase raises FL entry
probability by 0.02~pp ($\hat{\beta} = 0.0021$, SE = 0.0008). The
elasticity is too small to explain much of the 6.4\% price
association, though it cannot fully separate reverse causality from
strategic selection.

\paragraph{Alternative explanations.}
FL firms are roughly one year younger than non-FL firms. This is what
one would find if cartels create short-lived shells to serve as cover
bidders \citep{marshall2012economics}, but it is equally compatible
with young firms exploring procurement markets before giving up. The
preg\~ao--convite asymmetry (9.3\% vs.\ 3.8\%) fits the framework:
preg\~ao's real-time format may help coordinate cover-bid placement,
while convite's minimum-bidder rule forces some participation
regardless of conditions, diluting the signal. When we interact FL
with a constraint-binding indicator
(Section~\ref{sec:additional_id}), the price association turns out to
be driven entirely by voluntary FL participation (7.6\% premium),
not by rule-forced entry. None of these patterns pins down the
mechanism; each admits more than one reading.

\paragraph{Rationality of FL participation.}
\label{sec:rationality}
With zero wins, expected profit per bid is $-c < 0$ for any positive
bidding cost. At the FL threshold (14 tenders), cumulative losses are
R\$700--7,000---conceivably bad luck. At the 90th percentile (50+
tenders), losses reach R\$2,500--25,000, and a Bayesian learner
updates $\Pr(\text{win})$ to 0.019---making exit the rational
response even under generous assumptions.%
\footnote{With $\bar{V} = \text{R\$}86{,}000$ and margin $= 0.10$,
expected gain is R\$163---below the lowest bidding cost (R\$50).
Win rate zero in BEC does not mean zero elsewhere, but 50+ zero-win
BEC participations require explanation even for firms profitable
outside the platform \citep{asker2010leniency, marshall2012economics}.}
The rationality calculation documents anomalous participation, not
proven side payments; its role is to strengthen the case that FL
captures behavior worth screening for, not to establish the
mechanism. Full calculations are in Table~\ref{tab:rationality}
(Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{M4: Dyadic linkage concentration.}
A stratified permutation test (1,000 iterations, preserving
participation quartiles) shows FL firms form 4,696 high-frequency
co-bidding pairs ($\geq$5 shared tenders) versus a permuted mean of
3,271 ($p < 0.001$). The excess of stable FL--winner pairs is
consistent with the persistent co-bidding relationships the
framework emphasizes
(Table~\ref{tab:dyadic_permutation}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{M5: Firm exit in FL-exposed markets.}
A Cox model shows FL exposure is associated with \emph{lower} exit
hazard (HR~$= 0.60$, $p < 0.01$): firms in FL-exposed markets survive
longer, the opposite of crowding-out.%
\footnote{The PH assumption is rejected ($p < 0.001$); the hazard
ratio is descriptive. ``Exit'' means cessation of BEC participation.}
Together with M1, the result suggests FL firms add
noise rather than competition
(Table~\ref{tab:cox_survival}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Strategic adaptation.}
A sophisticated cartel could rotate its cover bidders or let them
win occasionally to stay below the threshold---and the threshold
sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) already suggests
some do. Any operational deployment would therefore need periodic
recalibration alongside bid-level tools
\citep{harrington2008detecting}, which is why the three-stage
workflow in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} treats the screen as a
first stage rather than a final word.

\paragraph{Joint assessment.}
Each diagnostic, taken alone, admits other readings. Taken together,
the pattern---entry without displacement, reference-price anchoring,
small reverse-causality elasticity, excess dyadic linkage, and lower
exit hazard in FL-exposed markets---is hard to square with a simple
competitive account and consistent with coordinated cover bidding.
The diagnostics do not prove the mechanism; they strengthen the case
that the screen is worth deploying.
