\section{Conceptual Framework}
\label{sec:structural}

This section lays out a framework that organizes the screening
intuition and generates five testable diagnostic implications. Cover
bidding is well documented in prosecuted cartels
\citep{porter1999ohio, pesendorfer2000study, asker2010leniency,
marshall2012economics}; the framework stylizes this practice to
clarify when dispersion-based tools lose power and why a
participation-based screen may fill the gap. Full
assumptions, proofs, and the structural likelihood are in
Appendices~\ref{app:proofs} and~\ref{app:structural_id}.

Consider a cartel that controls a designated winner and deploys
$m \geq 0$ cover bidders (FL firms), each bidding above the winning
bid $b^*$. The optimal $m^*$ falls with detection probability
$\theta_k$ and per-bidder cost $c_1$. Under convite, the
minimum-bidder rule ($\underline{n} = 3$) can force participation
even when market conditions would not justify it, mixing mandatory
and voluntary deployments and diluting the FL--price signal
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms}).

Two information structures generate two cover-bidding regimes.
Under \textbf{Regime~1} (complementary), cover bids scatter uniformly
above $b^*$, raising within-tender dispersion. Under \textbf{Regime~2}
(coordinated), cover bids follow a truncated normal centered near
$b^*$, \emph{lowering} dispersion. The implication for detection is direct: under Regime~2,
dispersion-based screens lose power precisely when coordination is
tightest, because cover bids end up where genuine bids would. A
participation-based screen sidesteps this problem---it does not
care how cover bids are distributed, only that cover bidders must
show up.

The model permits \textbf{strategic complementarity}: if the
marginal return to cover bidding is higher in competitive tenders
(many genuine bidders), then $m^*$ \emph{increases} in $n$. The
calibrated model confirms this pattern ($\hat{\gamma} = 0.69 > 0$;
Figure~\ref{fig:corner_solution} and
Appendix~\ref{app:structural_id}), indicating that cartels deploy
more cover bidders precisely where genuine competition is strongest.
This generates a \textbf{market-selection implication}: FL presence
should concentrate in competitive markets (low HHI).

\noindent Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:mechanisms} take these
implications to the data. For enforcement purposes, the framework's
main payoff is clarifying \emph{why} a participation-based screen
may complement dispersion-based tools---and thus motivating its
deployment where bid microdata are unavailable or where Regime~2
coordination renders variance-based screens unreliable. Illustrative
welfare calculations appear in Appendix~\ref{app:extensions}.
