\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

This section develops a simple model of cover-bidder deployment that
generates testable predictions about the minimum-bidder rule. The
model is intentionally spare: we need only the corner-vs-interior
distinction and the enforcement-capacity comparative static to
discipline the empirical analysis. The full structural model,
identification, and estimation appear in a companion paper.%
\footnote{See \citet{genicolomartins2026screening}, Appendix~A.}

\subsection{Setup}

A cartel controls a designated winner in a procurement tender and may
deploy $m \geq 0$ cover bidders---affiliated firms that submit
deliberately losing bids above the cartel's winning bid $b^*$. Each
cover bidder costs $c_1 > 0$ (coordination, side payments, and the
bidder's opportunity cost). Detection occurs with probability
$\theta_k \in (0,1)$ per tender at purchasing unit $k$, with
penalty $F > 0$.

The cartel's per-tender payoff is
\begin{equation}
  \Pi(m) = (b^* - c_0) - m \cdot c_1 - \theta_k \cdot F
  \label{eq:cartel_payoff}
\end{equation}
where $c_0$ is the cartel's cost of production. The cartel chooses
$m$ to maximize $\Pi$ subject to an institutional constraint that
depends on the procurement modality.

\subsection{Corner vs.\ Interior Solution}
\label{sec:corner_interior}

Under \textbf{preg\~ao}, there is no minimum-bidder requirement. The
cartel deploys cover bidders only if the marginal benefit (inflating
apparent competition, anchoring reference prices) exceeds $c_1$.
When the market has enough genuine competitors, the cartel may
optimally set $m^* = 0$. This is the \emph{interior solution}:
deployment is voluntary and responds to economic incentives.

Under \textbf{convite}, Art.\ 22, \S\,3 of Lei 8.666 requires at
least $\underline{n} = 3$ participants. If the number of genuine
competitors is $n_g < 3$, the cartel must deploy at least
$\underline{m} = 3 - n_g$ cover bidders or risk cancellation and
re-advertisement (Art.\ 22, \S\,7). The cartel's problem becomes
\begin{equation}
  m^*_{\text{convite}} = \max\bigl\{\underline{m},\; m^*_{\text{interior}}\bigr\}
  \label{eq:corner}
\end{equation}
where $m^*_{\text{interior}}$ is the unconstrained optimum. When
$\underline{m} > m^*_{\text{interior}}$, the rule forces deployment
beyond what the cartel would choose voluntarily. This is the
\emph{corner solution}: the constraint binds, and the cartel fills
the gap with cover firms.

The corner solution has a direct empirical implication. In convite
tenders where $n_g < 3$, cover-bidder deployment is a \emph{necessary
condition} for the tender to proceed---not a signal of unusually
aggressive collusion, but a mechanical consequence of the rule. The
FL--price coefficient in these tenders reflects a mix of mandatory
and voluntary deployments, compressing the estimate toward zero.

\subsection{Rationality of Cover-Bidder Participation}
\label{sec:rationality}

Cover bidders pay a bidding cost $c > 0$ per tender and earn zero
revenue (win rate = 0). Their expected profit per bid is
\begin{equation}
  E[\pi_{\text{FL}}] = 0 \times \bar{V} \times \text{margin} - c = -c < 0
  \label{eq:fl_profit}
\end{equation}
for any $c > 0$. On BEC, bidding is electronic; $c$ captures staff
time for bid preparation and documentation (1--2 hours at
R\$25--50/hour), yielding $c \in [\text{R\$50}, \text{R\$500}]$ per
tender.

Near the FL classification threshold (14 tenders over 2009--2019),
cumulative losses range from R\$700 to R\$7,000---conceivably
explained by bad luck or learning. At the 90th percentile (50+
tenders), losses reach R\$2,500--R\$25,000. A Bayesian learner with
a uniform prior updates $\Pr(\text{win}) = 1/(n+2)$ after $n$
zero-win trials; after 50 trials, expected gain per bid is
R\$163---below even the lowest bidding cost.%
\footnote{With $\bar{V} = \text{R\$}86{,}000$ (sample mean) and
10\% margin: $0.019 \times 86{,}000 \times 0.10 = \text{R\$}163$.}
Under any standard competitive search model, the firm should exit.
Persistent participation at negative expected profit is consistent
with side payments from a cartel---exactly the compensation term
that makes $m > 0$ individually rational for the cover bidder.

This argument does not prove collusion: firms may win tenders
outside BEC, derive non-monetary value from participation, or simply
behave irrationally. But it establishes that the FL pattern is
\emph{costly to maintain} and therefore requires explanation.

\subsection{Enforcement Capacity}
\label{sec:enforcement}

Detection probability $\theta_k$ varies across purchasing units.
Large PBUs have dedicated procurement staff, internal audit
departments, and higher visibility from the Tribunal de Contas;
small PBUs often have a single procurement officer handling all
modalities. From Equation~\eqref{eq:cartel_payoff}, $\partial
m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$: as detection probability rises, the
cartel deploys fewer cover bidders. The expected FL--price effect
should therefore be largest at low-capacity PBUs and smallest at
high-capacity ones.

\noindent P1 is the baseline association. P2 and P3 exploit the
convite--preg\~ao institutional variation: the minimum-bidder rule
forces deployment in convite, compressing the coefficient relative
to preg\~ao, where only voluntary (and presumably more profitable)

enforcement capacity.

None of these predictions identifies a causal effect. P2 and P4, in
particular, rely on cross-modality and cross-PBU comparisons that
may be confounded by selection. We discuss threats to identification
in Section~\ref{sec:empirical}.
