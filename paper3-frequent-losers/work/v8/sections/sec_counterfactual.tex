\subsection{Counterfactual Welfare Analysis} % V7: structural counterfactuals
\label{sec:counterfactual}

The structural model (Section~\ref{sec:structural}) generates
counterfactual predictions about how procurement outcomes change under
alternative policy regimes. We compute three counterfactuals, each
corresponding to a distinct policy lever: (i)~eliminating the
minimum-bidder requirement, (ii)~calibrating the optimal screening
threshold, and (iii)~increasing the detection probability.

% ── CF1: Minimum-bidder rule ─────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Counterfactual 1: Removing the minimum-bidder requirement.}
Under convite procurement (Lei~8.666/93, Art.~22, \S7), the procuring
agency must receive at least three bids. Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m}
implies that when a cartel controls the designated winner $j^*$ and
$n < 3$ genuine bidders participate, the cartel \emph{must} deploy
$m \geq 3 - n$ cover bidders---a corner solution driven entirely by
the institutional constraint rather than strategic optimization. In the
data, 41.3\% of convite FL-present tenders have fewer than three genuine
bidders, and 25.2\% have \emph{zero} genuine bidders (the cartel is the
sole real participant).

If the minimum-bidder rule were removed (setting $\underline{n}(\tau) =
0$ for all modalities), Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m} implies that
$m^*$ drops to the interior solution for these constrained tenders. For
tenders with $n = 0$ genuine bidders, the cartel faces no genuine
competition and the marginal return to cover bidding $\partial \pi /
\partial m$ approaches zero---the interior solution is $m^* = 0$. For
tenders with $n \in \{1, 2\}$, the cartel may still deploy cover
bidders for strategic reasons (noise creation, reference-price
manipulation), but the \emph{mandatory} component is eliminated.

We compute the welfare gain from removing the rule under two
assumptions. Under the \emph{full-elimination} scenario (all
constraint-binding cover bidding ceases), the price reduction for
the 47,611 affected tenders is $\exp(\hat{\beta}) - 1 = 6.6\%$,
yielding total savings of R\$74 million over 2009--2019 (R\$6.7
million per year). Under the \emph{partial-elimination} scenario
(only $n = 0$ tenders---28,561 pure cover-bidding tenders---lose all
cover bidders), savings are R\$41 million. Using the IV coefficient as
an upper bound, the full-elimination welfare gain rises to R\$211
million. These figures represent lower bounds: they assume cartels
do not strategically adapt by redeploying cover bidders to preg\~ao
tenders.

Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual_welfare} reports the full results. The welfare
gain requires no enforcement expenditure---it is a pure institutional
reform that removes the cartel's regulatory incentive for cover
bidding.

% ── CF2: Optimal screening threshold ─────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Counterfactual 2: Welfare-maximizing screening threshold.}
The FL screen's ROC curve (Table~\ref{tab:roc_detection}) maps each
IQR multiplier $t$ to a detection rate $\text{TPR}(t)$ and a
false-positive rate $\text{FPR}(t)$. We embed this ROC curve in a
welfare function that trades off detection benefits against enforcement
costs:
\begin{equation}
  W(t) = \underbrace{\delta \cdot \hat{\beta} \cdot
  S_{\text{FL}}(t)}_{\text{deterrence benefit}}
  - \underbrace{c_{\text{inv}} \cdot N_{\text{flagged}}(t)
  }_{\text{investigation cost}}
  \label{eq:welfare_screening}
\end{equation}
where $\delta \in [0,1]$ is the deterrence rate (fraction of cartels
that cease cover bidding after investigation), $\hat{\beta} = 0.064$
is the OLS price effect, $S_{\text{FL}}(t)$ is total spending in
tenders involving firms flagged under threshold $t$,
$c_{\text{inv}}$ is the per-firm investigation cost, and
$N_{\text{flagged}}(t)$ is the number of flagged firms.

The optimal threshold $t^*$ solves $\partial W / \partial t = 0$,
balancing the marginal detection benefit (higher $t$ reduces
$N_{\text{flagged}}$ and $S_{\text{FL}}$, lowering both benefit and
cost) against the marginal investigation cost. We vary the
investigation cost $c_{\text{inv}}$ from R\$10,000 to R\$1,000,000
per firm, spanning the range from automated screening (desk review)
to full forensic investigation (document subpoena, economic expert).

Figure~\ref{fig:counterfactual_screening} plots $t^*$ as a function
of $c_{\text{inv}}$ for deterrence rates $\delta \in \{0.25, 0.50,
0.75, 1.0\}$. At intermediate investigation costs typical of
competition-authority preliminary reviews (R\$100,000 per firm),
the optimal threshold is $t^* = 1.6\times$IQR with a deterrence rate
of $\delta = 0.50$, yielding net welfare of R\$135 million over
2009--2019 (2,537 flagged firms). This is close to the paper's
baseline of 1.5$\times$ and the Youden-optimal 1.45$\times$.
At high investigation costs ($c_{\text{inv}} > \text{R\$500,000}$),
screening becomes welfare-negative: the cost of investigating false
positives exceeds the deterrence benefit. The screening threshold
used in this paper (1.5$\times$IQR) is approximately
welfare-maximizing for investigation costs in the R\$100,000 range,
consistent with the per-case cost of preliminary CADE investigations
in Brazil.

% ── CF3: Detection probability ───────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Counterfactual 3: Increasing detection probability.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m} predicts that $m^*$ is decreasing
in $\theta_k$: higher detection probability raises the marginal cost
of cover bidding ($c_1 + \theta_k \phi_0$), reducing the optimal
number of cover bidders. We calibrate this comparative static using
cross-PBU variation in oversight intensity.

We proxy $\theta_k$ with PBU size (number of annual procurements),
under the assumption that larger PBUs have more procurement staff and
more standardized oversight procedures. Dividing PBUs into quartiles
by annual procurement volume, we compute the mean FL count per tender
within each quartile. If the model is correct, the mean FL count
should decrease with PBU size.

Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual_welfare}, Panel~C reports the results. The mean
FL count decreases from the smallest (Q1) to the largest (Q4) PBU
quartile, consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m}: cartels
deploy fewer cover bidders when facing more intense oversight. The
implied semi-elasticity of $m$ with respect to $\theta$ suggests that
a 10\% increase in detection probability (achievable through
systematic FL screening) would eliminate approximately one cover
bidder per tender in the median FL-present market, with an associated
annual price saving computed from the structural markup.
