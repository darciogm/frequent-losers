\begin{abstract}
Cartel enforcement begins before legal proof exists. Agencies
often observe contract-award records long before they recover
the bid-level evidence needed to prove coordination. This paper
asks how that cheap award layer should allocate costly forensic
attention---an enforcement-design question of the order in which
an agency spends cheap and costly evidence layers. Using
S\~ao Paulo's BEC procurement platform
($\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$) and CADE adjudications, we
construct a \emph{frequent-loser} screen from award records
alone: among zero-win firms, persistent participation ranks
loser-side cartel-adjacency risk. Used sequentially as a
gatekeeper, the award-layer ranking reduces the bid-microdata
pool for the forensic stage by \valGateFootprintPct{} while
recovering \valGateSeqKTwoKOneTPIn{} of \valCobidders{}
adjudicated cobidders, and it adds non-redundant information to
bid-distribution forensics on the same target. The screen
targets forensic priority, not cartel membership: validated
against adjudication-anchored cobidder labels and disciplined
against participation, opportunity-set exposure, timing, and
leakage threats, it ranks loser-side exposure while remaining
near-random against direct defendants---a deliberate scope
boundary, since liability lives in the richer evidentiary record.
Cheap administrative records can discipline where
proof-producing investigation should begin.
\end{abstract}
