
\section{Robustness and Extensions}
\label{sec:robustness}


We subject the baseline to five groups of checks:
(i)~FL-definition variants (IQR multiplier, win-rate cutoff, temporal
window); (ii)~sample and specification variants (unrestricted sample,
homogeneous subsamples, item$\times$year FE, matching);
(iii)~alternative clustering; (iv)~sensitivity to unobservables
(Cinelli--Hazlett); and (v)~staggered DiD.
Table~\ref{tab:robustness_summary} collects the point estimates, with
an extra column spelling out what each estimator assumes.

\paragraph{Threshold sensitivity.}
The FL coefficient decreases monotonically with stricter thresholds:
0.079 (IQR 1.0$\times$), 0.064 (baseline), 0.060 (2.0$\times$),
0.050 (3.0$\times$). The most likely explanation is classification noise: stricter
thresholds shrink the treated group, leaving fewer FL-present tenders
and more tenders misclassified as untreated. One could also read this
as evidence that the marginal FL firms (10--14 tenders) sit in markets
with larger cartel markups, while tail firms face more detection
pressure. The noise story is simpler and sufficient. A
two-dimensional sensitivity analysis varying both the IQR multiplier
(1.0--3.0$\times$) and the win-rate cutoff (0--5\%) produces
significant coefficients across all 36 cells
(Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_heatmap}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}),
confirming that the result does not hinge on the specific FL
definition.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to unobservables.}
\citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity analysis yields $RV_{q=1} =
17.5\%$: an unobserved confounder would need to explain 17.5\% of
residual variation in \emph{both} FL presence and log prices to nullify
the coefficient---stringent given the item, year, and PBU fixed-effects
structure. Full sensitivity plots are in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}.


\paragraph{Staggered difference-in-differences.}
A \citet{callaway2021difference} staggered DiD yields ATT~$= 0.014$
(SE~$= 0.039$). A stacked regression following
\citet{cengiz2019effect}---which avoids the ``bad comparison''
problem via cohort-specific market and year fixed effects---produces
ATT~$= -0.006$ (SE~$= 0.014$;
Table~\ref{tab:stacked_did}, Appendix~\ref{app:did}).
The stacked 95\% CI ($-0.034$, $0.022$) excludes the OLS
baseline of 0.064, while the C\&S CI is wide enough to include
it. The discrepancy is expected: positive pre-trend coefficients
at $t = -2$ and $t = -3$ (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) indicate
that FL entry is endogenous to market conditions, violating the
parallel-trends assumption that both estimators require. The DiD
designs are therefore \emph{invalid} for this setting, not merely
underpowered---precisely why the paper relies on conditional
comparisons rather than event-study identification.
\citet{rambachan2023more} sensitivity analysis and
event-study plots are in Appendix~\ref{app:did}.

\paragraph{Coefficient stability (Oster).}
We also compute the \citet{oster2019unobservable} bound
$\hat{\delta}$, which asks how large selection on unobservables would
need to be, relative to observables, to drive $\hat{\beta}$ to zero.
The result is $\hat{\delta} = 261.6$, but this number is
uninformative in this setting: because adding PBU fixed effects
barely changes $R^2$ (from 0.879 to 0.886), the denominator of
the Oster formula is near zero, making $\hat{\delta}$ mechanically
large. The bias-adjusted coefficient $\beta^*(\delta = 1)$ is
correspondingly near zero---not because unobservables destroy the
result, but because the Oster formula cannot discriminate when the
short and full specifications absorb nearly identical variation.
The \citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity analysis ($RV = 17.5\%$),
which does not depend on the $R^2$ gap, is the more informative
bound for this design
(Table~\ref{tab:oster_delta}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).


\paragraph{Illustrative welfare.}
Appendix~\ref{app:extensions} reports back-of-the-envelope welfare
calculations that mechanically apply the price coefficients to
FL-present spending. These figures assume full causality, which the
paper does not establish, and should be read as rough benchmarks
rather than policy estimates.


\paragraph{Oversight heterogeneity.}
The FL price effect varies steeply with PBU size: 0.214 in the
smallest quartile, 0.098 in Q2, 0.045 in Q3, and 0.017 in Q4
(Table~\ref{tab:regime_oversight}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).
The 12.5$\times$ gradient between Q1 and Q4 is the single largest
source of heterogeneity in the data---far larger than the
preg\~ao--convite gap---and runs in the direction the structural
model implies ($\partial m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$): smaller PBUs
with less oversight capacity see more FL activity and higher
associated price gaps. An alternative reading is that smaller PBUs
have thinner item--PBU cells, so item FE absorb less within-market
variation and the coefficient picks up residual heterogeneity. The
gradient is monotonic across all four quartiles, which is hard to
generate from pure FE noise, but we cannot fully rule it out.
Threshold stability across IQR multipliers and a comparison between
the IQR rule and model-based classifiers are in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}.
