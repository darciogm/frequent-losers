Dear Editor,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, **"Cheap Signals, Costly Proof: The
Reach and Limits of Award-Layer Screening in Cartel Enforcement,"** by Darcio
Genicolo-Martins and Paulo Furquim de Azevedo (both INSPER), for consideration
at the *Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization*. The manuscript is submitted
**exclusively** to JLEO; it is not under review at any other journal and has not
been published elsewhere.

The paper asks how far cheap administrative records can take cartel enforcement
before costly legal proof is produced. Enforcement agencies routinely observe
**award-layer** records — who participated and who won — long before they can
recover the richer **bid-layer** record needed to build a case. We study how
award-layer statistics can help allocate scarce proof-producing investigative
effort: a question of enforcement design under costly and incomplete
observability that sits squarely at the intersection of law, economics, and
organization.

The manuscript makes three contributions. First, it offers an
**evidence-allocation framing** that separates the cheap, legally thin
award layer from the costly, proof-producing bid layer and asks what the former
can order. Second, it provides an **adjudication-anchored validation** of a
minimal loser-side construct — persistent zero-win participation among
always-loser firms — against CADE adjudications, with a decomposition that
disciplines the result for procurement opportunity, case concentration, and
timing rather than reporting raw discrimination. Third, it develops a
**sequential award→bid cost-recall operating frontier** that organizes when an
agency should pay to recover bid records.

We are deliberately careful about the **boundaries** of the construct. It ranks
**forensic priority**; it does not classify cartel membership, prove liability,
or estimate damages. Co-bidders linked to adjudicated cases are described as
**adjudication-anchored exposure**, not as cartel members, and price differences
are treated as **scope evidence**, not as overcharge or damages. The bid layer
remains the proof-producing stage; the paper maps where cheap records can, and
cannot, order investigative effort.

On data and replication: the study uses São Paulo's BEC procurement platform
(2009–2019; 1,654,401 tender-items) together with CADE adjudications as legal
anchors. CADE rulings are public; the **BEC procurement microdata are
administrative and confidential** and cannot be redistributed, so we intend to
request the JLEO **proprietary-data exemption** for them. We will provide
analysis code, derived/anonymized firm-level frames, and an output-to-script map
so that the results can be reproduced by re-running on access-controlled inputs.
