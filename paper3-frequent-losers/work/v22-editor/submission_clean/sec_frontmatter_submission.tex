\begin{abstract}
Enforcement agencies must govern suspicion before they can produce
proof. Routine procurement-award records are cheap and broad but
legally thin, while the bid-level records close to evidence are
costly to recover. We study the minimal award-layer screen---persistent
zero-win participation, implemented as a frequent-loser flag and not
a legal category---and audit it against a reproducible,
adjudication-anchored cobidder label that never uses the flag.
Decomposing apparent screening power into opportunity exposure,
timing, and case concentration, we find the raw concentration real
but mostly mechanical exposure rather than conduct; strict
prospective ranking fails outside incumbent pools. The contribution
is a transferable governance protocol, stress-tested on a second
procurement platform, together with a cost--recall frontier that tells an agency
when cheap suspicion should trigger costly bid-layer follow-up.
Liability stays where the evidence is richest.
\end{abstract}
