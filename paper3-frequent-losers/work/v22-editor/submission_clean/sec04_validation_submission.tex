\section{Validating the Loser-Side Ranking}
\label{sec:validation}

This section validates the award-layer ranking, not the legal
status of ranked firms. The ranking is useful for enforcement
only if it orders the loser-side firms for which bid-layer
follow-up is most valuable. The validation target is therefore
the one defined in Section~\ref{sec:cade_adjacency_labels}:
always-loser firms observed alongside direct CADE defendants in
adjudicated procurement-cartel environments. These cobidders are
adjudication-anchored exposure labels. They are not treated as
legal members of a cartel.

The validation exercise has to do more than report a high AUC.
The main empirical threat is exposure. Firms that participate
often, or that operate in the same procurement environments as
CADE defendants, have more opportunities to appear near those
anchors even under ordinary competition. Procurement-screen
designs therefore have to separate suspicious conduct from market
structure, bidder heterogeneity, and ordinary repeated
participation \citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding}.
Network and large-scale procurement screens sharpen the same
concern \citep{conley2016detecting,kawai2022detecting}. A useful award-layer
screen must therefore concentrate cartel-adjacent loser-side
firms beyond what participation volume and opportunity-set exposure alone
would imply. A second threat is timing: an enforcement screen
should rank candidates before the target information is used.
A third threat is legal overreach: a loser-side screen should
not be read as a generic cartel-membership detector.

This section evaluates the ranking against those threats.

\subsection{Label Construction and Sample Reconciliation}
\label{sec:label_funnel}

The validation labels pass through a short construction funnel,
and it is worth fixing the funnel before reading any benchmark.
The full CADE legal portfolio comprises $12$ adjudicated
procurement-cartel cases with $65$ legal firm-defendants. We
match these defendants to BEC participant records by exact
$14$-digit CNPJ equality, with no fuzzy or manual step; of the
matched defendants, $\valDirectCADE$ are active in BEC and define
the direct-defendant set. Their defendant tender-items
($\valFunnelDefItems$ distinct items) locate the cartel-affected
bidding environments. Within those environments we identify the
always-loser stratum and, inside it, the cobidder label: a
zero-win firm sharing at least one tender-item with a direct
defendant.

The main validation target is the $\valCobidders$ always-loser
frequent-loser cobidders defined under a narrow cartel-tender
restriction, and we keep it as the primary benchmark for
comparability with prior drafts. A fully transparent, scripted
alternative---counting any always-loser sharing a tender-item
with a BEC-active direct defendant over the full portfolio
(FL14$\,=\,$tenders\_count$\,\geq\,$14)---yields
$\valFunnelFLcobBroad$ frequent-loser and $\valFunnelALcobBroad$
always-loser cobidders, overlapping the main target in
$\valFunnelStaticOverlap$ firms. We disclose one replication
caveat cleanly: the original builder of the narrow target is
archived and is not reproduced by the current scripts. We
therefore report the transparent funnel as a reproducible
robustness target and retain the narrow target as the primary
benchmark.

The conservative pre-$\valConservativeCutoff$ benchmark is not a
mechanical subset of the main validation target. It restricts the
portfolio to the $\valConservativeCases$ cases adjudicated by
end-$\valConservativeCutoff$ but applies the broad
shared-tender-item cobidder definition to the wider always-loser
stratum, yielding $\valConservativeCobidders$ always-loser
cobidders ($\valConservativeFL$ of them frequent losers) from
$\valConservativeFD$ BEC-active defendants. That a smaller case
set yields more cobidders than the $\valCobidders$ main target
reflects two definitional differences, not a coding error: the
conservative count is taken over the always-loser stratum rather
than the narrower frequent-loser subset, and under the broad
rather than the narrow cobidder definition. Under a common
definition the subset relation is restored: the conservative
always-loser (frequent-loser) cobidder set is a strict subset of
the full-portfolio $\valFunnelALcobBroad$
($\valFunnelFLcobBroad$). Table~\ref{tab:label_funnel_submission}
reports every benchmark side by side.

\subsection{Baseline Adjudication Benchmarks}
\label{sec:validation_baseline}

The baseline asks a simple question before adding stricter
discipline: do firms flagged by the award-layer rule appear
disproportionately among always-loser firms located near
adjudicated cartel anchors? The answer is yes. In the
conservative adjudication benchmark, we restrict the CADE
portfolio to cases resolved before the end of
$\valConservativeCutoff$. This leaves $\valConservativeCases$
cases, $\valConservativeFD$ BEC-active firm-defendants, and
$\valConservativeCobidders$ always-loser cobidders. Among those
cobidders, $\valConservativeFL$ are frequent losers.

This benchmark uses early legal anchors and asks whether the
award-layer rank concentrates the associated loser-side
footprint. Within the always-loser stratum, the observed
frequent-loser share among conservative cobidders is
$\valCADEenrich$, compared with a participation-stratified
placebo mean of $\valCADEbase$. The excess ratio is
$\valMultThreeTwo\!\times$ with $p<0.001$. The corresponding ROC
AUC is $\valAUCprePost$ (95\% DeLong CI $\valAUCprePostCI$)
\citep{delong1988comparing}.
These numbers establish the baseline empirical fact: persistent
zero-win participation is not random with respect to
adjudication-anchored loser-side exposure.

The second benchmark adds timing discipline. The score is
computed from $2009$--$2016$ participation and evaluated against
$2017$--$2019$ cobidder labels. This is a temporal holdout, not a
real-time field deployment: it asks whether earlier award records
rank firms whose cartel adjacency is later observed in the
adjudication-anchored record. The firm-level AUC is
$\valAUCFLfirmTemp$ (95\% CI $\valAUCFLfirmTempCI$), below the
full-sample AUC $\valAUCFLfirm$ but well separated from random
ranking. Figure~\ref{fig:temporal_holdout_roc_submission} reports the
rolling-origin version of the same exercise.

The baseline results are deliberately read as ranking evidence.
They show that the award-layer statistic is informative about
where cartel-adjacent loser-side exposure later appears. But a
high AUC on this benchmark is not yet enough: firms that
participate often, or that operate in the same procurement
environments as CADE defendants, mechanically have more
opportunities to appear near those anchors even under ordinary
competition. The next subsection is therefore the central
exercise of the paper. It asks how much of the ranking survives
once procurement opportunity is held comparable, and isolates the
score's contribution net of exposure.

\subsection{Opportunity-Adjusted Validation}
\label{sec:validation_exposure}
\label{sec:validation_exposure_timing}

This is the central validation exercise. A high raw AUC is not
sufficient evidence that the award-layer score captures anything
beyond procurement opportunity. The threat is mechanical. A firm
with high participation volume $T_i$ co-appears with CADE
defendants more often by construction, and a firm operating in
the same item, buyer, year, and modality cells as those
defendants is mechanically more exposed to them even under
ordinary competition. Because the raw score is increasing in
participation, part of its apparent discrimination is opportunity,
not conduct. The exercise below holds opportunity comparable and
asks what the score adds on top of it.

We measure opportunity directly. For each always-loser firm we
count its observed contact with direct defendants, $O_i$, defined
as the number of procurement cells it shares with a defendant. We
then construct expected contact under random matching,
$E_i=\sum_g n_{ig}\,p_g$, where $n_{ig}$ is the firm's
participation in opportunity cell $g$ and $p_g$ is the
defendant-density of that cell. We build $E_i$ at three cell
granularities (coarse to fine, defined by item, buyer, year, and
modality combinations) and restrict to the common support on
which expected contact is identified; the exposed subsample
retains $n=\valExpExposedN$ always-loser firms containing all
$\valExpNpos$ positives. The construction and the retained
support are detailed in \ref{app:market_opportunity_submission}.
Excess contact $O_i-E_i>0$ flags firms that meet defendants more
often than their opportunity set predicts. We hold $E_i$ fixed
and ask three questions: does the score reproduce the label
better than exposure alone; does it add anything net of exposure;
and does it rank firms within equal-opportunity strata.

The honest answer has two parts. First, exposure is a strong
baseline. An exposure-only model---no score---reproduces the
adjudication-anchored label at ROC-AUC $\valExpOnlyAUC$ in the
exposed subsample, \emph{above} the raw score's
$\valExpRawScoreAUC$, and on the rare-target metric that matters
its PR-AUC ($\valExpExpOnlyPRAUC$) also exceeds the raw score's
($\valExpRawScorePRAUC$). A meaningful share of the raw ranking
performance is opportunity exposure, and pure exposure reproduces
the label at least as well as the raw score. We state this
plainly because the combined exposure-plus-score logits reach
ROC-AUC near $0.98$ (Table~\ref{tab:opportunity_adjusted_validation});
those numbers are mechanically inflated because $E_i$ is built
from the cells where defendants live and therefore encodes the
label directly. We do not read them as evidence that the score
works, and we report them only to make the inflation visible.

Second, conditional on opportunity the score retains limited but
statistically significant residual value. The clean tests are
two. A nested model that adds the score to an exposure-only model
raises AUC by $\valExpIncrement$ (DeLong
$p=\valExpDeLongP$)---a small but precisely estimated increment
that is the score's isolated contribution, not the inflated
combined fit. And \emph{within} opportunity strata, where firms
are compared only against others with comparable expected
contact, the continuous score still ranks the label at AUC
$\valExpWithinAUC$ (FL14 $\valExpWithinFLAUC$): well above chance,
and far below the raw $\valExpRawScoreAUC$. The score also ranks
positive excess contact, $O_i-E_i>0$, at AUC
$\valExpScoreExcessAUC$. Within-stratum matching tells the same
story: the frequent-loser flag carries a within-exposure-stratum
gap in cobidder probability of $\valExpMatchedDelta$ relative to
non-flagged always-losers, and a coarsened-exact opportunity match
yields AUC $\valExpCEMmatchedAUC$.
Figure~\ref{fig:observed_vs_expected_contact} shows observed
versus expected defendant contact across score bins: the gap
$O_i-E_i$ is positive in the top score bins, which is the
within-opportunity signal made visible.

A permutation test disciplines the mechanical-exposure channel,
and we report both directions honestly because they are not the
same.
Table~\ref{tab:opportunity_permutation} reports both.
Under Approach~B, which simulates contact from a pure-exposure
binomial null, the observed PR-AUC $\valExpRawScorePRAUC$ does
\emph{not} beat the null (null mean $0.264$; empirical
$p=\valExpPermBp$): a pure-exposure model out-predicts the raw
score on the rare-target metric. Under Approach~C, which permutes
cobidder labels only \emph{within} matched exposure-by-participation
strata, the observed PR-AUC \emph{exceeds} the null
($p=\valExpPermCp$): once opportunity is matched, the labels are
non-randomly score-ranked. The frequent-loser enrichment beats
both nulls ($p<\valExpFLenrichP$). The synthesis is Verdict~B: a
pure-exposure model can match or beat the raw score on PR-AUC, yet
within matched exposure strata the score still ranks, the nested
increment is significant, and the FL enrichment survives.
Figure~\ref{fig:opportunity_permutation} shows the
exposure-preserving null distribution against the observed value.

The conservative reading is the one we carry forward. The
award-layer score does not reproduce the adjudication-anchored
label any better than firms' procurement opportunity does. What it
adds, net of opportunity, is a small and statistically reliable
residual ordering. That residual is a legal-economic object of a
specific kind: it is triage value, a reason to look first at one
loser-side firm rather than another at the bid-layer stage. It is
not proof of collusion, not a cartel-membership test, and not a
causal claim. Cobidders are not members; the score prioritizes
follow-up, it does not adjudicate.

The remaining threats are timing, leakage, and case composition,
and they are taken up next. The same ex ante logic applied to the
score holds under temporal discipline: scoring on
$\valYearStart$--$2016$ participation and testing later cobidder
labels gives firm-level AUC $\valAUCFLfirmTemp$, with firm
persistence of only $\valFirmPersist$ across windows (the test
population is largely fresh firms); refreezing the screen
definition itself before the window leaves frequent-loser AUC
$\valAUCStrictFirmFL$ and continuous AUC $\valAUCStrictFirmTC$;
and a leakage audit that holds cobidder firms out of score
construction leaves out-of-fold AUC $\valLeakCVAUC$ against an
in-sample reference of $\valLeakRefAUC$.
Tables~\ref{tab:three_classifier_submission} and the scope matrix
in Section~\ref{sec:validation_direct} report the full battery.
One composition fact motivates a dedicated case-robustness check:
the single largest CADE case contributes roughly
$\valExpTopCaseTPshare$ of true positives at the top
$500$ of the ranking, approaching dominance, so a
leave-one-case-out test is required before the residual signal can
be read as case-general rather than case-specific.

\subsection{Direct-Defendant Scope Check}
\label{sec:validation_direct}

The direct-defendant exercise aligns the validation target with
the screen's legal-economic role. Direct CADE defendants are the
legal anchors that locate adjudicated cartel environments. They
need not be persistent zero-win firms: in procurement cartels,
defendants may occupy winner-side, allocation, or rotating roles.
The award-layer statistic, by construction, ranks the loser side
of the procurement record. The empirical prediction is therefore
asymmetry: the rank should be informative for cobidders and much
less informative for direct defendants.

The data follow that prediction. Against direct defendants in
the broader BEC firm universe, AUC is $\valAUCdirectStd$, close
to random classification. This is the scope check doing its
work. If the statistic were a generic cartel-membership detector,
it should rank direct defendants as well as cobidders. It does
not, which is the expected result for a zero-win loser-side
ranking. Read together with the cobidder results, this pattern
supports the staged interpretation. Direct defendants anchor
cartel-affected environments; the award layer ranks the
loser-side exposure around those anchors; the bid layer then
supplies the conduct evidence for case-specific evaluation.

A complementary universe-anchored scope matrix systematically
varies the universe (always-loser firms vs full BEC firm
registry) and the positive class (cobidders vs direct defendants)
to map where the ranking targets succeed and where they fail. The
matrix produces a stronger result than a stand-alone null: raw
participation count on the full BEC universe against direct CADE
defendants returns AUC $\valAUCParticDefendants$, which is
\emph{below} random. A high-participation BEC firm is less
likely, not more likely, to be a direct CADE defendant under the
loser-side scoring logic. The score does not simply fail to
identify winner-heavy defendants; the relevant direction is
reversed, consistent with the role separation observed in D4
(\valDirectShareAL\% of direct defendants are always-losers; median win rate
$\valDirectMedWR$ versus $\valOthersMedWR$ for cobidders).

scope matrix.

\subsection{Validation Summary}
\label{sec:validation_summary}

Table~\ref{tab:validation_benchmarks_submission} is a threat map
rather than a proof table. Each row corresponds to a threat
identified before the tests are read: legal anchoring,
participation volume, market exposure, timing, leakage, and
scope asymmetry. The resulting claim is staged: the award-layer
ranking supports a priority queue for the bid-layer stage, where
tender-level conduct can be examined with richer evidence.
