Dear Editors,

We submit "Cheap Signals, Costly Proof: The Reach and Limits of Award-Layer Screening in Cartel Enforcement" for consideration at the Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization.

Instead of proposing a standalone algorithmic cartel screen, the paper contributes an organizational framework for auditing the reach, boundaries, and apparent performance of cheap administrative screens within public procurement platforms—before agencies commit to opening costly bid-level records. The underlying problem is one of enforcement design: how an agency should allocate proof-producing effort when routine contract-award data are cheap and abundant, but the bid-level evidence that establishes conduct must be recovered through expensive, case-specific processes. We treat suspicion, investigation, and proof as stages to be sequenced under costly observability, and we ask how far the cheap layer can responsibly order that sequence.

The empirical screen we examine is deliberately minimal. It is a persistent zero-win participation flag—a frequent-loser marker built only from award records—and not a legal category or a claim about any firm's conduct. We hold it to a validation label that is reproducible and non-circular: a positive is a unique always-loser firm that shares at least one tender-item with a BEC-active direct CADE defendant (651 such cobidders in the São Paulo BEC setting, 2009–2019). Direct defendants are excluded from the label, and the loser-side flag is never used to construct it, so the target the screen is graded against is independent of the screen itself.

The audit is designed to deflate, not to advertise. We separate raw award-layer concentration from procurement-opportunity exposure, from timing, and from case concentration, and we discipline the resulting order with volume placebos, exposure adjustment over comparable opportunity sets, a bid-layer benchmark, and a direct-defendant scope check. Within comparable opportunity sets, the residual ordering net of opportunity is marginal at best and not robust across designs. That boundary is the institutional finding. We then organize the award→bid decision as a cost-recall frontier: an operating point that an agency may choose given its resources, not an optimum we recommend. Legal liability is never located in the score; it remains in the richer evidentiary record, where bid-level conduct, communications, leniency material, and case-specific facts are weighed.

To show that what travels is the protocol rather than the score, the revised manuscript stress-tests the same audit, unchanged, on a second procurement system—the federal ComprasNet platform (2013–2019), which shares partially overlapping CADE legal anchors with the BEC setting, is effectively pure Pregão, and exposes no bid microdata. The same battery returns the same deflationary verdict, which is the cleanest evidence we can offer that the contribution is portability of the audit logic, not portability of a particular ranking.

We believe the paper fits JLEO because its central object is an enforcement institution rather than a classifier: how administrative suspicion should be converted into investigative priority, and where the line between suspicion and legal proof should fall, when information is costly. The manuscript makes no claim to identify cartel members, estimate damages, or replace bid-layer forensics; it proposes and evaluates a legally limited triage framework and is candid about how little the cheap layer can do on its own.

The manuscript is submitted exclusively to JLEO; it is not under review elsewhere and has not been published before. CADE rulings are public, but the BEC procurement microdata are administrative and confidential and cannot be redistributed, so we intend to request the JLEO proprietary-data exemption for them; we will provide analysis code, derived and anonymized firm-level frames, and an output-to-script map so the results can be reproduced on access-controlled inputs.

We are grateful for your consideration.
