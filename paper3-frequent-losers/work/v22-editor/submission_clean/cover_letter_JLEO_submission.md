Dear Editors,

We submit "Cheap Signals, Costly Proof: The Reach and Limits of Award-Layer Screening in Cartel Enforcement" for consideration at the Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization. The paper studies an enforcement-design problem that arises before legal proof exists: how should an agency allocate costly forensic attention when routine contract-award records are available but bid-level evidence must be recovered through more expensive, case-specific processes?

The paper's contribution is not a claim that an award-layer screen identifies cartel members. It is a framework for auditing how far cheap administrative records can order forensic priority before agencies open costly bid-level records. In the BEC–CADE setting, the audit is deliberately deflationary: much raw performance reflects procurement opportunity, timing, and case concentration. That boundary is the institutional point. Existing bid-distribution screens are valuable once bid microdata are available; we study the prior institutional stage, asking whether the cheaper award layer can order where bid-layer forensics should begin. The distinction is legal as well as empirical: award records can create forensic priority, but they cannot establish agreement, liability, or damages.

The empirical setting is São Paulo's BEC procurement platform from 2009 to 2019, linked to CADE procurement-cartel adjudications. We build the validation label by adjudication-anchored construction: a positive is a unique always-loser firm that shares at least one tender-item with a BEC-active direct CADE defendant. Direct defendants are excluded, and the loser-side flag is never used to construct the label, so the validation target is non-circular and reproducible. We then treat validation as a set of threats rather than a single performance statistic, disciplining the ranking with participation-volume placebos, exposure adjustment over comparable opportunity sets, timing tests, case-composition audits, a bid-layer benchmark, and a direct-defendant scope check. The verdict is honest: within comparable opportunity sets the residual ordering net of opportunity is marginal at best and not robust across designs.

The transferable object is the decomposition framework — adjudication-anchored label construction, opportunity adjustment, timing discipline, case-composition audit, bid-layer comparison, and cost-recall accounting — not a portable cartel score. The estimated BEC ranking is case-sensitive and should be read as retrospective, incumbent-firm triage, not as platform-wide prospective deployment. We organize the award→bid decision as a sequential cost-recall frontier, reporting recovery-footprint reductions against explicit denominators rather than a single "optimal" cutoff or unqualified cost-saving claim. Legal liability remains in the richer evidentiary record, where bid-level conduct, communications, leniency material, and case-specific facts are evaluated. To show that this transferable object is more than an assertion about one platform, the revised manuscript re-runs the entire audit, unchanged, on a second procurement system — the federal ComprasNet platform (2013–2019), which shares partially overlapping CADE legal anchors with the BEC setting, is effectively pure Pregão, and exposes no bid microdata; the same battery returns the same deflationary verdict there, which is the cleanest evidence we can offer that what generalizes is the audit protocol, not the raw screen.

We believe the paper fits JLEO because its central object is not a classifier but an enforcement institution: how suspicion, investigation, and proof should be sequenced when information is costly. This is a question of enforcement design and evidence allocation under costly observability, at the intersection of law, economics, and organization. The paper speaks to the organization of antitrust enforcement and the boundary between administrative suspicion and legal proof.

The manuscript does not claim to identify cartel members, estimate damages, or replace bid-layer forensics. It proposes and evaluates a legally limited triage framework: cheap administrative records can order where proof-producing investigation should begin, while adjudication remains grounded in richer case evidence.

The manuscript is submitted exclusively to JLEO; it is not under review elsewhere and has not been published before. CADE rulings are public, but the BEC procurement microdata are administrative and confidential and cannot be redistributed, so we intend to request the JLEO proprietary-data exemption for them; we will provide analysis code, derived/anonymized firm-level frames, and an output-to-script map so the results can be reproduced on access-controlled inputs.
