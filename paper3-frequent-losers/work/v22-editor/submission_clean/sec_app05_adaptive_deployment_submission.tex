\subsection{Instability of a Published Cutoff Under Strategic Response (Retrospective)}
\label{app:strategic_adaptation_submission}

This appendix quantifies the instability of a published administrative cutoff
under strategic response. Because Section~\ref{sec:screening_forensics}
establishes no conduct-specific residual, these simulations are not a
robustness defense of a behavioral signal; they are an institutional-design
exercise: how quickly does a static public threshold degrade as a triage device
when participants react to it? The diagnostic holds the empirical environment
fixed and asks how the participation score behaves under simple evasive
responses. It is a retrospective probe, not a fully prospective deployment test:
the labels remain adjudication-anchored, and the operating points should be
read as conditional on the validated incumbent ranking. Rotation of
losing-role firms and occasional small wins leave discrimination
essentially unchanged ($\valAUCAdaptRotation$ and
$\valAUCAdaptOccWins$ versus baseline $\valAUCBase$), absorbed by the
persistence signal; CNPJ splitting lowers AUC to $\valAUCAdaptCNPJsplit$
by breaking participation history; threshold-aware capping is the most
damaging ($\valAUCAdaptThreshCap$, and $\valAUCAdaptCombined$ combined
with rotation). The hierarchy maps to the cost--recall frontier: under an
incumbent-triage reading the rule should be refreshed as new participation
data arrive, and continuous ranks or capacity-constrained top-$k$ queues
should be preferred over a static published cutoff, since a published
threshold invites bunching (consistent with the local density ratio
$\approx 1.06$ at $T=14$ in the profile appendix, where the threshold was
computed ex post and never disclosed) and is the most gameable rule. The
summary table appears in Online Supplement~G. This is a diagnostic, not a
full equilibrium model of evasion: it measures how fast a static published
cutoff degrades as a triage device under strategic response, not how well a
behavioral signal survives.
