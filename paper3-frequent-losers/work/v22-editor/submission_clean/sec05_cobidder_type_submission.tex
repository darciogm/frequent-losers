\section{Economic Content and Ordinary-Loser Alternatives}
\label{sec:cobidder_type}

The validation results show that the award-layer ranking
concentrates adjudication-anchored loser-side exposure. This
section gives that exposure target economic content. The
comparison is intentionally demanding: among firms already inside
the frequent-loser stratum, do the firms our screen targets---%
those observed co-bidding with direct CADE defendants---differ
from other frequent losers in dimensions relevant for loser-side
forensic prioritization? Throughout, ``cobidder'' denotes an
adjudication-anchored exposure label, not legal membership; the
firms are frequent losers observed in tender-items touched by a
direct CADE defendant.

Because the samples are large, we report standardized mean
differences (SMD, Cohen's $d$) rather than $p$-values: with tens
of thousands of firms, statistical significance is uninformative,
while standardized differences speak to economic magnitude. The
evidence is strengths-forward and honest. Cobidders are
economically distinct from other frequent losers on procurement
environment, deployment breadth, persistence, and proximity to
the legal anchors (Section~\ref{sec:profile_5_1}). Much of that
raw distinction, however, is procurement-environment composition
and proximity that attenuates---and in one case
vanishes---once we adjust for opportunity
(Section~\ref{sec:opp_adjusted_5_2}). The ranking is monotone in
participation but flat in opportunity-adjusted excess contact: it
is an exposure ranking, not a collusion-intensity ranking
(Section~\ref{sec:monotonicity_5_3}). Ordinary-loser alternatives
are narrowed but not eliminated, because the proxies that would
settle them are not observed at the award layer
(Section~\ref{sec:ordinary_5_4}). The zero-win definition is
robust, and negative controls confirm the ranking is specific to
the real defendant network rather than to generic high-volume
co-bidding (Section~\ref{sec:negctrl_5_5}). The reading is
consistent with evidence that auction collusion can operate
through bidder roles, repeated relationships, and cartel
organization
\citep{baldwin1997bidder,porter1999ohio,pesendorfer2000study,asker2010leniency,clark2021collusion}.

\subsection{Profile of Frequent-Loser Cobidders}
\label{sec:profile_5_1}

Table~\ref{tab:economic_profile} compares the
$\valProfCob$ cobidders to the $\valProfNcob$ other frequent
losers across four panels: persistence, market footprint,
procurement environment, and proximity to legal anchors. Both
groups are always-losers; the comparison holds fixed the
award-layer selection margin and asks whether the
adjudication-anchored target marks a different loser-side profile
among firms already selected by persistent losing.

The differences are economically distinct, not marginal.
Cobidders operate almost entirely in the electronic-auction
environment: their Preg\~ao share is $\valProfPregaoCob$ against
$\valProfPregaoNoncob$ for other frequent losers
(SMD $\valProfPregaoSMD$). They are deployed more broadly,
reaching $\valProfBuyersCob$ distinct buyers versus
$\valProfBuyersNoncob$ (SMD $\valProfBuyersSMD$), and they are
more persistent, active across more years (SMD
$\valProfYearsSMD$). They are also far closer to the legal
anchors used in validation: the standardized difference in the
number of CADE cases touched is $\valProfNcasesSMD$, with
comparably large gaps in distinct defendants contacted and
contact intensity. These are deployment and proximity measures,
not legal conclusions; they show that cobidders occupy a wider,
longer-lived loser-side footprint within the already-selected
frequent-loser pool, anchored to the adjudicated network.

One caveat is built into the design: proximity to defendants is
partly mechanical. Cobidders are identified as frequent losers
who co-bid with direct CADE defendants, so a positive gap in
defendant contacts is in part a definitional consequence rather
than independent evidence. We return to this in
Section~\ref{sec:opp_adjusted_5_2}, where opportunity adjustment
removes the share of contact that mere exposure can explain. The
profile is read as adjudication-anchored exposure, not
membership.

\subsection{Opportunity-Adjusted Profile Differences}
\label{sec:opp_adjusted_5_2}

The raw profile mixes two things: real differences in how
cobidders deploy and bid, and differences that follow mechanically
from participating in more tender-items inside defendant-bearing
environments. Table~\ref{tab:opportunity_adjusted_profile} reruns
the key comparisons after adjusting for opportunity---the expected
contact and exposure a firm would accumulate given its
participation profile.

The adjustment is clarifying, and it cuts against the simplest
``cobidders are just a different kind of firm'' reading. The
item-group concentration difference, raw SMD $\valProfHHIigRawSMD$,
\emph{vanishes} under adjustment (adjusted SMD $\valProfHHIigAdjSMD$):
the apparent product specialization of cobidders is
procurement-environment composition, not an intrinsic portfolio
trait. Buyer breadth attenuates sharply, from raw SMD
$\valProfBuyersSMD$ to roughly $0.19$. Proximity to the legal
anchors roughly \emph{halves}---the CADE-cases SMD falls from
$\valProfNcasesSMD$ to $\valProfNcasesAdjSMD$---consistent with a
large part of raw proximity being the mechanical consequence of
broader participation in defendant-bearing cells. After
adjustment, much of the raw difference is procurement-environment
composition and exposure rather than a distinct firm type. What
survives adjustment is persistence (active years remains above
half a standard deviation) and a residual, attenuated proximity
to the adjudicated network.

\subsection{Monotonicity and the FL14 Implementation}
\label{sec:monotonicity_5_3}

The most informative diagnostic concerns what the ranking actually
orders. Table~\ref{tab:monotonicity_bins} and the two panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:mono_bins} bin firms by participation $T_i$ and
track, across bins, both the prevalence of cobidders and the
opportunity-adjusted excess contact $X_i = O_i - E_i$.

Two facts sit side by side. Cobidder prevalence rises
monotonically with participation: from zero in the lowest bins to
roughly $17\%$ in the highest, a rank correlation with mean
participation of $\valProfMonoPrevRho$. But opportunity-adjusted
excess contact is flat-to-negative across the same bins, with a
rank correlation of $\valProfMonoExcessRho$. The higher-ranked
firms are not contacting defendants \emph{more than their exposure
predicts}; they are simply more exposed. This is the key finding
of the section: the award-layer rank is an \textbf{exposure
ranking, not a collusion-intensity ranking}. That is exactly what
the reach-and-limits reframe expects---the screen reliably orders
firms by adjudication-anchored loser-side exposure, and does not
claim to order them by intensity of collusive conduct.

This also reframes the binary FL14 cutoff. FL14 is an
\emph{administrative} implementation of the exposure ranking, not
a structural break. Table~\ref{tab:binary_vs_continuous} shows
that the continuous score $\log(1+T_i)$ ties or beats the FL14
binary in every full-sample configuration (full-universe ROC-AUC
$0.939$ continuous vs.\ $0.924$ binary; the ordering holds when
the largest case or the largest item group is excluded). The one
configuration where the binary edges ahead is the
opportunity-common-support strict pool studied in the robustness
appendix, where FL14 reaches $0.767$ against $0.750$ for the
continuous form (DeLong $p \approx 0.085$, not significant); that
flip is sample-specific and does not generalize. There is no
bunching at the threshold---the density ratio at $T=14$ is
$\valProfBunchRatio$, essentially flat---so FL14 buys
deployability and a clean reporting cut, not a behavioral
discontinuity. The deployable rule and the underlying signal are
the same exposure ordering, read coarsely versus continuously.

\subsection{Ordinary-Loser Alternatives}
\label{sec:ordinary_5_4}

The exposure interpretation invites a sharper question: could the
same profile arise from ordinary, non-collusive reasons that
firms lose persistently? Table~\ref{tab:ordinary_loser_alternatives}
takes five such alternatives seriously and asks, for each, whether
the award-layer evidence supports it, weakens it, or leaves it
unresolved because the deciding proxy is not observed.

The honest summary is that ordinary-loser alternatives are
\emph{narrowed but not eliminated}. The low-capacity story is
weakened: cobidders are wider and longer-lived, not narrow and
short-lived. The quorum-padding story is weakened: cobidders have
a markedly lower Convite and minimum-quorum share, not the higher
share that story predicts. The explore-then-exit story is
weakened: cobidders have lower first-year concentration, not the
front-loaded participation that learning would imply. But three
alternatives remain unresolved at the award layer, because the
proxies that would settle them---distance-to-winner (a capacity
and specialization proxy), geography and logistics, and later
wins---are \textsc{not\_observed} in this data layer. Benign
specialization and geography therefore remain possible.
What distinguishes cobidders from these ordinary stories is the
one axis the alternatives do not predict: contact with the
adjudicated CADE network. Ordinary-loser alternatives remain
possible; CADE contact is the distinguishing axis, and it is the
axis the screen is built on.

\subsection{Market-Specific Zero-Win Robustness and Negative Controls}
\label{sec:negctrl_5_5}

Two further checks bound the construct; we summarize them here and
report full detail in Appendix~\ref{app:validation_audits_submission}.

First, the global zero-win definition of always-losers is not
dominated by any market-specific alternative. Re-defining
``loser'' within narrower market segments yields a best
alternative ROC-AUC of $\valProfMarketZeroWinBest$, well below the
global $\valProfNegCtrlRealAUC$, and the leave-one-item-group-out
ranking is stable in the range $[0.936, 0.941]$. The simple,
global definition is the right one.

Second, and more importantly, negative controls confirm that the
ranking is specific to the \emph{real} adjudicated defendant
network, not an artifact of generic high-volume co-bidding. When
we replace the true CADE defendants with matched placebo anchors
and re-score, the real-anchor ROC-AUC of $\valProfNegCtrlRealAUC$
dominates the placebo-anchor distribution (mean
$\valProfNegCtrlPlaceboAUC$; empirical $p < 0.001$). The screen's
ranking quality is tied to the actual defendant network, which is
the central strength of the validation: it is not reproduced by
arbitrary high-volume anchors. One caveat keeps the comparison
honest---PR-AUC is not a clean placebo comparison here, because
random anchors touch on the order of $830$ firms against $191$
real positives, inflating the placebo base rate. ROC-AUC, the
ranking-quality metric that is invariant to base rate, is the
decisive comparison, and on it the real network wins
unambiguously.

\subsection{What Section~5 Establishes}
\label{sec:cobidder_interpretation}

Section~5 gives the award-layer exposure target genuine but
bounded economic content. The strengths are real: cobidders are
economically distinct from other frequent losers---more
Preg\~ao-concentrated, broader, more persistent, and closer to
the adjudicated anchors---and the ranking is specific to the real
defendant network, beating matched placebo nulls on ROC-AUC at
$p < 0.001$. The limits are equally clear and stated in their
place: much of the raw distinction is procurement-environment
composition and partly mechanical proximity that attenuates under
opportunity adjustment (HHI vanishes; proximity halves); the rank
is monotone in exposure but flat in opportunity-adjusted excess
contact, so it is an exposure ranking rather than a
collusion-intensity ranking; FL14 is an administrative cut, not a
structural threshold; and ordinary-loser alternatives are
narrowed but not eliminated, with specialization and geography
left unresolved because their proxies are not observed at the
award layer.

The construct is therefore a disciplined exposure ranking with
genuine economic content. It does not establish membership,
agreement, liability, or cover bidding in any tender, and it is
not diagnostic by itself. Read together with the validation of
Section~\ref{sec:validation} and the operational sequencing of
Section~6, it supports triage, not liability: the award layer
orders attention toward adjudication-anchored loser-side exposure,
and richer records evaluate what the screen has prioritized. The
next section turns from content to implementation, asking whether
the award-layer rank can be placed before bid-distribution
forensics in a sequence that saves bid-microdata recovery while
preserving adjudication-anchored recall.
