\section{Evidence-Allocation Framework and Legal Scope}
\label{app:framework_submission}

\subsection{The pre-proof ranking problem}
\label{app:framework_primitives}

The model used in the main text is intentionally spare. Its job
is to justify an ordering rule for scarce forensic attention, not
to model the full internal organization of a cartel. Formation,
side payments, leniency incentives, and damages are treated in the
cartel literature (Asker, 2010; Marshall and Marx, 2012). The
narrower object here is the pre-proof enforcement problem: why a
firm that repeatedly loses, while continuing to appear in
procurement records, can be a useful target for bid-level
follow-up. The device is a reduced-form evidence-allocation rule,
not a structural cartel model.

Consider a procurement environment with repeated tender-items. In
a cartel-targeted tender, one firm is assigned the winning role
and submits the designated bid $b^*$. A losing-role participant
submits a bid $b_\ell>b^*$ and is not meant to win. The losing
role carries a participation cost. Winning by mistake or by
deviation creates an internal sanction $\kappa>0$, interpreted
broadly as the loss of future rents, relational standing, or
capacity-allocation rights. Let $r$ denote the private gain from
deviating into the winner role in a tender where the firm was
assigned to lose.

Firms outside the winning role are of two reduced-form \emph{model}
types. Type $C$ firms are cartel-deployed losing-role
participants. Type $G$ firms are ordinary losing participants:
they may be inefficient, capacity constrained, inexperienced, or
simply unlucky. The type notation is a device for stating the
maintained ranking condition; $C$ and $G$ are not observed
classes and the type labels are not an empirical classification
rule. The empirical screen conditions on the observed event
$W_i=0$, where $W_i$ is the firm's number of wins, and ranks
firms by participation count $T_i$.

\subsection{Losing-role discipline and zero wins}
\label{app:framework_zero_win}

\begin{lemma}[Zero-win losing role]
\label{lem:submission_zero_win}
If the internal sanction from winning in a losing role exceeds the
private deviation gain, $\kappa>r$, a losing-role participant
avoids winning: it submits $b_\ell>b^*$, so
$\Pr(W_i>0\mid C)=0$ over tenders in which the firm is deployed in
the losing role.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A losing-role participant that submits $b_\ell>b^*$ loses and
receives the deployment payoff net of the role cost. Deviating to
a winning bid gains $r$ but pays the sanction $\kappa$; the
deviation is unprofitable when $\kappa>r$. The inequality fixes
the win probability of the losing role, not the numerical bid: any
bid above the designated winner's bid is consistent with the
reduced-form role.
\end{proof}

The inequality $\kappa>r$ supports conditioning on the zero-win
event, but it does \emph{not} imply that all zero-win firms occupy
a losing role. The conditioning set is intentionally broad: many
ordinary firms also never win. The evidentiary content comes from
the second observable, participation intensity, and from the
dynamic margin that governs who remains in the zero-win set.

\subsection{Dynamic participation and exit}
\label{app:framework_exit}

The ranking statistic is informative only if persistent zero-win
participation is more characteristic of a deployed losing role
than of an ordinary losing firm. We obtain that property
\emph{endogenously}, from a participation-and-exit margin, rather
than assuming a type-specific rate gap.

Each period a firm decides whether to keep participating on the
platform. An ordinary zero-win firm $G$ pays a participation cost
$c_G>0$ and earns expected gross surplus $m_G(H_{it})$, where
$H_{it}$ is the firm's loss history at item $i$, time $t$. Under
Bayesian updating about its own competitiveness, repeated losses
weakly lower the firm's continuation surplus,
$m_G(H_{it})$ weakly decreasing in the accumulated loss count. The
firm continues if and only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exit_G}
  V_G(H_{it}) \;=\; m_G(H_{it}) - c_G + \beta\,
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[V_G(H_{i,t+1})\mid H_{it}\right] \;\ge\; 0,
\end{equation}
and \emph{exits} when $V_G(H_{it})<0$. After a long zero-win
history, $m_G$ has fallen enough that ordinary zero-win firms may
exit the platform.

A cartel-deployed losing-role firm $C$ does not value the tender
through the win prospect. Its continuation value rests on a role
value $a_C(H_{it})$ capturing future rents, relational or
subcontracting value, capacity-allocation standing, and any
internal compensation for sustained losing. It continues if and
only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exit_C}
  V_C(H_{it}) \;=\; a_C(H_{it}) - c_C + \beta\,
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[V_C(H_{i,t+1})\mid H_{it}\right] \;\ge\; 0 .
\end{equation}
Because $a_C$ derives from the cartel role rather than from the
firm's own win prospect, it does not erode with the firm's loss
count the way $m_G$ does. After sufficiently many zero-win periods,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exit_wedge}
  a_C(H_{it}) - c_C \;>\; m_G(H_{it}) - c_G ,
\end{equation}
so $C$ keeps participating while $G$ is selectively removed from
the zero-win risk set.

The empirical consequence is a \emph{selection} result. Among
surviving zero-win firms, the participation rate of cartel-deployed
losing roles exceeds that of ordinary losing firms,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lambda_rank}
  \lambda_C \;>\; \lambda_G \qquad
  \text{on the surviving conditioning set } W_i=0 ,
\end{equation}
\emph{derived} from differential exit under
\eqref{eq:exit_G}--\eqref{eq:exit_wedge}, not posited as an
exogenous type fixed effect. The frequent-loser ordering inherits
its content from this dynamic selection plus role value.

\begin{assumption}[Role-value persistence]
\label{assu:submission_persistence}
Among surviving zero-win firms, cartel-deployed losing-role
participants have weakly greater net continuation value from
repeated losing than ordinary zero-win firms after sufficiently
many losses: there is a loss count $\bar L$ such that for
histories with accumulated losses exceeding $\bar L$,
$a_C(H_{it})-c_C \ge m_G(H_{it})-c_G$.
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Monotone ranking statistic}
\label{app:framework_participation}

Conditional on survival and zero wins, let participation counts
follow a Poisson approximation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:poisson}
  T_i \mid \tau_i, q \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_q \tau_i),
  \qquad q\in\{C,G\},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_i$ is the length of time firm $i$ is observed and
$\lambda_q$ is the type-specific surviving participation rate.
Equation~\eqref{eq:lambda_rank} supplies the ordering
$\lambda_C>\lambda_G$ on this surviving set.

\begin{proposition}[Persistence ranking]
\label{prop:submission_monotone}
Suppose (1) losing-role firms avoid winning when $\kappa>r$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:submission_zero_win}); (2) ordinary zero-win firms
exit when continuation value falls below the outside option
(\eqref{eq:exit_G}); (3) role value keeps $C$ in the risk set
longer, so the selection wedge
\eqref{eq:exit_wedge}--\eqref{eq:lambda_rank} holds
(Assumption~\ref{assu:submission_persistence}); and (4) among
survivors effective participation intensity is higher for $C$ than
for $G$, $\lambda_C>\lambda_G$. Then the posterior
$\Pr(C\mid T_i=t,W_i=0,\tau_i)$ is non-decreasing in $t$, and
$T_i$ and $\log(1+T_i)$ are monotone ranking statistics for
forensic priority within the zero-win stratum.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Bayes' rule the posterior odds of type $C$ against type $G$ are
proportional to the Poisson likelihood ratio
\[
  \frac{\Pr(T_i=t\mid C,\tau_i)}
       {\Pr(T_i=t\mid G,\tau_i)}
  =
  \exp[-(\lambda_C-\lambda_G)\tau_i]
  \left(\frac{\lambda_C}{\lambda_G}\right)^t .
\]
Under $\lambda_C>\lambda_G$ from \eqref{eq:lambda_rank}, this ratio
is increasing in $t$: the monotone-likelihood-ratio property of the
Poisson family (Karlin and Rubin, 1956).%
\footnote{Karlin, S. and H.~Rubin (1956). The theory of decision
procedures for distributions with monotone likelihood ratio.
\textit{Annals of Mathematical Statistics} 27(2), 272--299.}
The posterior is therefore non-decreasing in the participation
count, and $\log(1+T_i)$ preserves the ranking.
\end{proof}

Condition~(4) is the key empirical content: it is the surviving
participation gap, and it is what \S\S4--6 validate against
adjudication-anchored labels. The proposition is a ranking result
under maintained behavioral conditions, not an identification
claim. The empirical binary rule used in the main text---flagging
firms above the median plus $1.5$ times the interquartile range of
zero-win participation---is an operational coarsening of this
ranking. The threshold is not a structural parameter; agencies
could instead use the continuous score, a capacity-constrained
top-$k$ list, or a budget-calibrated cutoff.

\begin{corollary}[Legal scope]
\label{prop:submission_scope}
Under Lemma~\ref{lem:submission_zero_win},
Assumption~\ref{assu:submission_persistence}, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:submission_monotone}, the frequent-loser
statistic orders expected investigative value within the zero-win
stratum. It is not sufficient for liability, damages, or cartel
membership.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Triage requires an ordering of expected investigative value;
Proposition~\ref{prop:submission_monotone} supplies that ordering.
Liability and damages require different predicates---proof of
agreement, conduct in particular tenders, and an economic measure
of price injury. The statistic carries participation and win
information only and omits those facts. The same statistic can be
useful for allocating forensic attention and insufficient for
legal adjudication.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Empirical survival audit}
\label{app:framework_survival}

The exit-margin logic of \S\ref{app:framework_exit} makes a
testable prediction about who remains on the platform: if role
value sustains cartel-deployed losing participation while ordinary
zero-win firms are selectively removed, then firms most exposed to
the cartel-adjacency labels should display longer continued
participation. We audit this descriptively on a firm-year panel of
the $16{,}843$ always-losers ($101{,}366$ firm-years, 2009--2019,
right-censored at 2019). Because the audit uses \emph{full-sample}
labels---FL cobidders, FL non-cobidders, and non-FL
always-losers---it is retrospective and descriptive, not
timing-disciplined; it is mechanism-supporting at most and does not
test cartel membership or conduct.

The pattern is in the predicted direction.
Table~\ref{tab:survival_summary_submission} shows that FL
cobidders persist markedly longer on the platform: mean active
years $4.37$ versus $2.84$ for FL non-cobidders and $1.61$ for
non-FL always-losers; median platform duration $6$ versus $2$
versus $1$ years; and an exit share before 2019 of $0.54$ versus
$0.73$ versus $0.85$ (equivalently, right-censored shares of
$0.46$, $0.27$, $0.15$). Survival here means continued BEC
\emph{participation}, not legal-entity survival.

The survival audit is not a test of cartel membership, conduct, or
overcharge. Because it uses full-sample labels it is descriptive
and retrospective, and ordinary zero-win firms may exit for many
reasons unrelated to cartel status. The result is consistent with
the appendix's exit-margin logic---role value sustaining persistent
losing participation---and is mechanism-supporting at most. The
paper's identification continues to rest on the
opportunity-adjusted and timing-disciplined tests of \S\S4--6.

\subsection{Evidentiary scope}
\label{app:framework_scope}

Even if the model is correct, a high value of $\log(1+T_i)$ raises
the posterior probability of loser-side cartel-adjacency exposure
only relative to other zero-win firms. It does not identify an
agreement, assign cartel membership, prove that any specific bid
was cover, or establish a damages base. Those claims require
evidence from the richer record: bid values, communications,
leniency material, winner-rotation patterns, or other
case-specific facts.

This boundary is the legal reason for the staged architecture in
the main text. The award layer can allocate attention because it
ranks firms on a statistic that is cheap, observable, and monotone
under a transparent behavioral assumption. The bid layer and case
record then do the evidentiary work that liability requires,
because liability turns on conduct and agreement, not on persistent
participation alone. The framework supplies only the ranking logic
for triage.
