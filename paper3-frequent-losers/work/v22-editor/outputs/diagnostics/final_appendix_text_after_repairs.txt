Appendix Roadmap
This appendix supplies technical support for the main text and is organized around
the evidentiary burden of an evidence-allocation design. Appendix A documents the
award-record universe, the CADE–BEC matching rules, the direct-defendant versus
adjudication-anchored cobidder distinction, and the label funnel and sample reconciliation. Appendix B gives the reduced-form ranking framework, the monotone-likelihoodratio result, the exit-margin logic, and the platform-survival audit. Appendix C
reports the opportunity-adjusted, timing, leakage, and case-composition audits that
discipline alternative interpretations of the ranking. Appendix D summarizes the
economic-profile and score diagnostics. Appendix E documents the bid-layer benchmark and the cost-recall frontier for sequential award→bid gatekeeping. Appendix F
reports the price-scope evidence and the adaptive-deployment protocol. Full robustness
grids, permutation draws, threshold sweeps, the bid-feature dictionary, and additional
diagnostic figures are collected in the Online Supplement, whose manifest maps each
item to its generating script. Throughout, the appendix interprets the ranking as

forensic priority, not proof of conduct or cartel membership, and treats price evidence
as scope information rather than damages.

Appendix A. Data Construction and Validation Labels
This appendix records the construction choices behind the award-layer screen and
the adjudication-anchored labels, and keeps each source in its proper evidentiary role.
The screen is built from procurement records; the validation labels come from CADE
adjudications and their BEC co-bidding environments. The design therefore tests
whether a cheap award-layer statistic ranks firms near adjudicated defendants, while
leaving legal membership to the case record. The labels are adjudication-anchored
exposure measures, not proof of conduct or cartel membership.
Appendix A.1. Award-Record Universe
The BEC procurement universe covers 2009–2019 and contains 1,654,447 tenderitem records before the final price-regression restriction; the main price sample contains
1,654,401 observations. For the screening exercise the unit is the firm: a firm enters
the outer screening universe if it appears as a participant in at least one BEC tender
during the sample window, giving 41,444 participating firms.
Appendix A.2. Screen Construction

Appendix A.3. Matching CADE Defendants and Constructing Cobidder Labels
The starting CADE portfolio contains 65 firm-defendants across 12 adjudicated
procurement-cartel cases linked to BEC subject matter. We match CADE defendants
to BEC participant records by exact string equality on the full 14-digit CNPJ (códigofornecedor), zero-padded to preserve leading zeros; no fuzzy, name-based, or manual
matching enters the direct-defendant set. Of the 65 firm-defendants, 47 have at least
3

one BEC tender-item record during the sample window and define the direct-defendant
set; the remainder operate outside BEC or lack matchable identifiers.
Direct defendants are not the screen’s target; matching them to BEC tender-items
locates the adjudication-anchored exposure set (52013 defendant tender-items, of
48 distinct crossmatch CNPJ of which 41 are firm_tender_map-active). The main
validation target is the always-loser cobidder, constructed from the current scripts by
a single rule: a unique always-loser firm (win rate = 0 over 2009–2019) that shares
at least one BEC tender-item with at least one BEC-active direct CADE defendant.
Three exclusions fix the object. Direct defendants are themselves excluded from the
cobidder set, so the label measures loser-side exposure around the anchors rather than
the anchors; the sentinel firm code -1 (unidentified bidder) is excluded; and firms
appearing across cases or under duplicate CNPJ are counted once. The rule yields
651 always-loser cobidders over the full 12-case portfolio.
The frequent-loser flag is not used to construct the label. It is the award-layer
score that is evaluated against the label. Of the 651 positives, 341 are frequent losers
and 310 are not; the composition is descriptive, not a label restriction. The label
is adjudication-anchored exposure, not legal membership: it says the firm appears
on the loser side of tenders that include adjudicated defendants, not that it was a
defendant or submitted a cover bid in any particular tender.

Appendix A.4. Conservative and Timing Benchmarks
The conservative validation benchmark restricts CADE cases to adjudications with
judgment date on or before 2020-12-31, sacrificing sample size to avoid using later
case resolution to define the target. Under the same cobidder definition, this leaves
4 cases, 19 crossmatch direct defendants, and 208 always-loser cobidders, of which
107 are frequent losers. The cut uses judgment dates because conduct-onset dates
are unavailable: only judgment dates are observed, for 9 of the 12 cases, and the 3
undated cases are placed last in any timing ordering. Per-case timing and benchmark
membership are reported in the Online Supplement.
The temporal-holdout exercise computes participation scores from 2009–2016 and
evaluates discrimination against labels realized in 2017–2019. The timing-benchmark
labels are retrospective adjudication anchors: the judgments post-date the bidding,
and we claim no real-time legal knowledge at the scoring date. The split follows the
enforcement logic rather than a statistical convention: at the moment an agency uses
the award layer it does not know the completed case file, and the relevant question is
whether earlier participation helps rank firms whose adjudication-anchored exposure is
later revealed. Among the 651 positives, 498 are rankable incumbents (some pre-2017
participation history) and 153 are entrant positives with no pre-window history to rank
(23.5% of the target). The design is conservative in computing the score before the
target period but imperfect because adjudications often post-date conduct that can
span several years; it is therefore a retrospective out-of-time discrimination exercise,

The device is a reduced-form evidence-allocation rule.
In a cartel-targeted tender one firm is assigned the winning role and submits the
designated bid b∗ ; a losing-role participant submits bℓ > b∗ and is not meant to win.
Winning by deviation creates an internal sanction κ > 0 (loss of future rents, relational
standing, or capacity-allocation rights); let r be the private gain from deviating into the
winner role. Firms outside the winning role are of two reduced-form model types: type
C, cartel-deployed losing-role participants, and type G, ordinary losing participants
(inefficient, capacity constrained, or unlucky). The labels are a device for stating the
ranking condition; C and G are not observed classes. The empirical screen conditions
on the observed event Wi = 0 (Wi is the firm’s number of wins) and ranks firms by
participation count Ti .
Lemma 1 (Zero-win losing role). If the internal sanction from winning in a losing
role exceeds the private deviation gain, κ > r, a losing-role participant avoids winning:
8

it submits bℓ > b∗ , so Pr(Wi > 0 | C) = 0 over tenders in which the firm is deployed
in the losing role.
Proof. Submitting bℓ > b∗ yields the deployment payoff net of the role cost. Deviating
to a winning bid gains r but pays κ, which is unprofitable when κ > r. The inequality
fixes the win probability of the losing role, not the numerical bid.

□

The inequality κ > r supports conditioning on the zero-win event but does not
imply that all zero-win firms occupy a losing role: the conditioning set is intentionally
broad, since many ordinary firms also never win. The evidentiary content comes
from the second observable—participation intensity—and from the dynamic margin
governing who remains in the zero-win set.

Appendix B.2. Dynamic participation, exit, and the ranking statistic
The ranking statistic is informative only if persistent zero-win participation is more
characteristic of a deployed losing role than of an ordinary losing firm. We obtain that
property endogenously from a participation-and-exit margin rather than assuming a
type-specific rate gap. An ordinary zero-win firm G pays a participation cost cG > 0
and earns expected gross surplus mG (Hit ), with Hit its loss history at item i, time t;
under Bayesian updating, repeated losses weakly lower mG . It continues iff

VG (Hit ) = mG (Hit ) − cG + β E[VG (Hi,t+1 ) | Hit ] ≥ 0,

Proposition 3 (Persistence ranking). Suppose (1) losing-role firms avoid winning

Proof. By Bayes’ rule the posterior odds of C against G are proportional to the Poisson
likelihood ratio exp[−(λC − λG )τi ](λC /λG )t , which under λC > λG is increasing in

posterior is non-decreasing in the count, and log(1 + Ti ) preserves the ranking.

□

Condition (4)—the surviving participation gap—is the key empirical content,
validated against adjudication-anchored labels in §§4–6. This is a ranking result under
maintained behavioral conditions, not an identification claim. The main-text binary
rule (flagging firms above the median plus 1.5 times the interquartile range of zero-win
participation) is an operational coarsening of this ranking; the threshold is not a
structural parameter, and agencies could instead use the continuous score, a top-k
list, or a budget-calibrated cutoff.

Appendix B.3. Empirical survival audit
The exit margin of §Appendix B.2 predicts who remains on the platform: if
role value sustains cartel-deployed losing participation while ordinary zero-win firms
are selectively removed, the most exposed firms should display longer continued
participation. We audit this descriptively on a firm-year panel of the 16,843 alwayslosers (101,366 firm-years, 2009–2019, right-censored at 2019). Because the audit uses
full-sample labels, it is retrospective and mechanism-supporting at most; it does not
test cartel membership or conduct. “Survival” means continued BEC participation,
not legal-entity survival.
The pattern is in the predicted direction. Table B.1 shows that FL cobidders
persist markedly longer: mean active years 3.51 versus 2.87 for FL non-cobidders and
1.61 for non-FL always-losers; median duration 3 versus 2 versus 1 years; exit share
before 2019 of 0.63 versus 0.73 versus 0.85. A discrete-time hazard model (cloglog,
duration and calendar-year fixed effects, clustered by firm) confirms the ordering, with

12

exit-hazard ratios of 0.231 for FL cobidders and 0.365 for FL non-cobidders relative
to non-FL always-losers—both far below one—so exposure to the labels is associated
with substantially lower exit hazard (full hazard estimates, the Cox specification,
and the sensitivity battery are reported in the Online Supplement). The cobidder
coefficient is sharply estimated (z = −22.2, p < 10−100 ), and the Kaplan–Meier curves
are clearly separated. The direction is stable across robustness variants—alternative
exit definitions, excluding late cohorts, and excluding the largest CADE case (ratio
0.281)—and a timing-disciplined to-date label attenuates it to 0.768 but leaves it
below one.

Appendix C. Validation Audits
The validation design addresses three mechanical channels. Frequent participants
have more chances to appear near any defendant, so raw co-bidding rates are not
enough; firms may operate in the same procurement environments as CADE anchors,
giving them more opportunities to meet those anchors even under ordinary competition;
and item-level labels can mechanically reuse the same participation history that
constructs the score. This appendix records the opportunity and timing audit logic
and the interpretation each audit licenses; the main text reports the headline results.
Throughout, “CADE-defendant contact” denotes an adjudication-anchored exposure
label: a firm shares a tender-item with a directly adjudicated CADE defendant. It
does not assert that the firm is a cartel member, party to any agreement, or liable for
anything. The audits validate forensic priority, not membership, damages, or proof.
Online Supplement C reports the full grids behind every audit summarized here.

Appendix C.5. Direct-Defendant Scope Check
If the statistic were a generic cartel-membership detector, it should classify direct
CADE defendants as well as cobidders. It does not: the FL-binary score (retrospective incumbent triage) ranks direct defendants at ROC ≈ 0.49 under both full and
strict timing (0.491 and 0.492), i.e. at chance, with precision@500 = 0. Continuous
participation volume does carry some information about defendants—ROC 0.66–0.70,
modestly above chance—but with precision@500 ≤ 0.004, so it does not operationally
retrieve them either (full grid in Online Supplement C). The asymmetry is the design:
the loser-side binary flag is uninformative about defendants, who typically sit on the
winner side of the arrangement, while participation volume is not entirely silent. This
is a scope boundary, not an escape hatch.

Appendix C.6. Limitations
Several limitations bound this appendix.

The opportunity cells are award-

layer proxies built from item, year, and procuring-unit identifiers, not a full
description of each firm’s competitive opportunity set, and procurement modality is dropped because it is absent from that layer.

All timing is year-level

(DAY_LEVEL_TIMING_UNAVAILABLE): the CADE files carry only judgment dates (2015–
2025), no conduct dates, so we cannot align the screen to when a cartel operated and
the adjudication-observable design is infeasible rather than estimated. There is an
entrant coverage hole (153/651, 23.5%, of positives carry score zero with no training
history) and the signal is concentrated by case (dropping the largest cuts PR-AUC
23

by 37%), item group, and year, so the pooled operational number overstates breadth.
ROC is a red herring at sub-1% prevalence: precision and recall—not ROC—are the
operational metrics, and they stay low under the pooled diagnostic and collapse to
zero on the full strict-timing universe. Most importantly, the CADE-defendant labels
are adjudication-anchored exposure labels, not membership findings: a tagged firm
shared a tender-item with an adjudicated defendant; it is not thereby a cartel member,
a party to any agreement, or liable for anything. Net of opportunity and timing, the
residual ordering is marginal at best and not robust across designs; what the award
layer reliably delivers is a coarse, retrospective basis for prioritizing scrutiny, and it is
not legal proof, not identification of cartel members, and not an estimate
of damages or causal effect.

Appendix D.3. Robustness: Monotonicity, Threshold, Markets, and Null Controls
Four robustness families bear on the exposure-ranking reading; full detail is in
Online Supplement D, which reports the threshold sweep, the bunching histogram,
the market-specific zero-win variants, and the negative controls. Monotonicity. Across

27

participation bins, cobidder prevalence rises (Spearman ρ = +0.99) while mean excess
contact shows no rising pattern (ρ = −0.25): the score ranks firms by exposure,
not collusion intensity. Threshold. The FL14 cut Ti ≥ 14 equals median + 1.5 ×
IQR (= 13.5, rounded up) of the always-loser participation distribution; it is an
administrative construction, computed ex post and never published. Discrimination
changes smoothly across a placebo-threshold grid with no discontinuity at 14, and
the participation distribution declines smoothly through the cut (local density ratio
1.06, indistinguishable from 1), so there is no strategic bunching. Market-specific
zero-win. Four home-market loser definitions overlap the global set on only 0.33–
0.55 of their losers; the global definition is not dominated by any of them (global
ROC-AUC 0.761 vs. the best home-market alternative 0.584) and is stable to leaveone-item-group-out re-estimation. Negative controls. Against matched null targets
(B = 500 draws, seed 20260603), the real-anchor ROC-AUC (0.761) is statistically
indistinguishable from the matched placebo-anchor distribution (mean 0.755; empirical
p = 0.46), and anchors built from non-CADE high-volume winners generate raw
discrimination at least as high (null mean 0.78; p = 0.91). The negative controls
therefore corroborate the opportunity decomposition rather than rescue the screen:
the raw ranking reflects generic co-participation arithmetic, not a signature specific to
the adjudicated defendant network.

The defendant-contact variables carry the largest standardized gaps but are

Appendix E. Bid-Layer Benchmark and Cost-Recall Details
This appendix documents the construction and diagnostics behind the bid-layer
benchmark used in Section 6.1, which asks whether the cheap, award-layer participation
signal carries ranking information comparable to a transparent bid-moment randomforest benchmark inspired by Imhof–Wallimann-style screens, built from full bid
microdata. The bid-layer benchmark is a forensic-stage, full-observability diagnostic: it
computes seven Imhof–Wallimann within-tender bid-distribution moments, aggregates
them to the firm, and feeds them to a random forest evaluated by cross-validation.
It is not a state-of-the-art horse race and not a first-stage triage tool, because the
moments require all within-tender bids to be observed and recovered. Throughout,
“cobidder” denotes an adjudication-anchored exposure label — an always-loser firm
that shares at least one BEC tender-item with a BEC-active direct CADE defendant
29

— and never a finding of participation in any agreement; the benchmark ranks and
prioritizes the adjudication-anchored cobidder target and never detects cartelists.
Granular per-feature, per-fold, per-case, and calibration diagnostics, the full feature
dictionary, and the supporting tables moved out of this appendix are deposited in
Online Supplement E.

Appendix E.1. LANCES Bid-Layer Data, Units, and Support
The bid layer is built from v3/data/processed/bid_level_with_prices.parquet
(approximately 245 MB), one row per priced bid with columns códigofornecedor
(firm), numerodaoc (OC sequence), códigoitem (item), and bid_price; a bid is
retained only if bid_price IS NOT NULL AND bid_price > 0.

The benchmark

is produced by script 31 (31_imhof_full_pipeline.R, arrow + data.table, seed
20260430) and a DuckDB twin, script 49 (49_imhof_incremental_value.R, seed
20260501); a dedicated validation run (09_bid_benchmark_validation.R, seed
20260603) generates the audit numbers. The bid layer links to the award layer
through the tender-item key (numerodaoc, códigoitem) and, for firm attachment,
through (numerodaoc, códigoitem, códigofornecedor); the within-tender moments
are not recoverable from the award layer, which records no per-bid prices.
The modeling unit is the firm: per-tender moments are shared across all firms
bidding on a tender-item and then firm-mean aggregated. Of the 16,843 always-losers,
64 are dropped because they have no priced bids or fewer than two bids per tender (so
the coefficient of variation, skewness, and kurtosis are undefined). Excluding the direct

CADE defendants by upstream label curation leaves an evaluation pool of 16,731

per-feature missingness tables are in Online Supplement E.)

Appendix E.2. Bid-Feature Families
Seven Imhof–Wallimann moments are constructed within each tender-item with ≥ 2
priced bids, then averaged across each firm’s priced tenders to the firm level. They fall
into four families: dispersion (coefficient of variation imhof_cv_mean and its withinfirm SD imhof_cv_sd, undefined for single-tender firms); level/relative spread (normalized spread and log min–max, imhof_spread_mean, imhof_minmax_mean); shape
(skewness imhof_skew_mean and excess kurtosis imhof_kurt_mean); and rank/order
(the second-low gap log(b2 /b1 ), imhof_second_low_mean, the only moment with a
winning-margin component, of low–moderate leakage risk but still a field-level moment
shared across firms, not a firm-specific outcome). Two auxiliary counts accompany
them, n_bids (gating the ≥ 2 filter) and n_tenders_priced (a firm exposure count).
No co-bidding/network or timing, bid-revision, or late-bid features are implemented:
the parquet read selects only firm, OC, item, and price, and no timestamp column
is touched. The full feature dictionary with all columns and formulas is in Online
Supplement E.

31

Appendix E.3. Model Specification
The learner is a random forest (ranger, 500 trees, probability = TRUE,
num.threads = 12) — not a logit, despite an earlier script header that mislabeled it.
There is no hyperparameter tuning (fixed 500 trees, default mtry and min.node.size,
no grid search) and no standardization (the forest is scale-invariant). Missingness is
handled listwise through the pool restriction. The outcome is binary is_cade (1 for
an always-loser cobidder in the candidate pool, not a direct defendant). Evaluation
is by 5-fold cross-validation, each firm receiving one held-out predicted probability;
AUC and DeLong tests are computed on these out-of-fold firm-level predictions. (The
full spec-by-spec reproduction audit is in Online Supplement E.)

Appendix E.4. Cross-Validation and Holdout Designs
Four validation designs are run, increasing in stringency: Design A (pooledrandom 5-fold) is a diagnostic upper bound; Design E (case-grouped / leave-one-caseout) holds out an entire CADE case so its clustered positives never co-train; Design F
(exclude-label-defining tenders) recomputes the moments after dropping defendant-coappearance tenders to test feature–target contamination; and Design G (environment
holdout) holds out an item-group. Strict bid-timing validation is blocked: the
within-tender moments cannot be cleanly time-limited without re-deriving them on a
per-period bid panel. Because positives cluster by case, random folds (Design A) are
optimistic — same-case positives appear in both train and test — and the bid ROC
drops from 0.717 (A) to 0.626 (E) while its PR-AUC falls from 0.116 to 0.062, against

an award score that is identical across designs by construction. (Full fold-composition
and per-case tables are in Online Supplement E.)

Appendix E.6. Limitations
The bid-layer benchmark is a full-observability diagnostic, not a first stage: the
Imhof moments require all within-tender bids, which is exactly the information cost
the award-layer screen avoids; the headline pooled-random numbers carry random-CV

moments cannot be cleanly restricted to a pre-target window; the benchmark is
deliberately modest (untuned, unstandardized Imhof–Wallimann moments in a random
forest), not a state-of-the-art horse race; and the target is the 651 always-loser cobidders,
an adjudication-anchored exposure measure, so false positives are unlabeled firms and
nothing here constitutes proof of conduct.
34

Appendix E.7. Sequential Gatekeeping and the Cost-Recall Frontier
The sequential architecture in the main text separates two tasks that are often
conflated. The award-layer stage ranks firms using fields available before any bid
microdata are recovered; the bid-layer stage then constructs richer forensic features
only for the firms that survive the first stage. This appendix states the sequence,
measures the recovery footprint each stage opens, and traces the cost-recall frontier
that results, so the empirical comparison reads as an evidence-allocation design rather
than a claim that thin records substitute for full bid files. The output is a forensicpriority queue, not a liability finding. Throughout, “cost” denotes a data-processing
footprint — the firms, tender-items, bid rows, and buyer-item-year cells that must be
opened to compute the bid-layer features — and never a monetary agency cost, which
we do not observe. Discriminating performance is adjudication-anchored: positives
are the 651 always-loser cobidder firms that link to a CADE-adjudicated case, against
the complete-Imhof pool of 16,772 firms. Throughout, the operating points should be
read as retrospective cost-footprint designs conditional on the validated incumbent
ranking, not as fully prospective deployment tests.

Appendix E.7.1. Candidate Pools and Ranking Rules
The evaluation pool (Pool A) is the complete-Imhof firm set: 16,772 firms, of
which 651 are adjudication-anchored positives; under full observability it opens 116,112
distinct tender-items and 3,393,915 within-tender bid rows. Three prediction scores are
defined over this pool: an award score, the zero-win-stratum continuous participation
35

score si = log(1+Ti ), computable from routine administrative fields with no bid values;
a bid score, a random forest on within-tender bid-distribution moments requiring the
full bid record of each opened tender-item; and a combined score on the union of the
two feature sets. We compare seven ranking rules distinguished by which layer they
consume and whether they triage before opening bid microdata: award-only (cheap, no
bid recovery; the main-text screen); bid-only and joint full-observability (both require
opening every tender-item up front; the joint rule is an upper-bound comparison,
not an operational baseline); sequential award → bid and award → combined (rank by
award, keep the top-K1 survivor pool, recover bid microdata only inside it, then rerank
survivors); FL14 → bid (a binary-cutoff first-stage triage followed by bid reranking);
and a random K1 -sized survivor pool, the no-information floor. An opportunityadjusted award score cannot serve as a gate — the exposure adjustment requires
the same tender-level coverage the bid layer recovers — so it is reported only as an
in-sample diagnostic in the main text. For the case-holdout exercise firms are grouped
by the CADE case they link to, so held-out positives share no case with the calibration
set.

Appendix E.7.5. Operating-Point Logic
No single (K1 , k) pair is preferred for every agency; the right point depends on
capacity and on whether the priority is recall or precision — the frontier is a menu,
not a cutoff. At the k = 500 queue depth the frontier spans a capacity-constrained
low end (K1 = 500, smallest footprint 1,356,346 bid rows, recall 0.166, precision
0.216) through K1 = 1,000 (recall 0.190, precision 0.248) and K1 = 2,000 (recall
0.178, precision 0.232) to K1 = 3,000 (recall 0.177, precision 0.230). Recovered
positives are non-monotone in K1 : K1 = 1,000 beats K1 = 2,000 (124 versus 116
true positives), one more sign that no K1 is optimal. The K1 = 2,000 point used
in the main text for the firm-versus-bid-row divergence is one operating point on
this frontier, not a universally preferred choice. The full operating-points table with
use-case labels, and the cost-per-true-positive and false-positive frontier figures, are in
Online Supplement E.

Appendix E.7.6. Algorithm
The full gatekeeping procedure can be stated compactly.

Algorithm E.1: Award-Layer Gatekeeping with Cost Measurement
1. Define the firm universe from award records (participant identity,
winner identity, item code, negotiated price, procuring unit, year,
modality).

41

2. For each firm i, compute the win count Wi and the participation
count Ti over the calibration window; restrict to the zero-win stratum
{i : Wi = 0}.
3. Compute the award score si = log(1 + Ti ).
4. Over a grid of first-stage cuts K1 , form the survivor pool SK1 of
the top-K1 firms (or apply the median-plus-1.5×IQR threshold, or a
capacity budget).
5. Recover bid microdata only for the tender-items touched by SK1 , and
record the cost denominators D1–D8 at that K1 .
6. Compute the bid-layer forensic features inside SK1 .
7. Rerank survivors by the bid score alone or by the award-plus-bid
combined score, over a grid of queue depths k.
8. Deliver the top-k queue from that ranking as a forensic-priority list
for investigative review.
9. Trace the cost-recall frontier over (K1 , k) and select the operating
point that fits the agency’s capacity and recall/precision priority;
treat the output as forensic priority, not as a liability finding.

Joint scoring requires bid microdata for the full pool before triage; sequential
gatekeeping requires bid microdata only after the award-layer survivor pool is formed.
The size of the survivor pool, the choice of threshold or top-K1 , the queue depth k,
the bid-versus- combined rerank, and the recalibration cadence are all institutional
42

choices that the algorithm leaves to the agency.

Appendix E.7.7. Limitations
Cost is a processing footprint, not money — we measure firms, tender-items,
bid rows, and cells opened, not monetary recovery cost, legal-request volume, or
LANCES export units. Firm reduction overstates the saving (an 88% firm reduction
at K1 = 2000 is only a 33% bid-row reduction); analyst-hours are illustrative only;
labels are limited and false positives are unlabeled, so precision is a lower bound on
the queue’s triage value; performance is case-fragile, with the largest case supplying
32.0% of positives and leave-largest-case-out recall falling materially; the operating
point is capacity-dependent with no dominating choice; and the sequential timing test
is blocked, so only the award-only gate has a timing-disciplined evaluation.

The main text (Section 7) treats price evidence as corroboration of economic
non-neutrality, not as causal identification. This appendix records the price sample,
the broad associations, the overlap-cell reweighting that reverses their sign, the highvalue exception, the direct-CADE null, and the boundary diagnostics that keep the
interpretation as scope rather than a price effect. Every number is the same object
reported in the main text; the full regression grids live in Online Supplement G. The
strategic-adaptation simulation is reported separately in Appendix F.6.

Appendix F.4. Within-cell mechanism
The within-cell price compression loads on the count of genuine bidders, not on
the frequent-loser count: the within-cell coefficient is −0.048 (SE 0.004) and falls to
+0.008 (not significant) once log(n_firms) is added, so the mechanism is not identified
by these data.

Appendix F.5. Limitations
The price evidence is descriptive, environment-specific, and not causal. It establishes economic non-neutrality of flagged environments, concentrated in the high-value
tail, but it is not a damages estimate, an overcharge, a markup, or a treatment effect
on price. The coefficients are BEC-specific; another agency should re-validate the
ranking against its own enforcement anchors and price benchmarks rather than reusing
these estimates or the BEC cutoff.

Appendix F.6. Adaptive Triage Designs (Retrospective)
The award-layer score supports an incumbent-firm triage design, so a diagnostic
simulation holds the empirical environment fixed and asks how the participation score
behaves under simple evasive responses. This is a retrospective robustness probe, not
a fully prospective deployment test: the labels remain adjudication-anchored, and the
operating points should be read as conditional on the validated incumbent ranking.
46

Rotation of losing-role firms and occasional small wins leave discrimination essentially
unchanged (0.730 and 0.720 versus baseline 0.722), absorbed by the persistence signal;
CNPJ splitting lowers AUC to 0.653 by breaking participation history; thresholdaware capping is the most damaging (0.676, and 0.607 combined with rotation). The
hierarchy maps to the cost–recall frontier: under an incumbent-triage reading the
rule should be refreshed as new participation data arrive, and continuous ranks or
capacity-constrained top-k queues should be preferred over a static published cutoff,
since a published threshold invites bunching (consistent with the local density ratio
≈ 1.06 at T = 14 in the profile appendix, where the threshold was computed ex post
and never disclosed) and is the most gameable rule. The summary table appears in
Online Supplement G. This is a diagnostic, not a full equilibrium model of evasion:
strategic response exposes the vulnerability of a static binary rule more than it
invalidates the two-stage architecture, and none of these magnitudes should be read
as a platform-wide prospective deployment result.
