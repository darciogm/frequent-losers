Appendix Roadmap

   The appendix follows the logic of the evidence-allocation design and builds from
definitions to validation. Appendix A fixes the objects: the award-record universe, the
CADE–BEC matching rules, the distinction between direct defendants and adjudication-
anchored cobidders, and the label funnel that reconciles every sample count. Appendix B
develops the reduced-form ranking model behind the screen—the monotone-likelihood-
ratio result, the exit-margin logic that gives the participation count its informational
content, and the platform-survival audit that tests it descriptively. Appendix C is the
core of the validation: the opportunity-adjusted, timing, leakage, and case-composition
audits that discipline what the ranking can and cannot be read to mean. Appendix D
characterizes the flagged firms and reports the score diagnostics; Appendix E and
Appendix E.7 turn to the bid-layer benchmark and trace the cost-recall frontier of
sequential award-to-bid gatekeeping. The price-scope evidence and the adaptive-use
diagnostics close the sequence in Appendix F and Appendix F.6; Appendix G re-runs the
validation battery on the federal ComprasNet panel. Full robustness grids, permutation
draws, threshold sweeps, the bid-feature dictionary, and the supporting figures are
deposited in the Online Supplement, whose manifest maps each item to the script that
generates it.
   One reading convention governs the whole appendix. The ranking is a statement
about forensic priority—where scarce investigative attention is most likely to pay off—not
a finding of conduct or cartel membership; the validation labels are adjudication-anchored
exposure measures, not legal findings; and the price evidence describes scope (where
flagged environments sit in the price distribution), not damages or a causal price effect.
We restate this boundary only where a specific result could otherwise be misread.


Appendix A. Data Construction and Validation Labels

   The screen and the labels it is validated against come from different sources kept
deliberately apart. The screen is built from procurement award records alone; the
validation labels come from CADE adjudications and the BEC co-bidding environments
around the adjudicated defendants. The exercise that follows asks whether a cheap
award-layer statistic concentrates firms near those defendants, while leaving the question
of legal membership entirely to the case record.

Appendix A.1. Award-Record Universe
   The BEC procurement universe covers 2009–2019 and contains 1,654,447 tender-
item records before the final price-regression restriction; the main price sample contains
1,654,401 observations. For the screening exercise the unit is the firm: a firm enters the
outer screening universe if it appears as a participant in at least one BEC tender during
the sample window, giving 41,444 participating firms.

Appendix A.2. Screen Construction
   For each firm i we construct two award-layer variables, Ti (number of BEC tender-
items in which firm i participates) and Wi (number of BEC tender-items won by firm
i). The always-loser stratum is the set {i : Wi = 0} and contains 16,843 firms. The
continuous screening score is si = log(1 + Ti ) within that stratum, and the discrete
frequent-loser flag is
                              F Li = 1{Wi = 0, Ti ≥ 14}.

The threshold comes from the always-loser participation distribution—median plus 1.5
times the interquartile range, with statistical cutoff 13.5 and integer rule Ti ≥ 14—and
is fixed from that distribution alone, not estimated from CADE labels. It flags 2,735
firms, or 16.2% of the always-loser stratum. Because the threshold is pre-specified, the
validation exercise asks a genuine question: whether an award-layer rule concentrates
firms later observed near adjudicated cartel defendants.

Appendix A.3. Matching CADE Defendants and Constructing Cobidder Labels
   The starting CADE portfolio contains 65 firm-defendants across 12 adjudicated
procurement-cartel cases linked to BEC subject matter. We match CADE defendants
to BEC participant records by exact string equality on the full 14-digit CNPJ (código-
fornecedor), zero-padded to preserve leading zeros; no fuzzy, name-based, or manual
matching enters the direct-defendant set. Of the 65 firm-defendants, 47 have at least one
BEC tender-item record during the sample window and define the direct-defendant set;
the remainder operate outside BEC or lack matchable identifiers.
   Direct defendants are not the screen’s target; matching them to BEC tender-items
locates the adjudication-anchored exposure set (52013 defendant tender-items, of 48
distinct crossmatch CNPJ of which 41 are firm_tender_map-active). The main validation
target is the always-loser cobidder, constructed from the current scripts by a single rule:
a unique always-loser firm (win rate = 0 over 2009–2019) that shares at least one BEC
tender-item with at least one BEC-active direct CADE defendant. Three exclusions fix
the object. Direct defendants are themselves excluded from the cobidder set, so the label
measures loser-side exposure around the anchors rather than the anchors; the sentinel
firm code -1 (unidentified bidder) is excluded; and firms appearing across cases or under
duplicate CNPJ are counted once. The rule yields 651 always-loser cobidders over the
full 12-case portfolio.
   The frequent-loser flag is not used to construct the label. It is the award-layer score
that is evaluated against the label. Of the 651 positives, 341 are frequent losers and 310
are not; the composition is descriptive, not a label restriction. The label is adjudication-
anchored exposure, not legal membership: it says the firm appears on the loser side of
tenders that include adjudicated defendants, not that it was a defendant or submitted a

Appendix A.4. Conservative and Timing Benchmarks
   The conservative validation benchmark restricts CADE cases to adjudications with
judgment date on or before 2020-12-31, sacrificing sample size to avoid using later case
resolution to define the target. Under the same cobidder definition, this leaves 4 cases, 19
crossmatch direct defendants, and 208 always-loser cobidders, of which 107 are frequent
losers. The cut uses judgment dates because conduct-onset dates are unavailable: only
judgment dates are observed, for 9 of the 12 cases, and the 3 undated cases are placed
last in any timing ordering. Per-case timing and benchmark membership are reported in
the Online Supplement.
   The temporal-holdout exercise computes participation scores from 2009–2016 and
evaluates discrimination against labels realized in 2017–2019. The timing-benchmark
labels are retrospective adjudication anchors: the judgments post-date the bidding, and we
claim no real-time legal knowledge at the scoring date. The split follows the enforcement
logic rather than a statistical convention: at the moment an agency uses the award layer
it does not know the completed case file, and the relevant question is whether earlier
participation helps rank firms whose adjudication-anchored exposure is later revealed.
Among the 651 positives, 498 are rankable incumbents (some pre-2017 participation
history) and 153 are entrant positives with no pre-window history to rank (23.5% of the
target). The design is conservative in computing the score before the target period but
imperfect because adjudications often post-date conduct that can span several years; it is
therefore a retrospective out-of-time discrimination exercise, not causal prediction or a
field deployment.

Appendix B. Evidence-Allocation Framework and Legal Scope

Appendix B.1. The pre-proof ranking problem
     The model is intentionally spare: it justifies an ordering rule for scarce forensic
attention, not the internal organization of a cartel (formation, side payments, leniency,
and damages are treated in Asker, 2010, and Marshall and Marx, 2012). The narrower
object is the pre-proof enforcement problem—why a firm that repeatedly loses while still
appearing in procurement records can be a useful target for bid-level follow-up. The
device is a reduced-form evidence-allocation rule.
     In a cartel-targeted tender one firm is assigned the winning role and submits the
designated bid b∗ ; a losing-role participant submits bℓ > b∗ and is not meant to win.
Winning by deviation creates an internal sanction κ > 0 (loss of future rents, relational
standing, or capacity-allocation rights); let r be the private gain from deviating into
the winner role. Firms outside the winning role are of two reduced-form model types:
type C, cartel-deployed losing-role participants, and type G, ordinary losing participants
(inefficient, capacity constrained, or unlucky). The labels are a device for stating the
ranking condition; C and G are not observed classes. The empirical screen conditions
on the observed event Wi = 0 (Wi is the firm’s number of wins) and ranks firms by
participation count Ti .



                                                                    6
Lemma 1 (Zero-win losing role). If the internal sanction from winning in a losing
role exceeds the private deviation gain, κ > r, a losing-role participant avoids winning: it
submits bℓ > b∗ , so Pr(Wi > 0 | C) = 0 over tenders in which the firm is deployed in the
losing role.

Proof. Submitting bℓ > b∗ yields the deployment payoff net of the role cost. Deviating to
a winning bid gains r but pays κ, which is unprofitable when κ > r. The inequality fixes
the win probability of the losing role, not the numerical bid.                            □

   The inequality κ > r supports conditioning on the zero-win event but does not imply
that all zero-win firms occupy a losing role: the conditioning set is intentionally broad,
since many ordinary firms also never win. The evidentiary content comes from the
second observable—participation intensity—and from the dynamic margin governing who
remains in the zero-win set.

Appendix B.2. Dynamic participation, exit, and the ranking statistic
   The ranking statistic is informative only if persistent zero-win participation is more
characteristic of a deployed losing role than of an ordinary losing firm. We obtain that
property endogenously from a participation-and-exit margin rather than assuming a
type-specific rate gap. An ordinary zero-win firm G pays a participation cost cG > 0 and
earns expected gross surplus mG (Hit ), with Hit its loss history at item i, time t; under
Bayesian updating, repeated losses weakly lower mG . It continues iff


                 VG (Hit ) = mG (Hit ) − cG + β E[VG (Hi,t+1 ) | Hit ] ≥ 0,           (B.1)


and exits when VG (Hit ) < 0. A cartel-deployed losing-role firm C instead values con-
tinuation through a role value aC (Hit ) (future rents, relational or subcontracting value,
internal compensation for sustained losing). It continues iff


                 VC (Hit ) = aC (Hit ) − cC + β E[VC (Hi,t+1 ) | Hit ] ≥ 0.           (B.2)


Because aC derives from the role rather than the firm’s own win prospect, it does not
erode with the loss count the way mG does, so after sufficiently many zero-win periods

Assumption 2 (Role-value persistence). Among surviving zero-win firms, cartel-
deployed losing-role participants have weakly greater net continuation value from repeated

such that for histories with accumulated losses exceeding L̄, aC (Hit ) − cC ≥ mG (Hit ) − cG .

Proposition 3 (Persistence ranking). Suppose (1) losing-role firms avoid winning
when κ > r (Lemma 1); (2) ordinary zero-win firms exit when continuation value
falls below the outside option ( (B.1)); (3) role value keeps C in the risk set longer,
so the selection wedge (B.3)–(B.4) holds (Assumption 2); and (4) among survivors
effective participation intensity is higher for C than for G, λC > λG . Then the posterior
Pr(C | Ti = t, Wi = 0, τi ) is non-decreasing in t, and Ti and log(1 + Ti ) are monotone
ranking statistics for forensic priority within the zero-win stratum.

Proof. By Bayes’ rule the posterior odds of C against G are proportional to the Poisson
likelihood ratio exp[−(λC − λG )τi ](λC /λG )t , which under λC > λG is increasing in t
(monotone likelihood ratio of the Poisson family; Karlin and Rubin, 1956).1 The posterior
is non-decreasing in the count, and log(1 + Ti ) preserves the ranking.                            □

       Condition (4)—the surviving participation gap—is the key empirical content, validated
against adjudication-anchored labels in §§4–6. This is a ranking result under maintained
behavioral conditions, not an identification claim. The main-text binary rule (flagging
firms above the median plus 1.5 times the interquartile range of zero-win participation)

Corollary 4 (Legal scope). Under Lemma 1, Assumption 2, and Proposition 3, the
frequent-loser statistic orders expected investigative value within the zero-win stratum. It
is not sufficient for liability, damages, or cartel membership.

Proof. Triage requires an ordering of expected investigative value, which Proposition 3
supplies. Liability and damages require different predicates—proof of agreement, conduct
in particular tenders, and a measure of price injury—that the statistic, carrying only
participation and win information, omits.                                                 □


Appendix B.3. Empirical survival audit
   The exit margin of §Appendix B.2 predicts who remains on the platform: if role value
sustains cartel-deployed losing participation while ordinary zero-win firms are selectively
removed, the most exposed firms should display longer continued participation. We
examine this descriptively on a firm-year panel of the 16,843 always-losers (101,366
firm-years, 2009–2019, right-censored at 2019). Because the panel uses full-sample labels,
the exercise is retrospective and at most mechanism-supporting. “Survival” here means
continued BEC participation, not legal-entity survival.
   The pattern is in the predicted direction. Table B.1 shows that FL cobidders persist
markedly longer: mean active years 3.51 versus 2.87 for FL non-cobidders and 1.61 for
non-FL always-losers; median duration 3 versus 2 versus 1 years; exit share before 2019
of 0.63 versus 0.73 versus 0.85. A discrete-time hazard model (cloglog, duration and
calendar-year fixed effects, clustered by firm) confirms the ordering, with exit-hazard
ratios of 0.231 for FL cobidders and 0.365 for FL non-cobidders relative to non-FL always-
losers—both far below one—so exposure to the labels is associated with substantially
lower exit hazard (full hazard estimates, the Cox specification, and the sensitivity battery
are reported in the Online Supplement). The cobidder coefficient is sharply estimated
(z = −22.2, p < 10−100 ), and the Kaplan–Meier curves are clearly separated. The
direction is stable across robustness variants—alternative exit definitions, excluding late
cohorts, and excluding the largest CADE case (ratio 0.281)—and a timing-disciplined
to-date label attenuates it to 0.768 but leaves it below one.

Appendix B.4. Evidentiary scope
   The survival result is consistent with the exit-margin logic—role value sustaining
persistent losing participation—but is mechanism-supporting at most; ordinary zero-win
firms may exit for many reasons. More generally, even if the model is correct, a high
value of log(1 + Ti ) raises the posterior probability of loser-side exposure only relative to
other zero-win firms: it does not identify an agreement, assign cartel membership, prove
any specific bid was cover, or establish a damages base. Those claims require the richer
record (bid values, communications, leniency material, winner-rotation patterns). This
boundary is the legal reason for the staged architecture in the main text: the award layer
ranks firms on a statistic that is cheap, observable, and monotone under a transparent
behavioral assumption, while the bid layer and case record do the evidentiary work that
liability requires. The paper’s identification continues to rest on the opportunity-adjusted
and timing-disciplined tests of §§4–6.


Appendix C. Validation Audits

   The validation design addresses three mechanical channels. Frequent participants
have more chances to appear near any defendant, so raw co-bidding rates are not enough;
firms may operate in the same procurement environments as CADE anchors, giving
them more opportunities to meet those anchors even under ordinary competition; and
item-level labels can mechanically reuse the same participation history that constructs
the score. This appendix gives the logic of the opportunity and timing audits and states
the interpretation each one licenses; the main text carries the headline numbers, and
Online Supplement C holds the full grids behind every audit summarized here.
   Throughout, “CADE-defendant contact” is an adjudication-anchored exposure label:
a firm shares a tender-item with a directly adjudicated CADE defendant. The label
records proximity, not liability—it does not make the firm a cartel member or a party to
any agreement—and the audits below test forensic priority alone.

Appendix C.1. Opportunity-Cell Construction and Expected Contact
   Purpose. A firm can appear near a CADE anchor simply because it operates in
environments where defendants are active; the opportunity cells discipline this exposure
before any score is credited.

   Method. We partition the award layer into procurement-opportunity cells g and
benchmark each firm against others facing the same cells, using three nested definitions:

on it would further fragment already-sparse cells (revisited as a sensitivity below). For
firm i, with nig the tender-items held in cell g and pg the cell defendant-contact rate,

Appendix C.5. Direct-Defendant Scope Check
   If the statistic were a generic cartel-membership detector, it should classify direct
CADE defendants as well as cobidders. It does not: the FL-binary score (retrospective
incumbent triage) ranks direct defendants at ROC ≈ 0.49 under both full and strict

timing (0.491 and 0.492), i.e. at chance, with precision@500 = 0. Continuous participation

chance—but with precision@500 ≤ 0.004, so it does not operationally retrieve them
either (full grid in Online Supplement C). The asymmetry is the design: the loser-side
binary flag is uninformative about defendants, who typically sit on the winner side of the
arrangement, while participation volume is not entirely silent. This is a scope boundary,
not an escape hatch.

Appendix C.6. Limitations
   Several limitations bound this appendix. The opportunity cells are award-layer proxies
built from item, year, and procuring-unit identifiers, not a full description of each firm’s
competitive opportunity set, and procurement modality is dropped because it is absent
from that layer. All timing is year-level (DAY_LEVEL_TIMING_UNAVAILABLE): the CADE
files carry only judgment dates (2015–2025), no conduct dates, so we cannot align the
screen to when a cartel operated and the adjudication-observable design is infeasible rather
than estimated. There is an entrant coverage hole (153/651, 23.5%, of positives carry
score zero with no training history) and the signal is concentrated by case (dropping the
largest cuts PR-AUC by 37%), item group, and year, so the pooled operational number
overstates breadth. ROC is a red herring at sub-1% prevalence: precision and recall—not
ROC—are the operational metrics, and they stay low under the pooled diagnostic and
collapse to zero on the full strict-timing universe. Most importantly, the CADE-defendant
labels are adjudication-anchored exposure labels, not membership findings: a tagged firm
shared a tender-item with an adjudicated defendant; it is not thereby a cartel member,
a party to any agreement, or liable for anything. Net of opportunity and timing, the
residual ordering is marginal at best and not robust across designs; what the award layer
reliably delivers is a coarse, retrospective basis for prioritizing scrutiny, and it is not legal
proof, not identification of cartel members, and not an estimate of damages
or causal effect.

Appendix D.3. Robustness: Monotonicity, Threshold, Markets, and Null Controls
   Four robustness families bear on the exposure-ranking reading; full detail is in Online
Supplement D, which reports the threshold sweep, the bunching histogram, the market-
specific zero-win variants, and the negative controls. Monotonicity. Across participation
bins, cobidder prevalence rises (Spearman ρ = +0.99) while mean excess contact shows
no rising pattern (ρ = −0.25): the score ranks firms by exposure, not collusion intensity.
Threshold. The FL14 cut Ti ≥ 14 equals median + 1.5 × IQR (= 13.5, rounded up) of the
always-loser participation distribution; it is an administrative construction, computed ex
post and never published. Discrimination changes smoothly across a placebo-threshold
grid with no discontinuity at 14, and the participation distribution declines smoothly
through the cut (local density ratio 1.06, indistinguishable from 1), so there is no strategic
bunching. Market-specific zero-win. Four home-market loser definitions overlap the
global set on only 0.33–0.55 of their losers; the global definition is not dominated by
any of them (global ROC-AUC 0.761 vs. the best home-market alternative 0.584) and is
stable to leave-one-item-group-out re-estimation. Negative controls. Against matched
null targets (B = 500 draws, seed 20260603), the real-anchor ROC-AUC (0.761) is
statistically indistinguishable from the matched placebo-anchor distribution (mean 0.755;
empirical p = 0.46), and anchors built from non-CADE high-volume winners generate
raw discrimination at least as high (null mean 0.78; p = 0.91). The negative controls
therefore corroborate the opportunity decomposition rather than rescue the screen: the
raw ranking reflects generic co-participation arithmetic, not a signature specific to the
adjudicated defendant network.

construction artifact, and the non-mechanical breadth and persistence gaps largely collapse

Appendix E. Bid-Layer Benchmark and Cost-Recall Details

   Section 6.1 asks whether the cheap, award-layer participation signal carries ranking
information comparable to a transparent bid-moment benchmark built from full bid
microdata, and this appendix supplies the construction and diagnostics behind that
comparison. The benchmark is a forensic-stage, full-observability diagnostic: it computes
seven Imhof–Wallimann within-tender bid-distribution moments, aggregates them to
the firm, and feeds them to a random forest evaluated by cross-validation. It is neither
a state-of-the-art horse race nor a first-stage triage tool, because the moments require
every within-tender bid to be observed and recovered. “Cobidder” is an adjudication-
anchored exposure label throughout — an always-loser firm that shares at least one BEC
tender-item with a BEC-active direct CADE defendant — so the benchmark ranks that
exposure target for forensic priority and makes no claim to identify cartelists. Granular
per-feature, per-fold, per-case, and calibration diagnostics, the full feature dictionary, and
the supporting tables moved out of this appendix are deposited in Online Supplement E.

Appendix E.2. Bid-Feature Families
   Seven Imhof–Wallimann moments are constructed within each tender-item with ≥ 2
priced bids, then averaged across each firm’s priced tenders to the firm level. They fall
into four families: dispersion (coefficient of variation imhof_cv_mean and its within-firm
SD imhof_cv_sd, undefined for single-tender firms); level/relative spread (normalized
spread and log min–max, imhof_spread_mean, imhof_minmax_mean); shape (skewness
imhof_skew_mean and excess kurtosis imhof_kurt_mean); and rank/order (the second-
low gap log(b2 /b1 ), imhof_second_low_mean, the only moment with a winning-margin
component, of low–moderate leakage risk but still a field-level moment shared across firms,
not a firm-specific outcome). Two auxiliary counts accompany them, n_bids (gating
the ≥ 2 filter) and n_tenders_priced (a firm exposure count). No co-bidding/network
or timing, bid-revision, or late-bid features are implemented: the parquet read selects
only firm, OC, item, and price, and no timestamp column is touched. The full feature
dictionary with all columns and formulas is in Online Supplement E.

Appendix E.3. Model Specification
   The learner is a random forest (ranger, 500 trees, probability = TRUE,
num.threads = 12) — not a logit, despite an earlier script header that mislabeled
it. There is no hyperparameter tuning (fixed 500 trees, default mtry and min.node.size,
no grid search) and no standardization (the forest is scale-invariant). Missingness is


                                            23
handled listwise through the pool restriction. The outcome is binary is_cade (1 for
an always-loser cobidder in the candidate pool, not a direct defendant). Evaluation is
by 5-fold cross-validation, each firm receiving one held-out predicted probability; AUC
and DeLong tests are computed on these out-of-fold firm-level predictions. (The full
spec-by-spec reproduction audit is in Online Supplement E.)

Appendix E.4. Cross-Validation and Holdout Designs
   Four validation designs are run, increasing in stringency: Design A (pooled-random
5-fold) is a diagnostic upper bound; Design E (case-grouped / leave-one-case-out) holds
out an entire CADE case so its clustered positives never co-train; Design F (exclude-
label-defining tenders) recomputes the moments after dropping defendant-co-appearance
tenders to test feature–target contamination; and Design G (environment holdout)
holds out an item-group. Strict bid-timing validation is blocked: the within-tender
moments cannot be cleanly time-limited without re-deriving them on a per-period bid
panel. Because positives cluster by case, random folds (Design A) are optimistic —
same-case positives appear in both train and test — and the bid ROC drops from 0.717
(A) to 0.626 (E) while its PR-AUC falls from 0.116 to 0.062, against an award score that
is identical across designs by construction. (Full fold-composition and per-case tables are
in Online Supplement E.)

Appendix E.6. Limitations
   The bid-layer benchmark is a full-observability diagnostic, not a first stage: the Imhof
moments require all within-tender bids, which is exactly the information cost the award-
layer screen avoids; the headline pooled-random numbers carry random-CV optimism;
strict bid-timing validation is blocked, because within-tender dispersion moments cannot be
cleanly restricted to a pre-target window; the benchmark is deliberately modest (untuned,
unstandardized Imhof–Wallimann moments in a random forest), not a state-of-the-art
horse race; and the target is the 651 always-loser cobidders, an adjudication-anchored
exposure measure, so false positives are unlabeled firms and nothing here constitutes
proof of conduct.


                                                        25
Appendix E.7. Sequential Gatekeeping and the Cost-Recall Frontier
   The sequential architecture in the main text separates two tasks that are often
conflated. The award-layer stage ranks firms using fields available before any bid microdata
are recovered; the bid-layer stage then constructs richer forensic features only for the firms
that survive the first stage. We state the sequence, measure the recovery footprint each
stage opens, and trace the resulting cost-recall frontier, so the comparison reads as an
evidence-allocation design rather than a claim that thin records substitute for full bid files.
The output is a forensic-priority queue, not a liability finding. Throughout, “cost” denotes
a data-processing footprint — the firms, tender-items, bid rows, and buyer-item-year cells
that must be opened to compute the bid-layer features — and never a monetary agency
cost, which we do not observe. Discriminating performance is adjudication-anchored:
positives are the 651 always-loser cobidder firms that link to a CADE-adjudicated case,
against the complete-Imhof pool of 16,772 firms. Throughout, the operating points should
be read as retrospective cost-footprint designs conditional on the validated incumbent
ranking, not as fully prospective deployment tests.

Appendix E.7.1. Candidate Pools and Ranking Rules
   The evaluation pool (Pool A) is the complete-Imhof firm set: 16,772 firms, of which
651 are adjudication-anchored positives; under full observability it opens 116,112 distinct
tender-items and 3,393,915 within-tender bid rows. (The bid-benchmark evaluation in
Table 5 uses the 16,731-firm subset of this pool with all seven Imhof moments non-missing;
all 651 positives are in both pools, and the award and bid scores are compared on the
same restricted pool.) Three prediction scores are defined over this pool: an award
score, the zero-win-stratum continuous participation score si = log(1 + Ti ), computable
from routine administrative fields with no bid values; a bid score, a random forest
on within-tender bid-distribution moments requiring the full bid record of each opened
tender-item; and a combined score on the union of the two feature sets. We compare
seven ranking rules distinguished by which layer they consume and whether they triage
before opening bid microdata: award-only (cheap, no bid recovery; the main-text screen);
bid-only and joint full-observability (both require opening every tender-item up front;
the joint rule is a hypothetical full-observability ceiling, never an operating policy —
an upper-bound comparison, not an operational baseline); sequential award → bid and


                                              26
award → combined (rank by award, keep the top-K1 survivor pool, recover bid microdata
only inside it, then rerank survivors); FL14 → bid (a binary-cutoff first-stage triage
followed by bid reranking); and a random K1 -sized survivor pool, the no-information floor.
An opportunity-adjusted award score cannot serve as a gate — the exposure adjustment
requires the same tender-level coverage the bid layer recovers — so it is reported only as
an in-sample diagnostic in the main text. For the case-holdout exercise firms are grouped
by the CADE case they link to, so held-out positives share no case with the calibration
set.

Appendix E.7.4. Baselines, Timing, and Case-Holdout
   The sequential rule sits well above a same-size random survivor floor across the grid,
confirming that it is the award ranking, not the mere act of opening K1 firms, that carries
the signal. The award-ranked survivor pool captures most of the 651 positives before any
bid rerank, so the cheap first stage alone already gathers the positives into the survivor
pool, and the bid rerank then reorders that pool into a forensic-priority queue rather than
recovering positives the award gate missed. This is the precise sense in which the bid
layer reorders rather than discovers: the marginal contribution of the costly bid features
is ordering, an evidence-allocation gain, not a detection gain.

Appendix E.7.5. Operating-Point Logic
   No single (K1 , k) pair is preferred for every agency; the right point depends on capacity
and on whether the priority is recall or precision — the frontier is a menu, not a cutoff.
The operating-point grid itself, with recall, precision, and recovered-positive counts at
each K1 , is the main text’s Table 6 and is not re-narrated here; the construction is the
gatekeeping procedure of Appendix E.7.6, applied across the K1 grid at queue depth k.
The K1 = 2,000 point used in the main text for the firm-versus-bid-row divergence is one
operating point on this frontier, not a universally preferred choice. The full operating-
points table with use-case labels, and the cost-per-true-positive and false-positive frontier
figures, are in Online Supplement E.

Appendix E.7.6. Algorithm
   The full gatekeeping procedure can be stated compactly.

     Algorithm E.1: Award-Layer Gatekeeping with Cost Measurement

                                             30
        1. Define the firm universe from award records (participant identity, winner
           identity, item code, negotiated price, procuring unit, year, modality).
        2. For each firm i, compute the win count Wi and the participation count Ti
           over the calibration window; restrict to the zero-win stratum {i : Wi =
           0}.
        3. Compute the award score si = log(1 + Ti ).
        4. Over a grid of first-stage cuts K1 , form the survivor pool SK1 of the
           top-K1 firms (or apply the median-plus-1.5×IQR threshold, or a capacity
           budget).
        5. Recover bid microdata only for the tender-items touched by SK1 , and
           record the cost denominators D1–D8 at that K1 .
        6. Compute the bid-layer forensic features inside SK1 .
        7. Rerank survivors by the bid score alone or by the award-plus-bid com-
           bined score, over a grid of queue depths k.
        8. Deliver the top-k queue from that ranking as a forensic-priority list for
           investigative review.
        9. Trace the cost-recall frontier over (K1 , k) and select the operating point
           that fits the agency’s capacity and recall/precision priority; treat the
           output as forensic priority, not as a liability finding.

   Joint scoring requires bid microdata for the full pool before triage; sequential gate-
keeping requires bid microdata only after the award-layer survivor pool is formed. The
size of the survivor pool, the choice of threshold or top-K1 , the queue depth k, the
bid-versus- combined rerank, and the recalibration cadence are all institutional choices
that the algorithm leaves to the agency.

Appendix E.7.7. Limitations
   Cost is a processing footprint, not money — we measure firms, tender-items, bid rows,
and cells opened, not monetary recovery cost, legal-request volume, or LANCES export
units. Firm reduction overstates the saving (an 88% firm reduction at K1 = 2000 is
only a 33% bid-row reduction); analyst-hours are illustrative only; labels are limited and
false positives are unlabeled, so precision is a lower bound on the queue’s triage value;

                                             31
performance is case-fragile, with the largest case supplying 32.0% of positives and leave-
largest-case-out recall falling materially; the operating point is capacity-dependent with
no dominating choice; and the sequential timing test is blocked, so only the award-only
gate has a timing-disciplined evaluation.


Appendix F. Price Scope and Adaptive Use

   The main text (Section 8) reads the price evidence as corroboration of economic
non-neutrality, not as causal identification. Here we lay out the supporting detail: the
price sample, the broad associations, the overlap-cell reweighting that reverses their
sign, the high-value exception, the direct-CADE null, and the diagnostics that keep the
interpretation on the scope side of the line. Every number matches the object reported
in the main text; the full regression grids live in Online Supplement G. The companion
exercise on how a published administrative cutoff degrades under strategic response—an
institutional-design probe, not a defense of any behavioral signal—is reported separately
in Appendix F.6.

Appendix F.1. Price sample and outcome
   The outcome is the log negotiated unit price at the item–award level. The estimation
sample is the broad item-level panel with N = 1,654,401 awards, with item, year, and
(where indicated) procuring-unit (PBU) fixed effects. The overlap, quartile, and trim
variants reweight or restrict this sample but do not change the outcome or the unit of
observation.

causal effects; the full broad grid by modality is in Online Supplement G.

Appendix F.3. High-value segment, direct-CADE null, and the scope boundary
   The negative ATT reading survives both modalities (Convite −0.099, Pregão −0.098)
and every tender-value quartile except the highest; the high-value tail (Q4) is the only
systematically positive cell (+0.041, p = 0.045), where coordination costs are spread over
larger contracts. Trimming the most heavily weighted overlap cells does not eliminate the
negative aggregate. The full quartile and trim grid is in Online Supplement G. Restricting
the overlap to items involving firms named as direct CADE defendants yields a null
price estimate (−0.061, p = 0.45; non-direct overlap −0.097): the price regressions do
not recover the legal object of the adjudicated cases. Three facts keep the evidence
on the scope side of the line. The broad positive imprint is selection into structurally
higher-priced cells (among non-treated items, mean log price rises from 1.35 to 6.93 across
cell FL-share quintiles, a ∆ = 5.58 log-point gap, with a partialled-out coefficient +3.55

component. The direct-CADE null means there is no price base tied to the adjudicated
conduct. And the design holds the procurement environment fixed rather than against
a counterfactual price, so no overcharge can be computed. These are scope facts about
where flagged environments sit in the price distribution, not damages or overcharge.

Appendix F.4. Within-cell mechanism
   The within-cell price compression loads on the count of genuine bidders, not on the

Appendix F.5. Limitations
   The price evidence is descriptive, environment-specific, and not causal. It establishes
economic non-neutrality of flagged environments, concentrated in the high-value tail, but
it is not a damages estimate, an overcharge, a markup, or a treatment effect on price.
The coefficients are BEC-specific; another agency should re-validate the ranking against
its own enforcement anchors and price benchmarks rather than reusing these estimates
or the BEC cutoff.

Appendix F.6. Instability of a Published Cutoff Under Strategic Response (Retrospective)
   How quickly does a static, published threshold decay as a triage device once partici-
pants can react to it? Because Section 7 finds no conduct-specific residual, the simulations
here defend nothing behavioral; they are an institutional-design exercise. Holding the
empirical environment fixed, we ask how the participation score behaves under simple
evasive responses. The exercise is retrospective, not a fully prospective deployment
test: the labels remain adjudication-anchored, and the operating points should be read
as conditional on the validated incumbent ranking. Rotation of losing-role firms and
occasional small wins leave discrimination essentially unchanged (0.730 and 0.720 versus
baseline 0.722), absorbed by the persistence signal; CNPJ splitting lowers AUC to 0.653
by breaking participation history; threshold-aware capping is the most damaging (0.676,
and 0.607 combined with rotation). The hierarchy maps to the cost–recall frontier: under
an incumbent-triage reading the rule should be refreshed as new participation data arrive,
and continuous ranks or capacity-constrained top-k queues should be preferred over a
static published cutoff, since a published threshold invites bunching (consistent with the
local density ratio ≈ 1.06 at T = 14 in the profile appendix, where the threshold was
computed ex post and never disclosed) and is the most gameable rule. The summary
table appears in Online Supplement G. This is a diagnostic, not a full equilibrium model


                                            34
of evasion: it measures how fast a static published cutoff loses its triage value once firms
respond to it, and makes no claim about the durability of any underlying behavioral
pattern.

Appendix G.7. Negative Controls
    Purpose. Establish whether any surviving federal ordering is cartel-specific or generic
co-participation geometry, mirroring the negative-control logic of Section 6.
    Method. The same award-layer ordering is re-evaluated against (i) placebo anchors—
random federal firms matched on participation volume to the CADE defendants—and (ii)
non-CADE high-volume-winner anchors. If the ordering reproduces under these placebos,
it is not cartel-specific.
    Result. Against placebo anchors the score retains AUC 0.728, and against non-CADE
high-volume-winner anchors 0.746, versus 0.744 against the CADE anchors—placing the
surviving ordering in the realm of generic opportunity/volume geometry.

Reading the graduation.. The federal battery does not return a degraded-but-positive
deployable ranking, and the appendix does not claim one. What ports is the audit: the
raw award-layer concentration looks informative until opportunity exposure is held fixed,
after which the within-stratum residual is read directly off Table G.4, and the prospective

is therefore the decomposition that tells it how far a cheap award-layer statistic can
be trusted before costly bid-layer work begins—and on the federal system that lesson
binds harder, because the bid-layer step cannot be constructed from public data at all
