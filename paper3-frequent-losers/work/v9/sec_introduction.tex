\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


Bid-rigging cartels in public procurement rely on a specific mechanism to
avoid detection: \emph{cover bidding}---the deployment of firms that
submit deliberately losing bids to simulate competition and satisfy
minimum-bidder requirements \citep{marshall2012economics}. We call these
firms \emph{frequent losers} (FL): firms that never win a single tender
yet participate in an abnormally large number of procurement processes.
Using 4.5 million tender-items from S\~ao Paulo's electronic procurement
platform, we show that FL presence is associated with 3.6--6.4\% higher
prices across five independent approaches, and that the FL screen
achieves an AUC of 0.94 when validated
against Brazil's competition authority (CADE) cartel convictions---
substantially outperforming bid-level detection methods that require
granular price data. For procurement agencies in developing countries,
where bid-level data are often unavailable, the FL screen offers the
first firm-level, ex ante screening tool operable with participation
records alone.

Public procurement accounts for 12--15\% of GDP in OECD countries and
more in developing economies \citep{oecd2019government}, and bid
rigging inflates costs by an estimated 10--50\% \citep{connor2007price}.
Existing detection relies on \emph{reactive} methods (leniency,
whistleblowers) or \emph{proactive} screens requiring granular bid-level
data \citep{harrington2008detecting, imhof2019detecting}. The FL screen
complements these with two advantages: (i)~it requires only
participation and outcome data, not bid values; and (ii)~it produces
firm-level flags implicating all tenders in which a flagged firm
participates, enabling targeted investigation.

This paper advances two hypotheses of increasing strength:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (Screening marker):] FL presence is a useful
    \emph{screening marker} for potential collusion---it correlates
    with price anomalies and overlap with known cartel participants,
    regardless of whether every FL firm is itself a cover bidder.
  \item[H2 (Causal mechanism):] FL firms are \emph{themselves} cover
    bidders deployed by cartels to simulate competition---FL presence
    marks cartel operations that sustain supra-competitive prices.
\end{description}

\noindent Our primary contribution is to establish FL presence as a
robust screening marker for bid rigging (H1). Independent empirical
tests---instrumental variables, co-bidding network analysis, and
bid-level diagnostics---then converge on a cover-bidding mechanism (H2).
We do not claim to identify all cartels, estimate structural overcharges,
or measure aggregate welfare losses. The FL screen complements
tender-level forensic tools \citep{imhof2019detecting} with a
\emph{firm-level} criterion operable with participation records and
outcome flags alone---particularly relevant where bid-level data are
unavailable.

\subsection{Contributions}

Using 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids from S\~ao Paulo's
Bolsa Eletr\^onica de Compras (BEC) platform (2009--2019), we make three
contributions.

\emph{First}, we show that FL presence is a reliable empirical marker
for procurement anomalies (H1). OLS specifications with item, year, and
purchasing-unit fixed effects yield a baseline price effect of 6.4\%.
Temporal cross-fitting---defining FL on odd years and estimating on
even years, and vice versa---eliminates any within-sample mechanical
link between classification and outcomes, yielding an out-of-sample
estimate of 3.6\%. The headline range is 3.6--6.4\%, with matching
estimators (5.5--7.7\%) confirming robustness to observable selection. External validation against CADE
(Brazil's competition authority) convictions shows that 7.1\% of FL
firms co-participate with convicted cartelists---3.5 times the rate
expected by chance after controlling for participation intensity
($p < 0.001$, permutation test). The FL--price association persists after excluding CADE-involved
markets, indicating detection beyond known cartels. As a detection
tool, the FL screen achieves AUC~$= 0.94$
(Section~\ref{sec:results_detection}), outperforming bid-level
screens (AUC~$= 0.79$; DeLong $p < 0.001$).

\emph{Second}, we provide convergent evidence for a cover-bidding
mechanism (H2) from four independent tests. Co-bidding network analysis
shows that the FL--price effect concentrates in competitive markets (low
winner HHI), where cover bidding is most profitable---directly
confirming the structural model's market-selection prediction.
\cites{bajari2003deciding} tests reject both exchangeability
($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$) and conditional independence (pairwise
product = 4.28, $t = 81.0$) of FL bid residuals. Structural estimation
selects coordinated cover bidding (Regime~2;
$\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$). A supplementary
leave-one-out IV ($F = 396$, markup = 21.4\%) provides an upper bound
consistent with measurement-error attenuation in the OLS.

\emph{Third}, we develop a structural model of cover-bidder deployment
that generates five testable predictions, identifies the cover-bid
distribution, and enables policy counterfactuals---including the
welfare-maximizing screening threshold as a function of enforcement
capacity.
