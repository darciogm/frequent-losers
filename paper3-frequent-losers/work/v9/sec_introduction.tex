\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Bid-rigging cartels in public procurement face a practical constraint:
they must simulate competition to avoid detection. A common solution is
\emph{cover bidding}---the deployment of firms whose role is not to win
auctions but to submit losing bids that legitimize the procurement
process, satisfy minimum-bidder requirements, and create the appearance
of competitive participation
\citep{marshall2012economics, asker2010leniency}. In procurement data,
these firms appear as bidders that repeatedly participate but never
win---what we call \emph{frequent losers} (FL). This paper shows that
FL presence is a powerful marker of cartel activity, associated with
systematically higher procurement prices and strongly predictive of
known cartel involvement.

Using 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids from S\~ao Paulo's
Bolsa Eletr\^onica de Compras (BEC) platform over 2009--2019, we
identify 2,735 FL firms and document that tenders in which they
participate exhibit 3.6--8.0\% higher prices. Conditional on item
fixed effects, the FL--non-FL price gap narrows from 2.43 to 0.050
log points (overlap coefficient 0.978;
Table~\ref{tab:conditional_descstats} in
Appendix~\ref{app:competition}), confirming that the regressions
exploit within-item variation where FL and non-FL tenders are
comparable. This finding is robust across five independent
estimation approaches---including temporal cross-fitting that breaks
any mechanical link between the FL classification and the price
outcome---and survives validation against cartels prosecuted by
Brazil's competition authority
(Section~\ref{sec:results_detection}).

Public procurement accounts for 12--15\% of GDP in OECD countries and
a larger share in developing economies \citep{oecd2019government}, where
bid rigging inflates costs by an estimated 10--50\%
\citep{connor2007price}. Existing detection relies on \emph{reactive}
methods (leniency, whistleblowers) or \emph{proactive} screens that
require granular bid-level data \citep{harrington2008detecting,
imhof2019detecting}. In many developing countries, such data are
unavailable or unreliable. The FL screen addresses this gap: it requires
only participation and outcome records, produces firm-level flags that
implicate all tenders in which a flagged firm participates, and yields
an operationally implementable detection threshold.

This paper advances two hypotheses of increasing strength:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (Screening marker):] FL presence is a useful
    \emph{screening marker} for potential collusion---it correlates
    with price anomalies and overlap with known cartel participants,
    regardless of whether every FL firm is itself a cover bidder.
  \item[H2 (Causal mechanism):] FL firms are \emph{themselves} cover
    bidders deployed by cartels to simulate competition---FL presence
    marks cartel operations that sustain supra-competitive prices.
\end{description}

\noindent Our primary contribution is to establish FL presence as a
robust screening marker for bid rigging (H1). Independent empirical
tests---co-bidding network analysis, bid-level diagnostics, and a
structural model of cover-bidder deployment---then converge on a
cover-bidding mechanism (H2). We do not claim to identify all cartels,
estimate structural overcharges, or measure aggregate welfare losses.
The FL screen complements tender-level forensic tools
\citep{imhof2019detecting} with a \emph{firm-level} criterion operable
with participation records alone---particularly relevant where bid-level
data are unavailable.

\subsection{Contributions}

We make three contributions, each leveraging the scale and
institutional detail of the BEC platform.

\emph{First}, we document a systematic pattern of cartel organization:
the deployment of repeat-losing firms to simulate competition at scale.
OLS specifications with item, year, and purchasing-unit fixed effects
yield a baseline price effect of 6.4\%; temporal cross-fitting yields
an out-of-sample estimate of 3.6\%. Five non-IV estimators span
3.6--8.0\% (cross-fit, IPW, OLS, structural, CEM), with a
leave-one-out IV diagnostic indicating that the OLS is conservative
due to measurement error in the binary FL indicator. External validation against CADE (Brazil's
competition authority) convictions shows that 7.1\% of FL firms
co-participate with convicted cartelists---3.5 times the rate expected
by chance ($p < 0.001$, permutation test). The FL--price association
persists after excluding CADE-involved markets, indicating detection
beyond known cartels. As a detection tool, the FL screen achieves an
out-of-sample AUC of 0.94 (Section~\ref{sec:results_detection}),
outperforming bid-level screens (AUC~$= 0.79$; DeLong $p < 0.001$).

\emph{Second}, we provide suggestive evidence for a cover-bidding
mechanism (H2) from multiple independent diagnostics. Co-bidding network
analysis shows that the FL--price effect concentrates in competitive
markets (low winner HHI), where cover bidding is most profitable---consistent
with the structural model's market-selection prediction.
\cites{bajari2003deciding} diagnostics indicate that FL bid residuals
violate exchangeability ($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$) and conditional
independence (pairwise product = 4.28, $t = 81.0$), though the
magnitude comparison reverses under tender fixed effects
(Section~\ref{sec:results_bajari}). Structural estimation favors
coordinated cover bidding (Regime~2;
$\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 0.72$). None of these tests is
individually conclusive; their value lies in the consistency of the
pattern across distinct identification strategies.

\emph{Third}, we develop a structural model of cover-bidder deployment
that generates five testable predictions, identifies the cover-bid
distribution, and enables policy counterfactuals---including the
welfare-maximizing screening threshold as a function of enforcement
capacity.
