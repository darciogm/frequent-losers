\begin{abstract}
Bid-rigging cartels simulate competition by deploying firms that
submit deliberately losing bids to legitimize procurement processes.
Using 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids from Brazilian
public procurement (2009--2019), we identify 2,735 such firms---which
we call \emph{frequent losers} (FL)---and show that their presence is
associated with 3.6--6.4\% higher prices across five independent
estimation approaches; a measurement-error-corrected IV provides an
upper bound of 21.4\%. Validated against competition-authority
convictions, the FL screen achieves an out-of-sample AUC of
0.94, outperforming bid-level screens that require granular price
data (AUC~$= 0.79$). A structural model of cover-bidder deployment
confirms coordinated cover bidding (cover-bid dispersion 28\% below
genuine-bid dispersion), with the price effect concentrated in
competitive markets. Policy counterfactuals show that removing the
minimum-bidder requirement would save R\$74M (OLS) to R\$211M (IV
upper bound), and the welfare-maximizing screening threshold nearly
coincides with the statistical rule used for FL classification.
\end{abstract}
