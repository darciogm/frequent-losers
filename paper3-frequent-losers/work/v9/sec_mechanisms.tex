\section{Mechanisms and Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

We examine three mechanisms to distinguish the cover bidding hypothesis
from alternative explanations, then address two additional concerns.
Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms} (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}) summarizes
all tests.

\subsection{M1: Strategic Selection vs.\ Competitive Displacement}

Competitive displacement predicts that FL firms crowd out genuine
competitors; cover bidding predicts they are added on top. We regress
genuine (non-FL) competitor count on FL presence: the OLS coefficient
is $+0.143$ ($p < 0.01$) and the IV estimate $+0.404$ ($p < 0.01$).
FL-present tenders have \emph{more} genuine competitors, not fewer.
This rules out crowding-out: cartels select into competitive tenders
and add cover bidders.

\subsection{M2: Reference Price Calibration}

BEC reference prices are set administratively---typically as the median
of three supplier quotations or the average of recent comparable
prices---and are publicly observable, making them natural coordination
anchors. We regress $\log(p_{\text{negot}} / p_{\text{ref}})$ on FL
presence and find a coefficient of $-0.041$ ($p < 0.01$): winning bids
in FL-present tenders are 4\% closer to the reference price, consistent
with calibrated cover bidding \citep{marshall2012economics}. This also
informs the bad-control concern in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}:
conditioning on $\log r$ may remove part of the treatment effect if
prior cartel bids have already inflated $r$.

\subsection{M3: Reverse Causality}

Could FL firms select into high-priced tenders rather than cause higher
prices? We regress FL entry on lagged log prices within market
(item $\times$ PBU) using 427,474 market-year observations. The
coefficient is $0.0021$ (SE~$=0.0008$, $p = 0.012$)---statistically
detectable but economically negligible (implied elasticity $\approx
0.004$). A 10\% price increase raises FL entry probability by 0.02
percentage points against a 4.8\% baseline---two orders of magnitude
below the 6--9\% main estimate, ruling out price-chasing selection.

\subsection{Alternative Explanations: Learning and Auction Format}

FL firms are approximately one year younger than non-FL firms at first
BEC participation---consistent with recently formed shell firms, though
incomplete market-specific learning by younger entrants is also
possible. Disentangling these channels would require firm-level entry
sequencing data beyond our scope; both interpretations are consistent
with the main findings.

The FL coefficient is larger for preg\~ao (9.3\%) than for convite
(3.8\%). Preg\~ao's real-time format lets cover bidders observe
competing bids and stay above the designated winner---directly
facilitating Regime~1 coordination. Convite's sealed-bid format makes
calibration harder and reduces the marginal need for cover bids when
three genuine bidders already satisfy the minimum-bidder rule.


\subsection{Rationality of FL Participation}
\label{sec:rationality}

We compute expected profit per bid: $E[\pi] = \text{win\_rate} \times \overline{V} \times 0.10 - c$, where $\overline{V}$ is mean procurement-order value, 0.10 a conservative gross margin, and $c$ a bidding cost (Table~\ref{tab:rationality}). FL firms have zero win rate, so $E[\pi] = -c < 0$ for any $c > 0$. At $c = \text{R\$200}$, FL firms incur average career losses of R\$5,950 over 29.8 participations; regular competitors earn R\$31,770. No rational profit-maximizer would persist through 30 tenders at guaranteed loss---the pattern requires side payments from a cartel \citep{asker2010leniency, marshall2012economics}.
