\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% FINAL-MOVE-2: results opening

This section compares prices in tenders with and without FL
participation, within narrow item $\times$ year $\times$ buyer cells.
The conditional price gap is 3.6--7.7\% across specifications. This
is an association, not a causal effect: within-market selection is not
resolved by any of the estimators we deploy. After absorbing item
fixed effects, the raw FL--non-FL gap compresses from 2.43 to 0.050
log points (overlap 0.978), so the regressions compare similar tenders
within tight product categories. We first assess the FL classification
itself, then present the price regressions, the CADE consistency
check's detection performance, and the supporting diagnostics.

% ── 7.0 Classification Diagnostics ──────────────────────────────

\subsection{Classification Diagnostics}
\label{sec:class_diagnostics}

Before turning to the price regressions, we assess whether the binary
FL classification captures something real or is an artifact of the
threshold. The zero-win condition is strict: relaxing it to include
firms with win rates below 1\% or 2\% attenuates the price coefficient
substantially (Table~\ref{tab:fl_lowwinrate},
Appendix~\ref{app:online_robustness}), so the bright line at zero is
doing real work. A continuous measure---the log of the most active
always-loser's participation count---yields a coefficient of 0.022
(SE = 0.005, $p < 0.01$) per log-point.%
\footnote{The continuous measure correlates moderately with the number
of always-losers in the tender ($r = 0.33$), so part of the
coefficient may capture always-loser count rather than individual-firm
intensity. Using the mean (not max) participation count reduces the
correlation to 0.15 and yields qualitatively similar results.}
At the FL threshold ($\log 14 \approx 2.64$), the continuous prediction
is $2.64 \times 0.022 \approx 5.8\%$---close to the binary OLS of
6.4\%, indicating that the binary coefficient reflects the underlying
continuous relationship evaluated at the threshold rather than an
artifact of the cutoff. A permutation placebo that randomly reassigns
the FL indicator across tenders (preserving the 4.8\% treatment rate)
produces coefficients centered at zero (mean 0.001, SD 0.003 over 20
replications), with no permutation reaching 0.064. The FL--price
association reflects the specific allocation of treatment across
tenders, not chance.

% ── 7.1 Reduced-Form Evidence (H1 core) ─────────────────────────

\subsection{OLS and Matching Results}
\label{sec:results_ols}

Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports the baseline OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main} for log negotiated prices
(Prediction~\ref{pred:price_v6}). Across all four
specifications, FL presence is associated with significantly higher
prices: 6.8\% in the general specification (column~1), 6.4\% with PBU
fixed effects (column~2), 9.3\% for preg\~ao (column~3), and 3.8\% for
convite (column~4). Adding PBU fixed effects barely changes the
coefficient, indicating that FL presence does not proxy for high-cost
purchasing units. The unrestricted sample (including items that never
attract FL firms) yields a nearly identical coefficient, so the
item-type restriction does not inflate the baseline.

\input{./output/tables/tab_prices}

FL-present tenders also have 0.19 log points more non-FL firms
(Prediction~\ref{pred:selection_v6}; PBU FE specification). This
contradicts crowding-out and is consistent with \emph{strategic
selection}---though it could also reflect FL firms gravitating toward
inherently competitive tenders for non-collusive reasons
(Table~\ref{tab:nfirms_excl} in Appendix~\ref{app:competition}).

\paragraph{Cross-fit estimate.}
FL status is defined using the same 2009--2019 data on which the price
regression is estimated, potentially creating a mechanical link between
classification and outcomes. To address this, we define FL using only odd years and estimate on
even years, and vice versa (FL incidence is similar across odd and
even years---5.1\% vs.\ 4.5\%---so the temporal split is
approximately exchangeable). The cross-fit
average is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{CF}} = 0.036$ (3.6\%), attenuated by
44\% relative to full-sample OLS. The attenuation reflects a
combination of fewer FL firms per subsample
(1,885--2,153 vs.\ 2,735), classification noise near the threshold,
and---most importantly---the elimination of any within-sample
mechanical correlation between the FL rule and prices.

\paragraph{Cross-fit decomposition.}
Because this last factor removes a potential source of upward bias, the
cross-fit deserves weight. But how much? To decompose the attenuation,
we apply the odd-year FL list to the \emph{full} sample (all years):
the coefficient is 0.043 (SE = 0.019), roughly midway between the
full-sample OLS (0.064) and the cross-fit (0.036). About two-thirds of
the drop from 0.064 to 0.036 reflects classification noise (the
odd-year list has fewer firms and is noisier near the threshold); the
remaining third comes from restricting estimation to the half-sample.
The decomposition coefficient (0.043, SE = 0.019) is not
statistically distinguishable from the baseline OLS
($p \approx 0.45$ for the difference),\footnote{The difference
$0.064 - 0.043 = 0.021$ has approximate SE
$\sqrt{0.019^2 + 0.020^2} \approx 0.028$, yielding $t \approx 0.75$.}
so the attenuation is largely noise. We do not elevate the
decomposition to a preferred estimate: it clarifies the source of the
OLS--cross-fit gap but does not resolve the within-market selection
that affects both. The robust range remains 3.6--6.4\%, with the
cross-fit removing the mechanical link and the OLS providing the
full-sample baseline; matching estimators (below) offer a further
non-parametric check.

\paragraph{Matching estimators.}
Coarsened exact matching (CEM: 0.077, $N = 969{,}751$) and inverse
probability weighting (IPW: 0.055, $N = 830{,}194$) bracket the OLS
and cross-fit estimates: the price gap holds up under non-parametric
reweighting.

\paragraph{Measurement-error diagnostic (IV).}
The leave-one-out IV estimate (0.194, $p = 0.01$, $F = 396$) exceeds
OLS by a factor of three, as one would expect under measurement-error
attenuation in the binary FL indicator
(Section~\ref{sec:reduced_form}). However, if cartels operate across
PBUs within the same product market, the instrument may capture cartel
intensity rather than misclassification alone, biasing the IV upward.
We therefore treat the IV as a diagnostic indicating that the OLS is
conservative, not as a primary estimator
(Table~\ref{tab:iv_balance}, Table~\ref{tab:iv_main} in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Summary.}
Across four approaches---cross-fit (3.6\%), IPW (5.5\%), OLS (6.4\%),
CEM (7.7\%)---the FL--price association falls in a 3.6--7.7\% range.
All four rest on selection on observables plus fixed effects; they
differ in weighting and functional form, and the cross-fit additionally
eliminates any mechanical link between the FL rule and the price
outcome. That the estimates cluster rather than scatter is reassuring:
the association does not hinge on a particular specification. The IV
(19.4\%) points to measurement-error attenuation in the binary FL
indicator, but we keep it off the headline range because the
exclusion restriction is suspect. \citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity
analysis ($RV = 17.5\%$) shows that an omitted confounder would need
to explain a large share of residual variation in both FL presence and
prices to drive the coefficient to zero---a high bar given the
fixed-effects structure.

% ── 7.2 Detection Performance (H1 validation) ───────────────────

\subsection{Detection Performance}
\label{sec:results_detection}

Figure~\ref{fig:roc} (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}) presents the
FL ROC curve, evaluated against CADE cartel co-participation as ground
truth. The FL screen achieves an AUC of 0.94
(Table~\ref{tab:roc_detection} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).
The data-driven optimal threshold (Youden's $J = 0.84$) corresponds
to 1.45$\times$IQR---nearly identical to the 1.5$\times$ baseline
rule---achieving TPR~$= 1.00$ with FPR~$= 0.16$. Despite the small number of CADE positives (98), the bootstrapped 95\%
percentile CI is $[0.91, 0.94]$ (1,000 replications); the point
estimate of 0.94 falls at the CI upper bound, reflecting slight upward
skew in the bootstrap distribution. The AUC may
overstate detection performance because the ROC varies the
participation-count threshold, and higher counts mechanically
increase co-participation opportunities with any firm, including
CADE cartelists. The permutation test
(Section~\ref{sec:cade}), which controls for this by stratifying on
participation quartile, provides an independent check on the
FL--cartel association (3.5$\times$, $p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{Complementarity with Imhof-style screens.}
Against the same CADE ground truth, the FL screen achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$ versus 0.79 for a \citet{imhof2019detecting}-style
bid-level proxy (CV median split; DeLong $p < 0.001$;
Table~\ref{tab:imhof_comparison},
Figure~\ref{fig:roc_comparison} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).
The comparison is indicative: our Imhof proxy is coarser than the
full implementation (which combines CV, kurtosis, spread, and a
trained classifier), the two screens operate at different levels
(firm vs.\ tender), and the ground truth mechanically favors
participation-based screens. What FL adds is reach: it works where
bid-level records are unavailable and produces firm-level flags that
can triage investigations before detailed bid analysis begins.
Precision is low (3.6\%) at a 0.58\% base rate---FL is a
high-recall triage tool, not a standalone classifier.

\paragraph{Horse-race regression.}
To test whether FL adds incremental information beyond bid-level
screens, we include both FL presence and an Imhof-style CV flag
(within-item-group median split) as regressors in
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}. The FL coefficient rises from 0.064 to 0.084
($p < 0.01$) when the Imhof flag is added---a suppression effect,
since Imhof-flagged tenders have lower FL incidence on average,
creating a negative omitted-variable bias in the FL-only regression.
The Imhof flag itself enters at 0.021 ($p < 0.01$), and the
correlation between the two indicators is only 0.060. The suppression
pattern is predicted by Regime~2: coordinated cover bidding produces
low within-tender dispersion (evading CV-based screens) while
maintaining FL participation (detected by the participation-based
screen). The two screens are complementary, not redundant
(Table~\ref{tab:horse_race}, Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\medskip

\subsection{Network-Split Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:results_network}

Table~\ref{tab:network_split} splits FL firms into
concentrated-market and competitive-market subgroups based on
co-bidding network metrics (Section~\ref{sec:empirical_network}).\footnote{Markets
above the winner-HHI sample median are classified as concentrated;
firms sharing at least two tenders are classified as repeat
co-participants.}
Competitive-market FL firms---operating in markets with diversified
winner patterns (low HHI)---drive the entire price effect: 0.126
($p < 0.01$, PBU FE). Concentrated-market FL firms show no significant
effect ($-0.018$, $p > 0.3$).

The pattern is consistent with Proposition~\ref{prop:market_selection}---cover bidding is most profitable in
competitive markets---but it is not the only reading. Cartels in
concentrated markets may already sustain high prices through market
power, making cover bidders redundant insurance rather than active
price inflators. Or the FL label may be noisier in concentrated
markets, where some flagged firms are genuinely unsuccessful entrants.
Or thin cells in concentrated item markets may simply absorb the
effect into the fixed effects. With cross-sectional data we cannot
tell these stories apart, and we regard the concentrated-market null as
an open puzzle. What is clear is that the FL marker bites hardest in
competitive markets---which, from a detection standpoint, is where it
is needed most.

\subsection{Bajari--Ye Tests}
\label{sec:results_bajari}

The price effect concentrates in competitive markets; the next
question is whether FL bids look like they come from a different
data-generating process. The \citet{bajari2003deciding} framework
provides two tests
(Prediction~\ref{pred:exchange_v6};
Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_corrected} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Exchangeability.}
The KS test rejects the null that FL and non-FL bid residuals share
the same distribution ($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$): FL bids are drawn
from a distinct process.

\paragraph{Conditional independence.}
The mean pairwise product of FL residuals is 4.28 ($t = 81.0$,
$p < 0.001$), with the bootstrap FL--non-FL difference
excluding zero, indicating systematically different within-tender
correlation that fits coordinated bidding. Enriching the
first stage with firm age and CNAE sector dummies alongside firm
size leaves R-squared virtually unchanged (0.770) and does not
alter the KS or pairwise-product results, ruling out the concern
that the exchangeability violation is driven by omitted firm-level
cost shifters (Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_corrected}).

\paragraph{Tender FE.}
Under tender FE (Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_tender_fe} in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}), both products drop substantially
(FL: $5.16 \to 0.38$; non-FL: $2.21 \to 0.86$), with the FL product
falling below non-FL---a reversal predicted by Regime~2. Cover bids
cluster near $b^* + \epsilon$ with low variance; removing the tender
mean eliminates the shared focal-point component. In short, the tender-FE specification absorbs the very
variation that cover bidding generates, so the reversal is
mechanically expected but limits the test's power to discriminate
between cover bidding and common cost shocks.

\subsection{Structural Estimation Results}
\label{sec:results_structural}

BIC selects Regime~2 (coordinated cover bidding) over Regime~1
($\Delta$BIC~$= -91{,}473$;
Table~\ref{tab:structural_params} in
Appendix~\ref{app:structural_id}). Under Regime~2,
$\hat{\sigma}_c / \hat{\sigma}_g = 1.19/1.64 = 0.72$: cover bids are
\emph{less} dispersed than genuine bids---a pattern coherent with
coordinated calibration near a focal point above $b^*$. The ratio is below one but
not near zero, indicating imperfect coordination---cover bidders receive
general instructions rather than precise bid levels. The $n$-conditional markup (6.4\%) is virtually identical to baseline
OLS; this is a calibration, not an independently identified parameter.
The structural model pins down the cover-bid mechanism---regime
identification and distributional parameters---but does not
independently identify the price effect. The practical payoff of
knowing the regime is concrete: under Regime~2, cover bids cluster
tightly above the winning bid, so within-tender dispersion screens
(e.g., \citealt{imhof2019detecting}) will underperform precisely in
the markets where cover bidding is most coordinated. This reinforces
the case for a participation-based screen like FL that does not depend
on bid dispersion. Its empirical bite comes from
out-of-sample predictions on moments not used in estimation. The
sharpest is the corner-vs-interior test: mean FL count is 10.7 when
$n_{\text{genuine}} = 0$ (the minimum-bidder constraint forces $m
\geq 3$) and drops to $\approx 2$ when $n \geq 2$---exactly as
Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m} predicts. The within-tender
dispersion test and $m$--$\theta$ comparative static are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:structural_id}.
