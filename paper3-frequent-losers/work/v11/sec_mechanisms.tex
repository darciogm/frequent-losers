\section{Supporting Diagnostics and Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

The exercises in this section do not identify cover bidding. They
assess whether the FL marker behaves in ways coherent with that
mechanism and examine competing explanations. Each test is
individually consistent with cover bidding but also with
non-collusive alternatives (Table~\ref{tab:mechanisms},
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{M1: Do FL firms crowd out genuine competitors?}
If FL firms displace real bidders, we should see fewer genuine
participants in FL-present tenders. Instead, we find $+0.143$ more
non-FL firms ($p < 0.01$). This fits strategic selection, though FL
firms may also gravitate toward inherently competitive tenders for
non-collusive reasons.

\paragraph{M2: Are winning bids anchored to the reference price?}
BEC reference prices are publicly posted before bidding opens.
Regressing $\log(p_{\text{negot}} / p_{\text{ref}})$ on FL presence
yields $-0.041$ ($p < 0.01$): winners in FL-present tenders bid 4\%
closer to the reference price, as expected under calibrated cover
bidding \citep{marshall2012economics}. Within-item variation in
reference-price accuracy could also contribute.

\paragraph{M3: Do FL firms chase high prices?}
Regressing FL entry on lagged prices within market yields $0.0021$
(SE = 0.0008): a 10\% price increase raises FL entry by 0.02~pp
against a 4.8\% baseline. Price-chasing is too weak to account for
the main results, though it does not rule out other selection
channels.

\paragraph{Alternative explanations.}
FL firms are roughly one year younger than non-FL firms, fitting
either the shell-company hypothesis or ordinary learning by younger
entrants. The preg\~ao--convite asymmetry (9.3\% vs.\ 3.8\%) reflects
two forces: preg\~ao's real-time format facilitates coordinated
cover-bid placement, while convite's minimum-bidder constraint forces
some cover bidding regardless of market conditions, diluting the FL
signal. The convite interaction analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:additional_id}) supports this reading: the voluntary
deployment premium (7.6\%) exceeds the constraint-driven component.

\paragraph{Rationality of FL participation.}
\label{sec:rationality}
With zero wins, $E[\pi] = -c < 0$ for any bidding cost $c > 0$. Near
the threshold (14 tenders) persistent bad luck is conceivable; at the
90th percentile (50+ tenders, career losses above R\$10{,}000) it is
not. Win rate zero in BEC does not mean zero everywhere---FL firms may
win federal or municipal tenders we do not observe---but 50+ zero-win
participations require explanation even for firms profitable outside
BEC \citep{asker2010leniency, marshall2012economics}
(Table~\ref{tab:rationality}).

\paragraph{Strategic adaptation.}
If a cartel reads this paper, the obvious responses---rotating FL
firms, allowing occasional wins, staying below the IQR threshold---would blunt the screen. The threshold sensitivity already hints at
this: the coefficient weakens as the threshold rises. The zero-win
condition is doing heavy lifting; relaxing it to low-win-rate firms
attenuates the coefficient substantially
(Table~\ref{tab:fl_lowwinrate},
Appendix~\ref{app:online_robustness}). A competition authority
deploying FL screening would need to update the threshold periodically
and combine it with bid-level tools---the same cat-and-mouse dynamic
that applies to all proactive screens
\citep{harrington2008detecting}.
