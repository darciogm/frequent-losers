Referee Report — RAND Journal of Economics

Paper: Frequent Losers as Cover Bidders in Public Procurement
Authors: Genicolo-Martins & Furquim de Azevedo
Date: 2026-03-15

Reject with invitation to resubmit

1. Summary

This paper proposes "frequent losers" (FL)—firms that never win yet participate in an abnormally large number of public procurement tenders—as a screening marker for cover bidding. Using 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids from São Paulo's BEC platform (2009–2019), the authors identify 2,735 FL firms via a median + 1.5 × IQR threshold on the participation distribution of always-losers. The main findings are: (1) FL-present tenders exhibit 4–9% higher negotiated prices in OLS with item, year, and purchasing-unit fixed effects; (2) a leave-one-out IV yields a 21% LATE (F = 396); (3) 7.1% of FL firms co-participate with CADE-convicted cartelists, 3.5× the participation-adjusted rate; (4) Bajari–Ye tests reject exchangeability and conditional independence for FL bid residuals, though with important caveats under tender FE; (5) a network-based classification shows the price effect concentrates in competitive markets. The paper advances two hypotheses: H1 (FL as screening marker) and H2 (FL as causal cover bidding mechanism).

2. Overall Assessment

The paper does not currently meet RAND's identification standard for its central causal claim. The leave-one-out IV—the only strategy that moves beyond conditional correlation—fails balance on all four observable characteristics (Table B.8), produces an implausibly large estimate under a more saturated FE structure (3.64 in Panel C), and rests on an exclusion restriction that the authors themselves acknowledge is "more concerning" than originally presented. The Bajari–Ye tests produce internally inconsistent results: the fake-groups placebo (Panel C of Table 11) yields a pairwise product of 5.80 that exceeds the FL product of 5.16, and the tender-FE specification reverses the magnitude ordering (FL: 0.38 < non-FL: 0.86). The DiD is null (ATT = 0.014, SE = 0.039). RAND publishes papers where the primary identification strategy is credible; here, the primary strategy (IV) is supplementary by the authors' own admission, and the supplementary evidence (Bajari–Ye, DiD) is either contradictory or uninformative. The H1 contribution—FL as a practical screening tool—is genuine and empirically supported, but it is a policy contribution rather than the identification or structural contribution RAND typically requires.

3. Major Comments

[M1] The IV fails RAND's identification standard

Problem: The LOO instrument (Equation 3) counts FL firms active at other PBUs in the same product group and year. Table B.8 (balance tests) shows that the instrument significantly predicts all four observable characteristics after absorbing item, year, and PBU fixed effects. The authors report standardized differences below 0.03σ and frame this as "economically negligible." With N = 1.65 million, 0.03σ corresponds to t-statistics well above conventional thresholds. The authors now acknowledge that "the pattern of statistically significant imbalance across all four observables is more concerning than any single coefficient." This is correct, and it is fatal for a causal claim at RAND.

Location: Section 6.2, Section 7.2, Table B.8.

What would resolve it: Conley-Hansen-Rossi (2012) bounds allowing for exclusion restriction violations, or a different source of exogenous variation (institutional change, geographic discontinuity, policy shock).

[M2] The Bajari–Ye tests are internally inconsistent

Problem: (a) The fake-groups placebo product is 5.80—larger than the FL product of 5.16. (b) Under tender FE, FL (0.38) < non-FL (0.86)—a reversal. (c) The first stage includes number of bids, which is an equilibrium outcome endogenous to FL presence (Bajari & Ye 2003, p.978 require cost shifters, not market outcomes).

Location: Table 11 (Panels A–C), Table B.17, Section 7.4.

What would resolve it: Remove n_bids from Bajari–Ye first stage. Run fake-groups placebo within FL-present and FL-absent tenders separately. Bootstrap test the magnitude reversal under tender FE.

[M3] The theoretical framework is a descriptive taxonomy, not a model

Problem: Section 3 presents two regimes defined by distributional assumptions (uniform vs. normal) rather than equilibrium conditions. The FOC is stated without derivation. Prediction 3 (Regime identification) has no formal statistical test—only a visual density comparison (Figure 7).

Location: Section 3, Section 7.5.

What would resolve it: Derive the regimes as equilibrium outcomes from a game-theoretic model, or relabel Section 3 as a descriptive taxonomy.

[M4] The network split contradicts the cover-bidding hypothesis

Problem: Concentrated-market FL firms (HHI > median, repeat partners ≥ 2) show -0.018 (p > 0.3), while competitive-market FL firms show 0.126 (p < 0.01). Under cover-bidding theory, firms in concentrated markets with repeated co-bidders have the network characteristics most consistent with organized collusion. The null result for these 1,356 firms directly undermines H2.

Location: Table 9, Section 7.3.

What would resolve it: Interact FL with continuous market-level HHI. Acknowledge the original classification failed. Recalculate welfare using only competitive-market FL firms.

[M5] The null DiD undermines the causal narrative

Problem: C&S ATT = 0.014, SE = 0.039, insignificant. MDE = 0.076 exceeds OLS (0.064). The cleanest quasi-experimental design produces a null result.

Location: Section 9.4, Table C.1.

What would resolve it: Move DiD to a "Causal Evidence: Limitations" section. Discuss candidly that the null is consistent with both low power and no causal effect.

[M6] The welfare analysis is accounting, not structural

Problem: R$734M to R$2.4B (3.3× range) computed as (exp(β)-1) × total FL-present procurement. No equilibrium effects, no entry/exit, no structural counterfactual.

Location: Section 9.5, Table D.1.

What would resolve it: Build a structural welfare model or compress to "illustrative back-of-the-envelope."

[M7] FL novelty relative to Conley-Decarolis (2016, AEJ:Micro)

Problem: Conley & Decarolis detect bidder groups from co-bidding patterns without bid values. The FL screen's claimed advantage—a "natural partition" of bidders—is what their algorithm also produces, without requiring an IQR threshold.

Location: Section 2, Section 1.

What would resolve it: Direct feature-by-feature comparison. Implement Conley-Decarolis on BEC data and compare flagged firms.

[M8] The bid dispersion test is ambiguous

Problem: FL-present tenders show higher log bid SD (0.47–0.55), interpreted as Regime 1. But FL-present tenders also have more genuine bidders (+0.19 log), which independently increases bid dispersion. Is the SD computed on FL firms' own bids or all bids?

Location: Section 7.5, Table D.2.

What would resolve it: Decompose dispersion into within-FL, within-non-FL, and between-group components.

4. Minor Comments

  1. Missing citations: Kawai-Nakabayashi-Ortner (working paper), Decarolis (2014 AER), Decarolis-Giuffrida (2017), Kawai-Nakabayashi (2014).
  2. Sample restriction: Restricting to items with ≥1 FL tender is non-standard. The unrestricted check (0.063 vs 0.064) should be the default.
  3. IQR threshold: Monotonically increasing coefficients with stricter thresholds suggests no "optimal" threshold.
  4. Cross-fitting attenuation: 0.036 vs 0.064 (44% smaller). One fold insignificant. Overfitting of FL definition not ruled out.
  5. CADE overstatement: Abstract leads with 3.5× without noting unconditional OR = 1.21.
  6. Table formatting: Inconsistent across appendix tables (adjustbox, threeparttable usage).
  7. Convite coefficient: 3.8% < 9.3% pregao is counterintuitive given minimum-bidder incentive. Explanation is plausible but informal.
  8. Temporal FL: Coefficient 0.070 > baseline 0.064 unexplained. Shorter windows should attenuate.
  9. Notation: αg indexes "item group" or "item type"? Inconsistent usage.
  10. BEC representativeness: No discussion of external validity beyond São Paulo.

5. Identification Deep-Dive

5.1 OLS Interpretation

The OLS baseline (Table 5) is the paper's strongest result. The 0.064 coefficient with item + year + PBU FE represents a conditional correlation within the same item type, year, and purchasing unit. The FE-residualized gap of 0.050 log points shows the conditional comparison is meaningful. The Cinelli–Hazlett sensitivity (RV = 17.5%) provides a high bar for confounders. For H1 (screening marker), this is sufficient. It cannot be interpreted causally because FL presence is endogenous.

5.2 Leave-One-Out IV

The F-statistic of 396 (Panel A) indicates strong relevance, but the partial R² is extremely low (0.0002). The balance tests (Table B.8) fail on all four observables. Panel C produces 3.64 (19× Panel A), indicating extreme specification sensitivity. The LATE interpretation is compressed but appropriate; the complier population may not be representative.

5.3 Bajari–Ye Implementation

Critical concern: Number of bids is included in the first-stage bid equation. This is an equilibrium outcome, not a cost shifter. Bajari & Ye (2003, p.978) require cost variables. If n_bids is correlated with FL status (it is, per Table 6), the residuals mechanically correlate with FL classification, potentially generating the exchangeability violation.

5.4 Callaway–Sant'Anna DiD

ATT = 0.014 (SE = 0.039), null. MDE = 0.076 exceeds OLS (0.064). Treatment timing (first FL entry) may be endogenous to cartel formation. The design cannot distinguish low power from no causal effect.

6. Theory Evaluation

6.1 Framework Assessment

The two regimes are defined by distributional assumptions (U[b̅, b̅+δ] vs N(μc, σc²)), not derived from equilibrium conditions. The FOC for m* is asserted without derivation. No game tree, no strategic interaction, no equilibrium concept.

7. Literature Positioning

7.1 Conley-Decarolis (2016, AEJ:Micro)

The closest antecedent. Detects bidder groups from co-bidding patterns without bid values. FL is operationally simpler but less general. Direct comparison needed.

7.2 Chassang-Ortner (2022, Econometrica)

Develops robust screens valid under minimal assumptions. FL is not robust in the Chassang-Ortner sense—it relies on a specific threshold and distributional assumption.

7.3 Missing Citations

Kawai-Nakabayashi-Ortner (working paper), Decarolis (2014 AER), Decarolis-Giuffrida (2017), Kawai-Nakabayashi (2014).

7.4 Developing-Country Contribution

Genuine practical contribution but not an IO contribution. RAND distinguishes policy relevance from intellectual novelty.

8. Conclusion and Conditions

The H1 contribution—FL as a practical screening tool—is genuine and empirically well-supported. However, the causal claims (H2) rest on an IV that fails balance, Bajari–Ye tests with internal inconsistencies, and a null DiD. RAND requires either clean causal identification or a structural contribution.

Three conditions for R&R at RAND:

  1. Resolve identification. Find clean exogenous variation or drop the causal claim and reframe as a detection methodology paper.
  2. Fix Bajari–Ye. Remove n_bids from first stage. Re-test fake-groups placebo within FL-present/absent tenders separately.
  3. Explain network-split result. Why do concentrated-market FL firms show zero price effect despite having the strongest cover-bidding network signatures?

Is RAND the right journal? If the authors deliver on condition (1), yes. Otherwise, IJIO or JLEO would be more appropriate, where the policy contribution and CADE validation are more valued.
