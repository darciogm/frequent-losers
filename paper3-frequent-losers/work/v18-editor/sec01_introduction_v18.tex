\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cartel enforcement begins before cartel proof exists. Agencies
must decide where to spend investigative resources while the
evidence needed for liability is still costly to recover. The
economics of law enforcement
\citep{becker1968crime,stigler1970optimum,baker2002case,harrington2008detecting}
prescribes that when observation is costly, an initial screen
should use information already in the agency's hands and reserve
costly evidentiary stages for the candidates the screen ranks
highest. Procurement-cartel detection usually begins one layer
later. The empirical industrial-organization literature reads
coordination off the distribution of submitted bids
\citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine,chassang2022robust}:
per-bidder amounts, within-tender dispersion, bid rankings, and
sometimes bid histories. Many enforcement environments routinely
expose only a thinner layer: who participated, who won, what was
bought, and at what price. The bid-by-bid record may exist, but
it sits in operational files that have to be recovered through
a case-specific forensic request.

The question here is what antitrust enforcement can do at that
earlier layer. The answer is not cartel proof. It is evidentiary
triage: a cheap award-layer ranking of firms and procurement
environments that indicates where bid-layer forensics should
begin. We study this architecture on Sao Paulo's BEC procurement
platform
($\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$, $\valSampleN$ tender-items).

The screen uses four award-record fields: participant identity,
winner identity, item code, and negotiated price. It flags
\emph{frequent losers}: firms with zero wins whose participation
intensity among always-loser firms lies above a median plus
$1.5$ interquartile-range rule. The logic is simple. In a
cover-bidding arrangement, loser-side firms are deployed to
create the appearance of competition while avoiding the win. The
wins-zero filter isolates the side of the bidding pool the award
layer can plausibly see; participation intensity within that
stratum ranks exposure to the cartel's deployment problem. The
binary frequent-loser rule is a coarsening of that rank, not a
claim of legal membership.

That scope determines the validation target. We do not ask
whether award records identify cartel members for sanction. They
do not contain the evidence for that claim. We validate instead
against loser-side cartel adjacency: firms in the always-loser
stratum that bid alongside direct defendants in adjudicated
CADE procurement-cartel cases. Direct defendants are
legal-membership anchors and are often winner-side; cobidders
are the loser-side footprint the award-layer screen is designed
to rank. Procurement-cartel scholarship documents the same
asymmetry: when collusion takes the form of bid rotation or
market allocation, winners are designated by allocation rules
and rotate across tenders, while cover bidders are the assigned
losers \citep{marshall2012economics,kawai2022detecting}.
Stronger discrimination against cobidders than against direct
defendants is therefore a design signature, not a fallback
target.

Five empirical implications organize the exercise. The screen
should discriminate adjudicated cobidders inside the
always-loser population, including when the score is formed
before the labels are observed. As a loser-side construct, it
should perform worse against direct CADE defendants than against
cobidders. If cobidders are more than active losing firms, their
firm- and bid-level profiles should resemble credible cover
bidding: deployment near direct defendants, concentrated market
portfolios, plausibly close losing bids, and dispersion
consistent with rotation across cover roles. If award and bid
records carry different information, the award-layer statistic
should complement rather than replace bid-distribution methods.
Finally, if the architecture has enforcement value, sequencing
award-layer screening before bid-layer forensics should reduce
the bid-microdata pool while preserving most recall against
adjudicated cobidders.

The results line up with those implications. The main
institutional payoff is the sequential gatekeeper exercise:
placing the award-layer screen ahead of bid-layer forensics cuts
the bid-microdata pool the forensic stage must process by
$\valArchFootprintReduction$ while still recovering
$\valArchTPThouSeqK$ of $\valArchNPos$ adjudicated cobidders.
Discrimination is out-of-sample, not retrospective fitting:
built on $2009$--$2016$ participation and evaluated on
$2017$--$2019$ labels, the screen ranks later-adjudicated CADE
cobidders at firm-level AUC $\valAUCFLfirmTemp$. On the same
cartel-adjacency target, it reaches discrimination comparable to
a seven-feature Imhof--Wallimann bid-distribution pipeline and
adds non-redundant signal when the two are combined
($+\valImhofIncFL$ AUC, DeLong $p=\valImhofIncFLp$). A four-field
award-layer statistic does not dominate bid-distribution
forensics. The two layers are complements: the cheap layer tells
the agency where the costly layer should be applied first.

The cobidder evidence narrows the interpretation. Within the
frequent-loser stratum, cobidders face more unique winners, bid
more often alongside direct CADE defendants, and concentrate
activity in narrower item-group portfolios than other frequent
losers. The LANCES export lets us look one layer deeper:
cobidders bid closer to winners and show higher within-firm
dispersion in the bid gap, with both signs surviving a
multivariate logit that controls for participation intensity.
These facts do not prove cartel membership. They address the
most direct objection to the validation exercise: cobidders are
not merely high-volume losing firms; their loser-side profile is
consistent with credible cover bidding in the modern
cover-bidding literature
\citep{asker2010leniency,marshall2012economics}.

The price evidence is subordinate. Frequent-loser presence is
associated with a $\valHeadlineRange$ higher conditional log
negotiated unit price across four broad-sample estimators, with
the positive imprint concentrated in the highest tender-value
segment. Under overlap restriction the aggregate sign reverses,
but the highest segment remains positive. We report this as
corroboration that the screen tracks environments where
collusion-relevant behavior matters, not as a damages estimate
or a causal effect on prices. The main claim does not rest on
either sign of the price coefficient.

The contribution is to change where familiar tools enter the
enforcement sequence. Enforcement-design models explain why
costly investigation should be sequenced
\citep{becker1968crime,stigler1970optimum,baker2002case,harrington2008detecting};
bid-distribution screens show how to detect suspicious bidding
once bid microdata are available
\citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine,chassang2022robust};
bid-coordination tests, including \citet{conley2016detecting}-style
coalition inference, evaluate collusion conditional on suspected
partitions or rich bidding behavior inside an already suspected
environment; and cover-bidding studies characterize loser-side
roles after adjudication, leniency, or discovery
\citep{porter1999ohio,pesendorfer2000study,asker2010leniency}.
A more recent procurement-enforcement-design literature studies
how rules, discretion, and bureaucratic selection at the award
stage shape outcomes \citep{decarolis2014awarding,decarolis2020rules,decarolis2020bureaucratic};
this paper studies what an enforcement screen can do with the
information those settings take as their starting point. We
study the earlier stage at which none of those objects is yet
available to the agency at low cost: the empirical question is
whether award records alone can rank loser-side cartel-adjacent
firms well enough to decide where bid-layer forensics should
begin.

Three boundaries discipline the claim. The screen creates
suspicion, not legal proof. The validation target is
cartel-adjacent cobidding, not cartel membership. The price
imprint is descriptive, not a damages calculation. These limits
define the stage of enforcement studied here rather than
apologize for it. The resulting claim is deliberately narrower
than cartel detection and broader than a new statistic: when
proof is costly and suspicion can be generated from records the
agency already holds, the policy question is not whether the
cheap layer can decide liability---it cannot---but whether it
can discipline the allocation of costly forensic attention. In
this setting it can. The award layer supplies a legally limited
but empirically informative triage stage; the bid layer remains
where proof is assembled.
