\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cartel enforcement begins before cartel proof exists. Agencies
must decide where to spend investigative resources while the
evidence needed for liability is still costly to recover. That
sequencing problem is familiar in the economics of law
enforcement: when observation is costly, the first screen should
use information already in the agency's hands, and the expensive
evidentiary stage should be reserved for the candidates that
screen ranks highest
\citep{becker1968crime,stigler1970optimum,baker2002case,harrington2008detecting}.
Procurement-cartel detection usually begins one layer later. The
empirical industrial-organization literature reads coordination
off the distribution of submitted bids
\citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine,chassang2022robust}.
Those methods are powerful, but they presume the bid layer:
per-bidder bid amounts, within-tender dispersion, bid
rankings, and sometimes bid histories. Many enforcement
environments routinely expose only a thinner layer: who
participated, who won, what was bought, and at what price. The
bid-by-bid record may exist, but it sits in operational files
that have to be recovered through a case-specific forensic
request.

The question here is what antitrust enforcement can do at that
earlier layer. The answer is not cartel proof. It is evidentiary
triage: a cheap award-layer ranking of firms and procurement
environments that indicates where bid-layer forensics should
begin. We study this architecture on Sao Paulo's BEC procurement
platform
($\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$, $\valSampleN$ tender-items).
The screen uses four award-record fields only: participant
identity, winner identity, item code, and negotiated price. It
flags \emph{frequent losers}: firms with zero wins whose
participation intensity among always-loser firms lies above a
median plus $1.5$ interquartile-range rule. The logic is simple.
In a cover-bidding arrangement, loser-side firms are
deployed to create the appearance of competition while avoiding
the win. The wins-zero filter isolates the side of the bidding
pool the award layer can plausibly see; participation intensity
within that stratum ranks exposure to the cartel's deployment
problem. The binary frequent-loser rule is a coarsening of that
rank, not a claim of legal membership.

That scope determines the validation target. We do not ask
whether award records identify cartel members for sanction. They
do not contain the evidence for that claim. Instead, we validate
against loser-side cartel adjacency: firms in the always-loser
stratum that bid alongside direct defendants in adjudicated CADE
procurement-cartel cases. Direct defendants and cobidders play
different roles in the evidence. Direct defendants are the
membership labels in the legal file and are often winner-side
firms. Cobidders are the loser-side footprint the award-layer
screen is meant to rank. Stronger discrimination against
cobidders than against direct defendants is therefore a design
signature, not a fallback target.

Five empirical implications organize the exercise. A useful
award-layer screen should discriminate adjudicated cobidders
inside the always-loser population, including when the score is
formed before the labels are observed. Because the statistic is a
loser-side screen, it should perform worse against direct CADE
defendants than against cobidders. If cobidders are more than
active losing firms, their firm-level and bid-level profiles
should resemble credible cover bidding: deployment near direct
defendants, concentrated market portfolios, plausibly close
losing bids, and dispersion consistent with rotation across cover
roles. If award records and bid records carry different
information, the award-layer statistic should complement rather
than replace bid-distribution methods. Finally, if the
architecture has enforcement value, placing the award-layer
screen before bid-layer forensics should reduce the
bid-microdata pool while preserving most recall against
adjudicated cobidders.

The results line up with those implications. Built on
$2009$--$2016$ participation and evaluated on $2017$--$2019$
labels, the screen prospectively flags adjudicated cobidders at
firm-level AUC $\valAUCFLfirmTemp$. On the same
cartel-adjacency target, it reaches discrimination comparable to
a seven-feature Imhof--Wallimann bid-distribution pipeline and
adds non-redundant signal when the two are combined
($+\valImhofIncFL$ AUC, DeLong $p=\valImhofIncFLp$). In the
sequential gatekeeper exercise, the award-layer screen reduces
the bid-microdata pool for the forensic stage by
$\valArchFootprintReduction$ while still recovering
$\valArchTPThouSeqK$ of $\valArchNPos$ adjudicated cobidders.
The point is not that a four-field award-layer statistic
dominates bid-distribution forensics. It does not. The point is
that the two layers are complements, and the cheap layer can
tell the agency where the costly layer should be applied first.

The cobidder evidence also narrows the interpretation. Within
the frequent-loser stratum, cobidders face more unique winners,
bid more often alongside direct CADE defendants, and concentrate
activity in narrower item-group portfolios than other frequent
losers. The LANCES export lets us look one layer deeper:
cobidders bid closer to winners and show higher within-firm
dispersion in the bid gap, with both signs surviving a
multivariate logit that controls for participation intensity.
Those facts do not prove cartel membership. They do address the
most direct objection to the validation exercise. Cobidders are
not merely high-volume losing firms; their loser-side profile is
consistent with credible cover bidding in the modern
cover-bidding literature
\citep{asker2010leniency,marshall2012economics}.

The price evidence has a narrower role. Frequent-loser presence is
associated with a $\valHeadlineRange$ higher conditional log
negotiated unit price across four broad-sample estimators, and
the positive imprint is concentrated in the highest tender-value
segment. Under overlap restriction the aggregate sign reverses:
three lower tender-value segments are negative and the highest
segment remains positive. We report this as economic
corroboration that the screen tracks environments where
collusion-relevant behavior matters, not as a damages estimate
or a causal effect of cover bidding on prices. The main claim
does not rest on either sign of the price coefficient.

The contribution is to change where several familiar tools enter
the enforcement sequence. For enforcement-design work, we provide
an empirical architecture for staged information acquisition under
incomplete observability. For bid-distribution
screening, it supplies a first-stage award-layer ranking that
indicates where bid-distribution moments should be recovered,
rather than a substitute for those moments. For bid-coordination
tests, it offers an ex ante firm-level partition computable
before a case record defines suspected colluders and competitive
fringe. For cover-bidding studies, which usually identify cover
bidders ex post through leniency or judicial records
\citep{porter1999ohio,pesendorfer2000study,asker2010leniency},
it offers prospective loser-side candidates from public award
records alone.

Three boundaries matter throughout. The screen creates
suspicion, not legal proof. The validation target is
cartel-adjacent cobidding, not cartel membership. The price
imprint is descriptive, not a damages calculation. These limits
are not cosmetic disclaimers; they define the stage of
enforcement studied here. Award-layer triage is useful
precisely because it occurs before bid-layer evidence is
recovered and before legal proof can be assembled.

The resulting claim is deliberately narrower than cartel
detection and broader than a new statistic. When proof is costly
and suspicion can be generated from records the agency already
holds, the first policy question is not whether the cheap layer
can decide liability. It cannot. The question is whether that
layer can discipline the allocation of costly forensic attention.
In this setting it can. The award layer supplies a legally
limited but empirically informative triage stage; the bid layer
remains where proof is assembled.
