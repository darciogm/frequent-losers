\section{Sequencing Screening and Forensic Proof}
\label{sec:screening_forensics}

The previous sections establish the target and the type. The
remaining question is institutional: what should an agency do
with a signal that is cheap enough to compute before it has the
evidence needed for proof? The answer is not to treat the
award-layer screen as a substitute for bid analysis. The answer
is to put it before bid analysis. In that sequence, suspicion is
created on the record layer the agency already observes, and
forensic work is reserved for a smaller set of firms and
environments where the expected return to costly bid-level
inspection is higher.

\subsection{Bid-Layer Benchmark}
\label{sec:bid_layer_benchmark}

The natural benchmark is the bid-distribution tradition in
cartel screening. Imhof--Wallimann-style detectors use
within-tender bid moments, such as dispersion, skewness, spread,
and distance from the winning bid, to detect patterns consistent
with coordinated bidding \citep{imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine}.
Those moments live one layer deeper than the frequent-loser
screen. They require per-bid offer values and enough usable bid
microdata to construct a firm-level forensic profile. The
award-layer screen requires only participation counts and the
absence of wins.

Table~\ref{tab:imhof_cost_v18} puts the two layers on the same
cartel-adjacency target. Within the always-loser pool for which
bid-distribution features can be computed
($N=\valImhofPoolN$), the binary frequent-loser flag reaches AUC
$\valImhofFLBin$ ($95\%$ CI $\valImhofFLBinCI$). The continuous
participation score reaches AUC $\valImhofFLcont$
($\valImhofFLcontCI$). The full bid-layer pipeline reaches AUC
$\valImhofFull$ ($\valImhofFullCI$). This is not an
outperformance claim. The point is sharper: a record-layer
statistic, computed before forensic recovery of bid values,
discriminates the same adjudication-anchored target in the same
range as a much richer bid-layer benchmark.

\subsection{Complementary Signals}
\label{sec:complementary_signals}

Comparable AUCs do not by themselves justify a two-stage
architecture. If the award-layer statistic were just a noisy
version of the bid-layer score, it would be useful only where
bid microdata are missing. The stronger result is that the two
layers are not redundant. Adding the award-layer statistic to
the bid-layer benchmark raises discrimination to
$\valImhofComboCont$ ($\valImhofComboContCI$). In the identical
same-sample audit, where all models are evaluated only on firms
with complete bid-layer features, the frequent-loser flag adds
$+\valImhofIncFL$ AUC over the full Imhof--Wallimann benchmark
alone (DeLong $p=\valImhofIncFLp$). The combined specifications
gain $\valImhofIncCombo$ AUC over the bid-layer benchmark
($p<0.001$).

That gain is substantively important for JLEO purposes because
it separates two legal-economic objects. Bid moments are closer
to forensic proof: they speak to the shape of bidding inside a
tender. Participation intensity is closer to investigative
priority: it asks whether a firm repeatedly supplies losing
participation in markets where winners rotate through the award
layer. A regulator should not collapse those objects. The first
creates a reason to look; the second helps evaluate what is
found after looking. Their combination improves prediction
because the two stages measure different parts of the suspected
coordination process.

\subsection{A Sequential Gatekeeper}
\label{sec:sequential_gatekeeper}

The operational test is not whether the cheap screen
beats the forensic benchmark. The relevant test is whether it can
reduce the forensic workload without throwing away too many
adjudication-anchored positives. Table~\ref{tab:gatekeeper_v18}
reports that exercise for $\valArchPoolSize$ always-loser firms
with bid-layer features and $\valArchNPos$ CADE-cobidder
positives. Joint scoring, which gives the classifier both the
award-layer score and bid-layer moments for every firm, is the
upper bound for an agency willing to recover bid microdata on
the full pool. At the top $1{,}000$ flags, it finds
$\valArchTPThouJoint$ cobidders, or recall
$\valArchRecallThouJoint$.

The sequential design asks for less. It first ranks firms using
the award layer. It keeps the top
$K_1=\valArchSeqKone$ firms. Only then does it recover or
process bid microdata and apply the bid-layer benchmark within
that survivor pool. At the top $1{,}000$ final flags, the
sequential rule finds $\valArchTPThouSeqK$ of
$\valArchNPos$ cobidders, or recall $\valArchRecallThouSeqK$.
The loss relative to joint scoring is
$\valArchRecallCost$, while the bid-microdata footprint falls by
$\valArchFootprintReduction$.

The sequential rule is not the precision champion; joint scoring
has the easier job because it uses every information layer for
every firm. The sequential rule earns its place in a different
way. It makes the expensive forensic stage conditional on a
cheap suspicion stage, and the empirical cost of doing so is
modest relative to the reduction in bid-microdata exposure. That
is the tradeoff faced by an enforcement agency that cannot
inspect every bidder, every bid, and every tender with the same
forensic intensity.

\subsection{Temporal Audit}
\label{sec:temporal_gatekeeper}

The in-sample gatekeeper exercise still deserves skepticism. A
classifier trained and evaluated inside the full sample may rank
firms well because it sees a long history close to the
adjudicated outcome. We repeat the gatekeeper audit
with features restricted to the pre-target window,
$2009$--$2016$, while evaluating against the same post-sample
adjudication-anchored cobidder target. The temporal-holdout pool
contains $\valArchTHPoolSize$ always-loser firms with complete
train-window bid features and $\valArchTHNPos$ positives.

The temporal audit preserves the architecture. At top $500$, the
award-only rule loses $\valArchTHFLDrop$ of its in-sample true
positives, joint scoring loses $\valArchTHJointDrop$, and the
sequential rule loses only $\valArchTHSeqDrop$. In levels, the
sequential rule recovers $\valArchTHTPFiveHundSeqK$ positives at
top $500$, compared with $\valArchTHTPFiveHundJoint$ for joint
scoring. At top $1{,}000$, it recovers
$\valArchTHTPThouSeqK$ positives, compared with
$\valArchTHTPThouJoint$ under joint scoring. The result is not
that temporal holdout eliminates all fragility. It does not. The
result is narrower and more useful: conditioning bid-layer
forensics on an earlier award-layer screen remains competitive
out of time while keeping the forensic footprint small.

The legal interpretation follows the same boundary as the
empirical design. A frequent-loser flag cannot establish an
agreement, identify a damages base, or prove that a particular
bid was cover. It can identify where the agency should spend the
next unit of investigative effort. The bid-layer stage then
examines whether the bidding record contains the richer patterns
needed for proof. For a journal concerned with how law works
under institutional constraint, that sequencing is the
contribution: a cheap administrative record can improve the
allocation of costly legal evidence gathering without pretending
to be legal evidence itself.
