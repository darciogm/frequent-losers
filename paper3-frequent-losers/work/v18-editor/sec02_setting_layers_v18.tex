\section{Costly Proof and Observable Awards}
\label{sec:setting_layers}

\subsection{Screening, Investigation, and Proof}
\label{sec:setting_screening}

Antitrust agencies rarely begin with the evidence they need at
the end. They begin with coarse records, complaints, leniency
leads, procurement anomalies, or repeated interactions that make
some firms and markets worth a closer look. Legal proof of a
procurement cartel comes later. It combines case records, bid
histories, communications, leniency testimony, and economic
evidence into a file that can support adjudication. The
organizational problem is therefore not only how to classify
conduct once rich evidence is available. It is also how to decide
which files should receive the costly evidence-gathering effort
in the first place.

That distinction changes what a screen is asked to do.
Bid-distribution methods ask whether the submitted bids look
suspicious, conditional on access to per-bidder bid amounts. An
award-layer screen asks an earlier question: before recovering
those bid amounts, can the agency rank the firms and procurement
environments where bid-level forensics is most likely to pay off?
The first exercise is closer to proof; the second is triage. Our
interest is in the second exercise. Bid-distribution forensics
remains indispensable at the evidentiary stage, but award records
may help decide where that stage should start.

\subsection{The BEC Enforcement Environment}
\label{sec:setting_bec}

São Paulo's BEC platform contains both sides of this information
problem. It is a large electronic procurement environment,
covering
$\valSampleN$ tender-items during
$\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$, and it preserves a public
contract-award envelope for each tender-item. It also sits inside
a legal environment in which cartel cases are adjudicated by
CADE, the Brazilian competition authority, creating an external
source of legal labels against which an award-layer screen can be
evaluated.

Two procurement modalities dominate the sample. \emph{Convite}
is a sealed-bid invitation procedure under Lei~$8.666/93$ with a
statutory three-bidder quorum and a low value cap. \emph{Pregão}
is a reverse electronic auction under Lei~$\valLeiPregao$ in
which bidders submit progressively lower offers in real time,
with no minimum-bidder rule. These modalities differ in both
bidding rule and observability regime. Convite is closer to an
award-only environment from the analyst's perspective; pregão
generates a richer real-time interaction footprint. We use this
contrast later as scope information for the screen, not as a
source of causal identification. The common element is the award
layer that survives across modalities.

\subsection{Award Layer and Forensic-Recoverable Bid Layer}
\label{sec:setting_layers_two}

BEC exposes two analytically distinct layers. The
\emph{award layer} carries the fields an enforcement analyst can
query routinely: participant identity, winner identity, item
code, negotiated price, procuring unit, year, and procurement
modality. The \emph{forensic-recoverable bid layer} carries
per-bidder bid amounts, bid timestamps, and bid-revision
sequences. Those bid records exist in the BEC operational system,
but they are not exported to the ordinary analytical warehouse.
They must be recovered through an administrative LANCES export:
feasible for evaluation and forensic use, but costly relative to
querying the award layer. OECD reviews of Brazilian procurement
infrastructure describe this kind of administrative latency as a
practical constraint on routine monitoring
\citep{oecd2019government}.

Figure~\ref{fig:coarsening_v18} summarizes the distinction. The
left side reports the bid-layer features required by the
Imhof--Wallimann-style forensic benchmark: within-tender
coefficient of variation, dispersion across tenders, skewness,
kurtosis, normalized spread, log min--max, and second-lowest bid
distance. The right side reports the four fields used by the
award-layer screen. Our screen uses only the right side. The
bid-layer features enter as the forensic-stage benchmark and as a
bid-level validation bridge after the LANCES export has been
recovered.

The two-layer problem is not peculiar to BEC. In the United
States, FPDS-NG preserves federal contract awards as routine
administrative records, while bid files sit with contracting
agencies and are typically recovered through case-specific
process. In the European Union, TED mandates publication of
contract-award notices, but per-bidder bid amounts are preserved
heterogeneously across member-state systems; a recent procurement
screening review reports that fewer than half of surveyed systems
make bid amounts retrievable at the analytical-warehouse layer
\citep{sanchezgraells2019screening}. These examples are not used
as external validation. They establish the portability of the
information problem: award records are often routine, bid
microdata often forensic.

\subsection{CADE Labels and the Cartel-Adjacency Target}
\label{sec:setting_cade}

The legal labels come from CADE's procurement-cartel
adjudications. CADE enforces Brazilian competition law under
Lei~$12.529/2011$, and its leniency and adjudication records
identify direct defendants in procurement-cartel cases. The
portfolio linked to BEC contains $12$ adjudicated procurement
cartel cases with $65$ firm-defendants, of which
$\valDirectCADE$ are active in BEC during the sample window.
Final decisions span $2015$--$2025$, with process-to-ruling
intervals of $4$--$16$ years. That lag is central to the
screening interpretation. An award-layer screen is valuable only
if it can move suspicion toward forensic investigation before
the adjudication record exists.

The adjudication record creates two empirical targets. Direct
CADE defendants are the membership labels in the legal file, but
they are not the natural target of a loser-side award-layer
screen. They are often designated winners or otherwise
winner-side firms in the cartel's allocation. Cobidders are
different: always-loser firms that appear alongside direct
defendants in BEC tender-items. They are indirect labels, and we
do not treat them as proof of membership. They are, however, the
cartel-adjacency object the award layer can plausibly recover:
the loser-side participation footprint adjacent to adjudicated
cartel members.

The rest of the analysis follows that boundary. We evaluate the
screen primarily against cobidders inside the always-loser
stratum. Weak performance against direct defendants is not a
failure of the design; it is the expected consequence of a
statistic built to rank loser-side exposure. CADE adjudications
are used in a legally limited way: as external evidence that some
bidding environments were cartel-affected and as
direct-defendant anchors for constructing cartel-adjacent
cobidder labels. The screen remains a triage device. It does not
adjudicate liability.
