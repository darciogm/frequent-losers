Dear Editors,

We submit "Cheap Signals, Costly Proof: Award-Layer Evidence Triage in Cartel Enforcement" for consideration at the Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization. The paper studies an enforcement-design problem that arises before legal proof exists: how should an antitrust agency allocate costly forensic attention when routine contract-award records are observable but bid-level evidence must be recovered through more expensive case-specific processes?

**Contribution.** The paper reframes procurement-cartel screening as an evidence-allocation problem under incomplete observability. The empirical cartel-screening literature ranks tenders or firms using bid microdata --- the within-tender dispersion, rank, and timing screens of Porter and Zona (1993), Bajari and Ye (2003), Abrantes-Metz et al. (2006), Imhof et al. (2018), and the machine-learning approaches of Huber and Imhof (2019) and Wallimann et al. (2023). These tools sit close to the evidentiary stage. We study the prior institutional stage, at which agencies observe award records but have not yet recovered the bid-level evidence needed for forensic evaluation. The legal-economic content is that the two layers play different roles. Award records can rank forensic priority; they cannot establish agreement, liability, or damages.

**Setting and screen.** The setting is the BEC procurement platform of the State of São Paulo, 2009–2019: 1,654,401 tender-items, 41,444 firms, and the universe of CADE procurement-cartel adjudications anchored in this market. BEC is institutionally rich: a uniform award layer recorded across two procurement modalities (electronic auction and sealed-bid invitation), eleven years of repeated participation, observable firm identities, and a deep adjudication record at the federal antitrust authority. The screen itself is deliberately minimal: among always-loser firms (those with zero wins across the eleven-year window), we rank loser-side participation intensity using log(1 + tenders_count) and implement an auditable binary cutoff that flags frequent losers for forensic priority.

**Validation.** The screen is disciplined as a series of threats rather than as a single performance number. The object is the allocation of forensic priority, not a damages estimator or a membership label. We validate the ranking against adjudication-anchored cobidders --- always-loser firms observed alongside direct CADE defendants --- because that is the loser-side adjacency target the award layer can plausibly rank before bid-level proof is recovered. The validation battery includes participation-volume placebos, an opportunity-set exposure-discipline battery, a strict prospective temporal holdout, cross-validation leakage audits at the cobidder-firm level, and a direct-defendant scope check. We are explicit about the limit of the exposure battery: it disciplines, but does not fully hold fixed, every product-by-buyer-by-year-by-modality opportunity set.

The direct-defendant check is deliberately included as a boundary, not as a failure. Loser-side ranks do not rank winners; direct defendants are often associated with winning or allocation roles in procurement-cartel cases. Reporting that boundary prevents the screen from being read as a cartel-membership label and clarifies what kind of object it is and is not.

**Operational result.** The main operational finding is a sequential gatekeeping rule that turns the cheap-signal/costly-proof distinction into an architecture. Used as a gatekeeper before bid microdata are recovered, the award-layer ranking reduces the bid-microdata pool for the forensic stage by 83 per cent while recovering 131 of 193 adjudication-anchored cobidders. A joint scoring model that uses both layers simultaneously is reported as the full-observability upper bound; the sequential rule is the operationally relevant architecture for agencies that must decide where to spend costly bid recovery.

**External validity.** We focus on a single jurisdiction because the legal-economic question is cleanest inside a panel that combines a rich award layer, a deep adjudication anchor, and stable participant identities over eleven years. The contribution is the architecture rather than the BEC numerical estimates: an agency in a different system should not import the BEC cutoff mechanically but should validate the loser-side ranking against its own enforcement anchors and observability constraints. We have a cross-jurisdiction federal replication in progress on the Brazilian ComprasNet platform (2013–2019) and stand ready to report it in a revision if the editors and referees would find it useful at that stage.

**Fit with JLEO.** The paper's central object is not a classifier but an enforcement institution. The legal-economic issue is how suspicion, investigation, and proof should be sequenced when information is costly and asymmetrically observable across stages of an antitrust process. The manuscript engages institutional design, the boundary between administrative suspicion and legal proof, and the operational allocation of forensic capacity --- themes core to the journal.

**What the paper does not claim.** It does not identify cartel members, prove agreement, estimate damages, or replace bid-distribution forensics. It proposes and evaluates a legally limited triage architecture: cheap administrative records discipline where proof-producing investigation should begin, while adjudication remains grounded in the richer case record.

A working version was presented at internal Insper seminars and at events with members of the Brazilian Antitrust Authority (CADE). The manuscript has not been previously submitted to JLEO and is not under consideration at another journal. The authors have no conflict of interest with the cases or institutions referenced.
