\section{A Loser-Side Screen for Cartel Adjacency}
\label{sec:award_screen}

\subsection{The Participation Primitive}
\label{sec:award_primitive}

The award layer cannot observe whether a losing bid was sincere,
strategic, or coordinated. It can observe something more basic:
which firms repeatedly entered tenders and never won. That is the
primitive used here. The screen begins with the zero-win subset
and then ranks firms inside that subset by participation
intensity. In the BEC data this conditioning set contains
$\valAlwaysLosers$ always-loser firms. A firm above the
participation cutoff is a frequent loser.

The economic reason for starting on the loser side is simple.
Cover bidding is useful to a cartel because it supplies the
appearance of competition while leaving the designated winner in
place. A cover bidder is therefore expected to lose in the
cartel-targeted tenders where it is deployed. Zero wins alone is
not enough, of course. Many firms lose because they are small,
inexperienced, poorly matched to the item, or simply unlucky.
The additional signal is persistence: among firms that never
win, repeated participation is harder to rationalize as ordinary
competitive entry, and easier to reconcile with a role that pays
off outside the observed award outcome. The formal appendix
states this as a monotone-ranking result: conditional on zero
wins, $\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ ranks the posterior
probability of the cover-bidder type under a sparse
separating-equilibrium framework.

This argument is intentionally modest. It does not say every
frequent loser is a cover bidder, nor that cover bidding is the
only reason for repeated zero-win participation. Capacity
signaling, subcontracting relationships, eligibility
maintenance, and market-intelligence gathering are also possible.
The claim is narrower: the award layer supplies a ranking
statistic for loser-side cartel adjacency, and that statistic is
worth validating against adjudicated cartel records before any
membership language is used.

\subsection{The Binary Rule}
\label{sec:award_rule}

The continuous score is
$\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ computed among always-loser
firms. For a discrete screen, we use the median plus
$1.5\times$ interquartile range of the always-loser participation
distribution. The resulting cutoff is $\valThreshold$
tender-items with zero wins and flags $\valFL$ frequent losers.
The rule is fixed from the participation distribution, before
using CADE labels, and requires no bid amounts, bid rankings, or
within-tender dispersion measures.

The binary cutoff is useful because enforcement programs often
need a list, not only a rank. Still, the rank is the economic
object. The binary rule compresses a continuous participation
gradient into a manageable first-stage flag. Later sections
therefore report both objects where possible: the frequent-loser
indicator as the administrable screen, and
$\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ as the underlying ranking
statistic. This distinction matters in two places. First, a
sharp cutoff is easier for firms to manipulate than a rank, a
point we return to when discussing adversarial adaptation.
Second, bid-distribution forensics can be sequenced after either
object: the agency may request bid microdata for all firms above
the cutoff, or for the top-ranked firms under the continuous
score.

\subsection{Populations and Legal-Economic Role}
\label{sec:award_populations}

Table~\ref{tab:populations_v18} fixes the populations used in
the analysis. The broad BEC universe contains all firms that appear
as participants during the sample window. The screen operates
inside the always-loser stratum. Frequent losers are the flagged
subset of that stratum. CADE direct defendants are firms named in
adjudicated procurement-cartel cases and active in BEC. Cobidders
are always-loser firms that appear alongside those direct
defendants in BEC tender-items.

The table also clarifies what the screen is not designed to do.
Direct defendants are the legal membership anchors, but they are
not the natural target of a loser-side award-layer statistic.
Cobidders are indirect labels and cannot adjudicate liability.
They are the validation object because they occupy the part of
the bidding pool the screen can observe: firms that lose, keep
showing up, and appear near adjudicated cartel defendants. The
next section asks whether the frequent-loser ranking actually
concentrates on that population at rates above what participation
volume alone would imply.
