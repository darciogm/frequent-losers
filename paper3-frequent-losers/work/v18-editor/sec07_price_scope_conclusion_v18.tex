\section{Scope, Limits, and Price Corroboration}
\label{sec:price_scope_conclusion}

The paper's enforcement claim does not require a causal price
effect. A screen can be useful if it ranks where forensic
attention should begin, even when the price record is too
coarse to support damages, markup estimates, or welfare losses.
This section therefore does not treat price as proof of harm.
It uses price evidence more narrowly: as descriptive
corroboration and scope information.

The question is whether flagged environments look economically
non-neutral, and where that interpretation stops. Broad-sample
price associations can show whether the screen points toward
procurement environments with higher negotiated prices. Overlap
and segment results then discipline that reading. In
particular, the sign reversal under overlap restriction is not
a robustness failure to hide; it is the boundary that keeps
price evidence from becoming a damages claim.

The section also records the paper's portability and
strategic-response limits. Award-layer triage is portable only
where participant identities, winner identities, comparable
item definitions, repeated procurement, and forensic follow-up
are institutionally available. A static binary threshold can be
gamed, which is why the architecture should be paired with
threshold refresh, bunching checks, and bid-layer forensics.
The purpose of this section is therefore defensive and
clarifying: it shows where the screen has economic content, and
where the paper's claims stop.

\subsection{A Descriptive Price Imprint}
\label{sec:price_imprint_v18}

The price regressions are reported to assess economic
non-neutrality, not to estimate harm. If the award-layer screen
ranks cartel-adjacent environments, it is useful to ask whether
those environments also differ in the economic outcome agencies
care about. The answer can only play a corroborative role. The
estimates below are descriptive associations, not cartel
markups, damages estimates, or causal effects of frequent-loser
presence.

In the broad item-level price regressions, frequent-loser
presence is positively associated with log negotiated unit
price. The coefficient is $\valTabPricesColOne$ with item and
year fixed effects and $\valTabPricesColTwo$ after adding
procuring-unit fixed effects. The association is larger in
pregão, $\valTabPricesColThree$, than in convite,
$\valTabPricesColFour$. Across the main descriptive estimators,
the implied range is $\valHeadlineRange$
(Table~\ref{tab:price_scope_v18}).

The pregão--convite split is useful as scope information, not
causal identification. If frequent losers were mainly quorum
fillers created by the three-bidder rule in convite, the price
imprint should be stronger there. Instead, the larger estimate
appears in pregão, the real-time electronic environment where
loser-side positioning is easier to deploy repeatedly. This
contrast supports the screening interpretation, but it does not
identify a procedure-specific mechanism.

The boundary is important. The positive broad-sample
association is not a causal estimate of cover bidding's effect
on prices. The available quasi-experimental routes do not
support that claim: discontinuity designs around procedure
thresholds lack the required density behavior, and the
policy-change design does not supply credible parallel trends.
The role of the price evidence is therefore limited. It shows
that flagged environments are economically non-neutral in the
broad record; the next subsection shows where that
interpretation stops.

\subsection{Where the Price Evidence Stops}
\label{sec:price_scope_v18}

The overlap results define where the price evidence stops. The
sign reversal is not a robustness failure to hide; it is the
boundary that keeps price evidence from becoming a damages
claim. The broad-sample and overlap estimates answer different
questions over different parts of the procurement distribution,
and the difference is what disciplines the interpretation.

The broad-sample estimate averages over the environments into
which frequent losers actually sort. The overlap estimate
restricts or reweights toward cells where frequent-loser-present
and frequent-loser-absent items have comparable support on
observables. These are different descriptive objects. Neither
is a causal effect: both are reweighted averages of the same
conditional association under different sampling weights.

Under overlap-cell ATT reweighting, the aggregate coefficient
reverses to $\valScopeOverlapCoef$; under propensity-score
trimming it becomes $\valScopePSCoef$. Only
$\valDroppedItemsShare$ of treated items lack a within-cell
counterfactual, so the reversal is not mainly a
dropped-observation story. It is a reweighting story: the broad
estimate and the overlap estimate put weight on different parts
of the item-value distribution.

The segment decomposition makes that point explicit. In the
lowest three tender-value quartiles, the overlap estimates are
negative. In the highest tender-value quartile, the estimate
remains positive, $\valBetaQfour$ ($p=\valBetaQfourP$). That
segment is where cartel rents are most plausible ex ante,
because the same fixed cost of coordination is spread over
larger contracts. The interpretation remains limited: the
high-value result is segment-level scope evidence, not a
general price effect.

The direct-CADE overlap estimate is also null,
$\valBetaCADEDirect$ ($p=\valBetaCADEDirectP$), which reinforces
the same boundary. The price regressions do not identify a
treatment effect, a markup, or a damages base. They show that
the award-layer screen points to economically non-neutral
environments in parts of the distribution, while the overlap
reversal prevents that evidence from carrying the paper's main
claim. The next subsection turns from descriptive price
evidence to the institutional conditions under which the
screening architecture is portable.

\subsection{Portability Conditions}
\label{sec:portability_v18}

The architecture is more portable than the estimates. The BEC
results come from a particular procurement platform, legal
environment, and product mix; they should not be read as
numerical external validity for other jurisdictions. What can
travel is the information problem itself: an agency may
routinely observe an award layer while recovering bid-level
evidence only through costly forensic process.

A candidate setting needs several institutional primitives.
Participant identities and winner identities must be observed.
Item definitions must be stable enough for repeated
participation to be meaningful. Procurement must be repeated
enough to generate participation histories. Persistent losing
must carry economic content rather than being purely
mechanical. And the bid layer must be recoverable for forensic
follow-up, because the award layer only creates suspicion; it
does not supply proof.

Those conditions are more plausible in standardized, repeated,
electronically recorded procurement than in bespoke services,
sparse infrastructure projects, or systems with incomplete
participation records. BEC fits the favorable profile
particularly well: standardized goods are common, electronic
records are routine, and pregão-style auctions preserve the
loser-side interaction environment in which the signal is
strongest.

The architecture is harder to deploy where one or more
primitives fail. Bespoke services, large one-off infrastructure
contracts, and sparse procurement environments may not generate
the participation history the screen requires. Systems with
incomplete loser records, weak item comparability, or no
feasible bid-layer recovery would create suspicion without a
path to proof. Under those conditions, award-layer triage
offers little institutional value.

Portability therefore requires recalibration. Another agency
should not import the BEC threshold or item definitions
mechanically. It should rebuild the participation history,
define comparable procurement categories, choose a
capacity-constrained threshold or top-$k$ rule, and validate
against locally meaningful enforcement labels. The portable
lesson is the sequencing architecture, not the number
$\valThreshold$. The next subsection turns to the second
adaptation question: how the rule responds to firms that know
it exists.

\subsection{Strategic Response}
\label{sec:strategic_response_v18}

Strategic response is a real limit on any administrative
screen. Once firms understand that persistent zero-win
participation can trigger attention, they may rotate cover
roles, engineer occasional wins, split identities, or cap
participation below a known threshold. The question is not
whether the binary rule is manipulation-proof---it is
not---but how fragile different parts of the architecture are
under different adaptation strategies.

The adaptation simulation shows a clear hierarchy. Simple
rotation leaves AUC essentially unchanged,
$\valAUCAdaptRotation$ versus baseline $\valAUCBase$.
Occasional wins also leave AUC nearly unchanged,
$\valAUCAdaptOccWins$. CNPJ splitting is more damaging,
$\valAUCAdaptCNPJsplit$. Threshold-aware capping is more
damaging still, $\valAUCAdaptThreshCap$. Combining capping with
rotation lowers AUC to $\valAUCAdaptCombined$.

The pattern is informative. Simple rotation and occasional wins
are absorbed by the persistence signal; identity fragmentation
begins to break the participation history; behavior targeted at
the static cutoff is the most damaging. That is exactly why the
number $\valThreshold$ should be treated as operational rather
than structural. It is useful for an auditable first-stage
implementation in this setting, not as a permanent enforcement
boundary.

The practical implication is adaptive deployment. Agencies
should refresh thresholds, monitor bunching just below the
rule, use continuous ranks or capacity-constrained top-$k$
queues, and pair award-layer screening with bid-layer
forensics whenever bid files can be recovered. The screen
creates a moving queue for forensic attention, not a fixed
legal trigger.

The simulation is a diagnostic, not a full equilibrium model of
evasion. It shows how the ranking can degrade under simple
adaptation strategies; it does not solve the strategic problem
at its root. The paper's claim therefore remains limited:
award-layer triage can help allocate forensic attention, but it
requires adaptive implementation. Strategic response weakens
naive cutoff use more than it invalidates the two-stage
architecture.
