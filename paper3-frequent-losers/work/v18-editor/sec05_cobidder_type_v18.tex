\section{Economic Profile of Cartel-Adjacent Losers}
\label{sec:cobidder_type}

Section~\ref{sec:validation} validates the award-layer ranking
against cartel-adjacent loser-side labels. It does not explain
why those firms are adjacent to direct CADE defendants, and it
does not turn adjacency into membership. This section asks a
narrower profiling question: among firms already inside the
frequent-loser stratum, do cobidders look different from other
frequent losers in directions predicted by the screening
interpretation?

The main alternative is mechanical adjacency. High-volume losing
firms may appear near CADE defendants simply because they appear
in many tenders. Section~\ref{sec:validation} addresses that
concern statistically through participation-stratified
permutation, holdout, and timing audits. Here we address it
economically. We compare cobidders with frequent losers that do
not co-bid with direct defendants. This is the hard comparison:
both groups lose persistently and both cross the same
participation threshold.

The evidence in this section is descriptive profile evidence,
not adjudication. If cobidders differ from other frequent losers
in deployment intensity, proximity to defendants, market
concentration, and bid-level behavior, the cobidder label
becomes harder to reduce to mechanical high-volume losing alone.
The profile does not prove cover bidding in any particular
tender, assign cartel membership, or establish liability. Its
role is to discipline interpretation before the paper turns to
the forensic sequencing exercises in
Section~\ref{sec:screening_forensics}.

\subsection{Firm-Level Signature}
\label{sec:cobidder_firm}

The firm-level comparison is deliberately narrow. We compare
cobidders with frequent-loser non-cobidders, not with the full
BEC population. Both groups are always-losers, and both cross
the same participation threshold. The exercise therefore does
not ask whether cobidders look different from ordinary firms;
it asks whether cartel-adjacent losers look different from
other firms already selected by the screen.

We organize the comparison along four dimensions.
\emph{Deployment intensity} captures how broadly the firm is
used in losing participation. \emph{Cartel proximity} captures
how often its losing bids occur in tenders with direct CADE
defendants. \emph{Market specialization} captures whether
activity is concentrated in narrower product spaces.
\emph{Repeat-pair density} captures whether losses cluster
around the same winner relationships.

Cobidders move in the predicted direction on the first three
dimensions. They participate more often than other frequent
losers, $\valBridgeTendCob$ versus $\valBridgeTendFLnc$
participations (Cohen's $d=\valBridgeTendD$), and face more
unique winners, $\valBridgeUniqWinCob$ versus
$\valBridgeUniqWinFLnc$ ($d=\valBridgeUniqWinD$). They are also
more proximate to legal cartel anchors: $\valBridgeDirectCob$
of their loss-bids face a direct CADE defendant, compared with
$\valBridgeDirectFLnc$ for other frequent losers
($d=\valBridgeDirectD$). Their portfolios are more concentrated
as well, with item-group HHI $\valBridgeHHICob$ against
$\valBridgeHHIFLnc$ ($d=\valBridgeHHID$).

These patterns are inconsistent with a purely generic ``all
frequent losers are the same'' reading. They support the idea
that cobidders occupy a more targeted loser-side exposure
profile than other firms already inside the frequent-loser
stratum. The patterns do not prove cover bidding in any
particular tender, assign cartel membership, or establish
liability; they narrow the mechanical-adjacency alternative
without closing every interpretation.

One measure moves in the wrong direction. Cobidders have a
lower share of own loss-bids in repeat winner-pair clusters
than frequent-loser non-cobidders, $\valBridgeRepeatShareCob$
versus $\valBridgeRepeatShareFLnc$
($d=\valBridgeRepeatShareD$). We do not use that measure as
support for the cover-bidding interpretation. Its wrong sign is
a useful discipline: the cobidder profile is not a sufficient
statistic for cover bidding. The next subsection turns to
bid-level signatures, where the LANCES bid records allow a
complementary check on the same interpretation.

\subsection{Bid-Level Signature}
\label{sec:cobidder_bid}

The LANCES export lets us ask whether the distinction between
cobidders and other frequent losers also appears inside bids.
This evidence is not part of the award-layer screen; it is a
profile check using the richer forensic layer. For firms with
at least five usable losing bids in convite or pregão, we
compute the gap between each losing bid and the winning bid in
the same tender-item,
$(\text{bid}-\text{winning bid})/\text{winning bid}$,
winsorized at $[-0.99,10]$ before firm-level aggregation. The
usable sample contains $\valTBBLnCob$ cobidders and
$\valTBBLnFLnc$ frequent-loser non-cobidders.

The relevant comparison is not whether cobidders submit visibly
uncompetitive bids. In modern cover-bidding environments,
losing bids may need to remain plausible enough to preserve the
appearance of rivalry. A cobidder can therefore bid closer to
the winner than other frequent losers while still losing
systematically. Higher within-firm dispersion in bid gaps is
also consistent with a firm appearing in different losing roles
across tenders. These patterns are informative but not uniquely
diagnostic of cover bidding.

The data move in that direction. Cobidders bid closer to
winners: their median bid gap is $\valTBBLMedGapCob$, compared
with $\valTBBLMedGapFLnc$ for other frequent losers
($d=\valTBBLMedGapD$). At the same time, their within-firm
dispersion in bid gaps is higher, $\valTBBLSDGapCob$ versus
$\valTBBLSDGapFLnc$ ($d=\valTBBLSDGapD$). A logit of cobidder
status within the frequent-loser stratum, controlling for
$\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$, preserves both signs: the
median-gap coefficient is $\valTBBLLogitMedCoef$
($z=\valTBBLLogitMedZ$), and the dispersion coefficient is
$\valTBBLLogitSDCoef$ ($z=\valTBBLLogitSDZ$). The bid-level
distinction therefore survives controlling for participation
intensity.

The bid-level evidence has a limited but useful role. It shows
that the cobidder distinction is not only another way of
measuring high participation; it remains visible inside bids
after the participation channel is held constant. The pattern
is consistent with credible cover bidding and harder to reduce
to mechanical high-volume losing alone. It is also compatible
with some alternative economic mechanisms, including
capacity-constrained bidding strategies and item-level price
uncertainty. The evidence therefore narrows the
mechanical-adjacency story; it does not prove that any specific
bid was cover, establish agreement, or assign cartel
membership.

\subsection{What the Profile Does and Does Not Establish}
\label{sec:cobidder_interpretation}

The profile evidence establishes a limited but important point.
Cobidders are not simply indistinguishable high-volume losers.
Within the frequent-loser stratum, they display greater
deployment breadth, closer proximity to direct CADE defendants,
more concentrated product portfolios, and bid-level patterns
that survive controlling for participation intensity. These
differences make the cobidder label harder to reduce to
mechanical adjacency alone.

The profile does not establish legal or economic type in the
strong sense. It does not prove that any specific bid was
cover, that any cobidder was party to an agreement, or that the
firm belongs to a cartel. It does not supply liability evidence
or a damages base. The evidence is descriptive profile
evidence: it supports the loser-side screening interpretation,
but it does not adjudicate conduct.

The safest reading is therefore narrower than type
identification. The profile is consistent with credible cover
bidding---losing bids that remain plausible enough to preserve
the appearance of rivalry while a firm cycles through cover
roles across tenders \citep{asker2010leniency,marshall2012economics}---and
harder to reconcile with a purely mechanical
high-volume-loser interpretation. It is not uniquely diagnostic
of cover bidding; alternative explanations remain available.

That is enough for the section's role. The profile makes the
cobidder target economically meaningful before the paper turns
to the institutional question: whether an award-layer ranking
can organize the more expensive bid-layer tools used in cartel
forensics. Section~\ref{sec:screening_forensics} takes that
step, asking whether placing the award-layer screen ahead of
bid-layer forensics produces measurable savings in evidentiary
effort without unacceptable losses in recall.
