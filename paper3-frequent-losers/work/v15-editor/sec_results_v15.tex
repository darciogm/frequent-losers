\section{Main Results}
\label{sec:results}

The empirical case for treating frequent losers as an institutional
marker rests on two observations developed in this section. First,
within-item, within-year, within-procuring-unit comparisons
associate the presence of frequent losers with a higher log
negotiated price---positive and significant under every estimator
we deploy, with magnitude bounded by the $\valHeadlineRange$ range
that the cross-fit decomposition recovers as a structural floor.
Second, that price gap is sharply heterogeneous in procuring-unit
size, falling monotonically across quartiles to near zero among the
largest buyers. The first observation establishes that the
construct has a measurable price imprint; the second, that the
imprint is institutionally patterned, with administrative oversight
as the dominant moderating force. We read both as descriptive
associations consistent with the cover-bidding framework of
Online Appendix~A, not as causal estimates---a discipline
we maintain throughout.

\subsection{Within-Item Conditional Price Gap}
\label{sec:results_ols}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}. The presence of at least one frequent
loser is associated with a significantly higher log negotiated unit
price in all four specifications. The general within-item-and-year
estimate is $\valPctSixeight$ (SE clustered by item-year); adding
procuring-unit fixed effects moves it to $\valPctSixfour$, a shift
small enough to be inconsistent with concentration in high-cost
buyers as the operating mechanism. The modal subsamples bracket the
within-PBU estimate at $\valPctNinethree$ in preg\~ao and
$\valPctThreeeight$ in convite. The comparison set is the
$\valSampleN$ tender-items in the same product code × year ×
procuring unit that do not contain frequent losers.

\input{./output/tables/tab_prices}

The natural first question is whether the gap is a regulatory
artifact. Convite tenders in Brazilian procurement law
(Lei~$8.666/93$, Art.\ 22, \S\,3) require a minimum number of
qualified bidders, and procuring units sometimes fill the quorum
with firms that have no realistic chance of winning. If frequent
losers were just statutorily mandated quorum-fillers, their
presence would correlate mechanically with regulatory pressure
rather than with coordinated behavior. The data say otherwise. The
preg\~ao-only subsample ($\valFalPregN$ items) carries no analogous
minimum-bidder rule (Lei~$\valLeiPregao$); within it, the binary
frequent-loser specification yields $\valFalPregBin$
($\valFalPregBinPSig$), versus $\valFalConvBin$
($\valFalConvBinPSig$) in convite ($\valFalConvN$ items)---a ratio
of $\valFalRatio\!\!\times$
(Table~\ref{tab:falsification_modal}).\footnote{The two modal
subsamples sum to slightly less than the full $\valSampleN$-item
panel; the residual covers a small set of items in mixed-modal or
secondary procedure types (concorr\^encia, tomada de pre\c{c}os,
dispensa) that we exclude from the modal falsification but retain
in the pooled estimates of Table~\ref{tab:prices}.} The continuous
loss-intensity specification reproduces the pattern at finer
granularity ($\valFalPregLog$ in preg\~ao versus $\valFalConvLog$
in convite). The price imprint is not produced by the convite
quorum rule; it is, if anything, sharper where the rule is absent.

The conditional association is positive and significant under
every estimator we deploy, and the cross-fit decomposition is
informative rather than fragile. Cross-fit
estimation---defining the frequent-loser list on odd years and
estimating on even, then reversing---yields $\valPctThreeSix$;
applying the odd-year list to the full panel yields
$\valBidConvCoefPct$, midway between $\valPctSixfour$ (full-PBU
OLS) and $\valPctThreeSix$ (clean cross-fit). The OLS estimate
therefore decomposes cleanly into a structural component
($\valPctThreeSix$, recovered by cross-fit) and a within-sample
classification component (about $\valClassNoiseGapPct$ percentage
points, the gap between full-PBU OLS and cross-fit). We adopt
$\valPctThreeSix$ as the conservative floor and treat the resulting
$\valHeadlineRange$ across the four estimators (cross-fit, IPW,
OLS, CEM) as the headline reference in the remainder of the paper.

A leave-one-out instrumental-variable specification confirms that
OLS is conservative. The IV estimate is $\valBidPHigh$ at
$p<0.05$ with first-stage $F=396$---roughly three times the
within-PBU OLS, in the direction expected under classical
attenuation when a binary indicator proxies for an underlying
continuous participation count. Coarsened exact matching yields
$\valBidCoefMean$ on $N=\valCleanItems$ items, and
inverse-probability weighting yields $\valBidNonCV$ on
$N=\valFilteredItems$ items; both bracket the OLS and cross-fit
estimates on the common-support subsample, so the price gap holds
under non-parametric reweighting on observable item, year, and
procuring-unit characteristics.

The \citet{cinelli2020making} robustness value is
$RV_{q=1} = \valRVqOne$: an unobserved confounder would need to
explain at least $\valRVqOne$ of the residual variation in both
frequent-loser presence and log price to drive the coefficient to
zero, a stringent bar given the item-, year-, and
procuring-unit-fixed-effects structure. Under matching restricted
to regions of strict covariate overlap, the conditional coefficient
reverses ($-9.7\%$ overlap-cell ATT, $-30.7\%$ PS-trimmed ATT;
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:item_level_scope_match}).
We read the reversal as informative about selection within the
always-loser stratum: strict-overlap matching restricts comparisons
to the items that frequent losers \emph{would} have entered with
high probability, removing precisely the systematic differences
between selected and unselected items that the broad-sample gap
captures. The two estimates are consistent descriptions of the
same population from different angles, neither carrying causal
weight on its own. The heterogeneity that the broad-sample gap
conceals is the focus of the next subsection.

\subsection{The Procuring-Unit-Size Oversight Gradient}
\label{sec:results_oversight}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The procuring-unit-size gradient is the single strongest piece of
evidence in the paper that the price gap reflects institutional
structure rather than statistical noise. Splitting the sample into
quartiles of procuring-unit size and re-estimating the within-item
specification yields a steep, monotonic gradient:
$\valOversightQonePct$ in the smallest quartile,
$\valOversightQtwoPct$ in the second, $\valOversightQthreePct$ in
the third, and $\valOversightQfourPct$ in the largest, all
significant at conventional levels with standard errors clustered
by item. The $\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ ratio between the
extreme quartiles is the largest source of heterogeneity we find in
the data---larger than the preg\~ao--convite gap, larger than any
cross-sector spread---and is preserved across alternative measures
of buyer size (annual contract volume, cumulative item count,
headcount of distinct purchasers).
Table~\ref{tab:regime_oversight} reports the coefficients.

The framework in Online Appendix~A predicts this gradient
directly. Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m} gives
$\partial m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$: where the procuring unit's
principal cost of detection $\theta_k$ is lower, the cartel's
optimal cover-bidder deployment rate is higher, because lower
$\theta_k$ simultaneously raises the marginal expected payoff per
cover bid and lowers the marginal expected detection cost.
Procuring-unit size is not identical to detection cost, but the
empirical association between buyer size and oversight
infrastructure is a well-documented feature of decentralized
procurement systems
\citep{decarolis2017corruption,coviello2017tenure,oecd2019government}:
large state agencies and state-level secretariats employ dedicated
procurement staff, electronic monitoring systems, and internal-audit
units that small municipalities and decentralized purchasing units
typically lack. Read through this institutional mapping, the
monotonic gradient is what the framework predicts: where oversight
is thinner, the participation footprint we identify exhibits a
larger conditional price gap.

Three features distinguish the gradient from a mechanical artifact:
monotonicity across all four quartiles, a magnitude beyond what
fixed-effect noise typically produces, and a direction matching
$\partial m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:optimal_m}).
Two structural alternatives---thinner item-PBU cells in small
procuring units, and systematically different bidder pools or item
compositions across buyer sizes---could in principle deliver a
gradient absent any oversight mechanism, and we treat them as
scope rather than as competing readings: neither generates the
direction and monotonicity we observe without an oversight channel.
The configuration is what cover bidding under endogenous monitoring
predicts, and among the descriptive alternatives we examine,
oversight is the channel that matches the framework most directly.

The gradient is the central piece of evidence behind the
cartel-screen framing of the paper. The price imprint of the
construct is not a uniform artifact of the data envelope; it is
patterned in a way that institutional structure---specifically, the
gradient of oversight infrastructure across buyer sizes---directly
produces. The cover-bidding framework, the conservative empirical
scope, and the administrative pathway proposed in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} all rest on this finding.
Section~\ref{sec:mechanisms} reads the heterogeneity through the
framework's mechanism predictions, and
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} stress-tests both the price gap and
the gradient against threshold variants, alternative clustering,
sensitivity bounds, and strict-overlap matching.
