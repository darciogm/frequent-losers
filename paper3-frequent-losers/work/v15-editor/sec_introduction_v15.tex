\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Brazilian electronic procurement runs on a peculiar pattern:
thousands of firms register, prepare bids, show up auction after
auction, and never win. In São Paulo's Bolsa Eletrônica de
Compras (BEC, $\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$), $\valAlwaysLosers$
firms bid without ever winning a tender-item, and $\valFL$ of
them participated in more than $\valThresholdStat$ tender-items.
We call these firms \emph{frequent losers}, and we treat the
regularity they form as the central object of the paper. The
pattern is hard to rationalize: bid preparation is costly, and
the auctions these firms attend are open, electronic, and largely
commodity-based. Under standard expected-profit competition,
firms with persistent zero wins should exit. They do not. We read
this footprint as the empirical face of cover bidding in a
separating equilibrium, and we show that contract-award records
alone are enough to use it as a cartel screen.

Bid-rigging in public procurement is among the most consequential
and least successful areas of competition enforcement. The
diagnostic tools developed in the empirical literature since
\citet{porter1993detection}---bid-distribution tests, structural
estimation of cost markups, machine-learning detectors trained on
bid patterns
\citep{bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine}---require
bid-level microdata that few jurisdictions audit at scale. What
procurement systems universally preserve, instead, is the
contract-award record: who won, who participated, what was bought,
at what price. If a participation-only construct can identify
institutionally meaningful behavior in this thinner data envelope,
oversight bodies can act on what they already have.

The frequent-loser construct meets this test in a way that is more
than statistical noise. Within-item, within-year,
within-procuring-unit comparisons across $\valSampleN$ items
associate environments containing flagged firms with a
$\valHeadlineRange$ higher log negotiated unit price across
estimators (full-sample OLS, PBU fixed effects, IPW, CEM,
cross-fit). The association is sharply heterogeneous in
procuring-unit size: the price gap falls monotonically from
$\valOversightQonePct$ in the smallest quartile to
$\valOversightQfourPct$ in the largest---a
$\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ gradient that is the strongest
source of heterogeneity in the data, larger than the
preg\~ao--convite gap and larger than any cross-sector spread. The
configuration is consistent with the comparative static
$\partial m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$: where oversight is thinner,
cover-bidder deployment intensifies.

We organize these patterns through a separating-equilibrium
framework with cover bidders and genuine entrants
(Online Appendix~A). The framework yields two results that
ground the construct: $\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ is a sufficient
ranking statistic for cover-bidder type given award-record data
(Proposition~\ref{prop:exposure_stmt}), and the bright-line filter
$\text{wins}=0$ is the equilibrium choice of cover bidders themselves
(Lemma~\ref{lem:wins_zero_stmt}). It is an organizing device, not a
source of causal identification, but it delivers the comparative
static $\partial m^*/\partial \theta_k < 0$ that the
$\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ oversight gradient instantiates.

Identification is descriptive by design. Two design-based
strategies (a sharp regression discontinuity at the
convite/preg\~ao statutory caps; a difference-in-differences using
the 2018 cap raise) return null at the available bandwidths, and
we treat the empirical content as a descriptive association rather
than as a causal estimate. Within this scope, the construct
discriminates $\valCobidders$ adjudicated CADE-cobidder firms
inside the always-loser stratum at AUC $\valAUCFLfirm$ ($95\%$ CI
$\valAUCFLfirmCI$)---a strong within-stratum result. It does not
flag $\valDirectCADE$ direct CADE defendants in the broader BEC
firm universe (AUC $\valAUCdirectStd$), by design: direct
defendants in adjudicated cases are typically frequent winners,
while the construct targets the loser side of the cartel
population. The frequent-loser construct therefore operates as a
cartel screen for the cover-bidder population; competing readings
of the same participation footprint (capacity-transfer motives,
market intelligence) are observationally compatible at the firm
level and discussed in §\ref{sec:limitations}.

The administrative pathway built on the construct has three stages.
\emph{Screen} computes $\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ on award
records and flags top-$k$ environments by tender-item count; the
deployable binary rule (more than $\valThresholdStat$ tender-items,
zero wins) is the information-coarsening of this statistic.
\emph{Triage} cross-references flagged firms with adjudication
records (CADE, CGU, TCE) and operational priors of the oversight
body. \emph{Forensic} deploys bid-distribution methods where bid
microdata are available
\citep{imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine}. The construct
narrows the case-load that reaches the forensic stage; it does not
replace forensic analysis. Portability rests on three structural
features---commodity-heavy item composition, electronic platforms
with publicly auditable participant lists, and institutional
asymmetry between the winner and loser sides of the bidding
pool---shared by Brazilian state platforms and broadly present in
jurisdictions where bid microdata are not centrally collected.

The paper makes three contributions. First, the construct is
\emph{deployable}: we translate it into a three-stage administrative
pathway---Screen, Triage, Forensic---computable on contract-award
records alone, at near-zero data cost, in jurisdictions where bid
microdata are not centrally collected. Second, we document an
empirical regularity---the within-item conditional price premium
across estimators and its $\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$
procuring-unit-size oversight gradient---and show that administrative
oversight is the dominant moderating force in the data. Third, the
construct is \emph{theoretically grounded}: we derive it from a
separating-equilibrium framework with cover bidders and genuine
entrants (Online Appendix~A), under which
$\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ is a sufficient ranking statistic for
cover-bidder type given award-record data, and the bright-line filter
$\text{wins}=0$ is the equilibrium choice of cover bidders
themselves. The remainder of the paper proceeds in four blocks. Sections
\ref{sec:literature}--\ref{sec:empirical} situate the construct in
the bid-rigging-detection literature, set out the institutional and
legal framework, describe the BEC data, and state the descriptive
empirical strategy alongside the two design-based strategies that
return null. Section~\ref{sec:cade} reports the CADE consistency
check; Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:mechanisms} present the
within-item price associations, the procuring-unit-size gradient,
and mechanism heterogeneity. Section~\ref{sec:robustness} collects
the robustness work---matching, cutoff sensitivity, the horse race
against continuous loss intensity, and operational metrics---and
Sections~\ref{sec:limitations}--\ref{sec:conclusion} catalog scope
limitations and conclude.
