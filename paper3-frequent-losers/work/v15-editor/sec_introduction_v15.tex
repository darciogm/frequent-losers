\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Brazilian electronic procurement has a peculiar feature: thousands
of firms register, prepare bids, show up auction after auction, and
never win. In São Paulo's Bolsa Eletrônica de Compras (BEC,
$\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$), $\valAlwaysLosers$ firms bid
without ever winning a tender-item, and $\valFL$ of them
participated in more than $\valThresholdStat$ tender-items. We call
these firms \emph{frequent losers}. The pattern is hard to
rationalize under expected-profit competition once bid preparation
costs are accounted for, and is broadly consistent with cover
bidding on the loser side of bid-rigging arrangements; we do not,
however, claim that the construct identifies cover bidders or
detects cartels.

Bid-rigging detection in the empirical literature relies on bid
microdata that few jurisdictions audit at scale
\citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine}.
The data envelope that procurement systems universally preserve is
much thinner: contract-award records, with winner identity,
participant identity, item identifier, and negotiated price. The
question we ask is whether \emph{this} envelope alone carries
exploitable signal for triage. Our answer is conservative: yes, but
narrowly. Loser-side participation patterns associate with both
higher negotiated prices and cartel-adjacent ground truth at rates
above random matching, but the association is unstable across
scopes and the validation object is indirect. The
\emph{participation-based screen} we build is useful for
prioritizing procurement environments for oversight, not for
adjudicating membership in any specific cartel.

The paper makes three claims, in order of empirical strength.
First, frequent-loser presence on contract-award records contains
usable signal: within-item, within-year, within-procuring-unit
comparisons
across $\valSampleN$ tender-items associate frequent-loser presence
with a $\valHeadlineRange$ higher log negotiated unit price across
five estimators. Second, the signal is associated with
cartel-adjacent validation targets: the construct discriminates
$\valCobidders$ adjudicated cobidder firms inside the always-loser
stratum at AUC $\valAUCFLfirm$ in-sample, $0.864$ under temporal
holdout (train $2009$--$2016$, test $2017$--$2019$), and $0.748$
under a stricter contemporaneous benchmark restricted to four
pre-2020 cases. Cobidders are firms that participate alongside
adjudicated cartel members; this is an indirect label of cartel
adjacency, not legal proof of cartel membership. Against the
$\valDirectCADE$ direct CADE defendants in the broader BEC firm
universe, the construct discriminates at AUC $\valAUCdirectStd$, by
design: direct defendants are typically frequent winners and lie
outside the population the screen targets. Third, the signal is
heterogeneous across buyer size: the conditional price gap falls
from $\valOversightQonePct$ in the smallest procuring-unit-size
quartile to $\valOversightQfourPct$ in the largest, a
$\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ ratio. This is consistent with
thinner institutional capacity moderating the imprint at smaller
buyers, but buyer size proxies for several correlated institutional
features (procurement-officer tenure, internal-audit infrastructure,
item composition) and we do not separately identify oversight as
the operative channel.

The paper's most important interpretive challenge is that the
broad-sample price association reverses sign under strict-overlap
matching: $-9.7\%$ on overlap-cell ATT, $-30.7\%$ under
propensity-score trimming. We treat this as a first-order finding,
not a robustness footnote. The broad-sample estimate captures the
combined effect of (i) the within-cell association where
frequent-loser-present and -absent items genuinely overlap and
(ii) the systematic differences between items frequent losers
\emph{select into} and items they do not. Strict-overlap matching
isolates (i); the broad-sample estimate also includes (ii). Both
are valid descriptive scopes, but the screen's signal is partly
about selection. The operational implication is concrete: the
participation-based screen prioritizes environments where
frequent-loser presence is patterned, not environments where the
price gap is causally attributable to that presence. A triage
tool does not need to identify a stable causal effect; it needs
to flag environments at higher prior risk than random selection.
Within that scope, the screen is informative.

We organize the construct through a separating-equilibrium
framework with cover bidders and genuine entrants
(Online Appendix~A). The framework rationalizes why
$\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ functions as a sufficient ranking
statistic and why a $\text{wins}=0$ filter is the equilibrium choice
of the cover-bidder type given the data envelope. The framework is
an organizing device, not a source of identification. The continuous
loss-intensity statistic is the primitive; the binary
frequent-loser rule is its operational simplification. The horse
race in §\ref{sec:mech_horse_race} confirms that the continuous
score discriminates more sharply than the binary cutoff (DeLong
$p = \valDeLongP$); we report the binary rule because it maps onto
existing oversight workflows, not because it is theoretically
privileged.

\paragraph{What this paper does and does not do}
The participation-based screen flags procurement environments
statistically associated with higher prices and cartel-adjacent
validation targets. It is not a cartel detector, it does not
adjudicate firm-level guilt, and it does not identify a stable
causal effect of cover bidding on prices. The screen is designed
for triage: it prioritizes environments for oversight bodies
operating without bid microdata. Where bid microdata are
available, the screen integrates with bid-distribution detectors
(\S\ref{sec:rob_imhof}) rather than substituting for them.

The remainder of the paper proceeds in four blocks.
Sections~\ref{sec:literature}--\ref{sec:empirical} situate the
contribution, set out the institutional and legal framework,
describe the BEC data, and state the descriptive empirical strategy
alongside the design-based strategies that return null.
Section~\ref{sec:cade} reports the validation against CADE
adjudications, with explicit object discipline distinguishing
direct defendants, cobidders, and cartel adjacency.
Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:mechanisms} present the
within-item price association, the buyer-size heterogeneity, and
the mechanism evidence we read with caution. Section~\ref{sec:robustness}
collects the robustness work, including the strict-overlap
matching exercise that anchors §\ref{sec:results_ols}, and
Sections~\ref{sec:limitations}--\ref{sec:conclusion} catalog scope
limitations and conclude.
