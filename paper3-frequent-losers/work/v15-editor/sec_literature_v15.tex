\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

Four literatures bear on the screening problem, each constrained
by data or by identification timing: bid-coordination tests
require the cartel partition as input; bid-distribution screens
require bid microdata; cover-bidding identification has operated
retrospectively; and the developing-economy literature flags the
resource gap without closing it. The construct's positioning is
at the conjunction---not as a more powerful test than any of
these, but as a screen that operates where they cannot and
integrates with them where they apply (§\ref{sec:rob_imhof}).

\paragraph{Bid-coordination tests require the partition as input}
\citet{bajari2003deciding} establish the canonical framework for
detecting collusion through exchangeability of bid functions and
conditional independence of residuals, conditional on a
researcher-supplied partition between suspected colluders and a
competitive fringe. The papers that build on Bajari--Ye inherit
the constraint: \citet{porter1993detection} and
\citet{porter1999ohio} detect bid rotation conditional on assumed
cartel membership; \citet{baldwin1997bidder} use post-prosecution
records to identify timber-auction colluders;
\citet{conley2016detecting} infer bidder groups from co-bidding
patterns---the closest precedent in data parsimony, recovering
group structure rather than individual flags;
\citet{clark2021collusion} and \citet{schurter2020identification}
study cartel dynamics with ex-post-known membership. The
partition is constructed \emph{ex post} or by assumption in
every case. The frequent-loser construct supplies a candidate
\emph{ex ante} firm-level flag computable from contract-award
records, which bid-coordination tests can then take as input
where microdata exist.

\paragraph{Proactive bid-distribution screens require microdata}
\citet{imhof2019detecting,imhof2018screening,huber2019machine}
and \citet{wallimann2023machine} train machine-learning
classifiers on tender-level bid statistics
(within-tender coefficient of variation, kurtosis, skewness,
normalized spread), reaching AUC above $0.85$ against
ground-truth European cartel cases.
\citet{abrantesmetz2006a} pioneer the variance screen;
\citet{chassang2022robust} provide moment-based theoretical
foundations; \citet{harrington2008detecting} surveys the field.
Every implementation operates at the tender level and requires
per-bidder bid amounts. Electronic procurement platforms,
including BEC during much of our sample, archive winners and
participant lists but not bid-by-bid microdata. The
frequent-loser construct runs on the contract-award envelope
that survives this archiving, and integrates with bid-distribution
screens where microdata are available (§\ref{sec:rob_imhof}).

\paragraph{Cover-bidding identification has been retrospective}
\citet{porter1999ohio,pesendorfer2000study,asker2010leniency},
and \citet{marshall2012economics} document cover bidders in
prosecuted cartels via case records (Ohio milk, Italian highway,
NYC stamps, and the cases reviewed by Marshall and Marx);
\citet{athey2011comparing} compare cover-bidding incentives
across auction formats theoretically. In every empirical case
the cover bidders are identified \emph{ex post}, through leniency
testimony, post-conviction records, or judicial discovery. We
are not aware of a published prospective identification of
cover-bidder candidates from public award-record data. The
construct flags firms whose participation patterns are coherent
with cover bidding, shifting the post-adjudication observation
into a pre-investigation screen.

\paragraph{Procurement institutions shape what screens can
observe} \citet{coviello2017tenure} show that longer political
tenure worsens procurement outcomes in Italy;
\citet{decarolis2020rules} show that discretionary procedures and
limits to competition jointly predict corruption risk; and
\citet{decarolis2017corruption} documents the
procurement-officer channel. These papers do not propose
participation-only screens, but they motivate the
procuring-unit-size gradient we report in
§\ref{sec:results_oversight}: the participation footprint the
construct flags is amplified where institutional capacity is
thinner.

\paragraph{Procurement enforcement is hardest where data are
scarcest} The bid-rigging literature is dominated by
developed-economy settings with mature data infrastructure,
where procurement spending represents $5$--$10\%$ of GDP. In
developing economies, where the share can exceed $\valPctTwelve$
of GDP \citep{oecd2019government} and oversight resources are
thinner, settings are simultaneously more vulnerable to bid
rigging and less equipped to run bid-distribution detectors.
\citet{sanchezgraells2019screening} surveys the operational
limits of existing screens in low-resource jurisdictions. The
construct generalizes wherever a procurement system records
winners and participants electronically, regardless of bid-level
archiving (§\ref{sec:lim_external}).

\paragraph{Synthesis} Each of the four literatures requires more
information than typical oversight bodies routinely have, or
operates only after collusion is established. The frequent-loser
construct addresses the conjunction: it supplies a candidate
flag that bid-coordination tests can take as input, runs on the
contract-award envelope that bid-distribution screens cannot
use, recasts cover-bidding identification as prospective, and
deploys in low-resource settings. It is not a test that
dominates any of the four benchmarks; it is a tool that runs
where they cannot and integrates with them where they apply.
