\begin{abstract}
Can we screen for bid-rigging cartels in jurisdictions where bid
microdata are not centrally collected? We document a peculiar
pattern in public-procurement records from the State of São
Paulo, Brazil ($\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$), that suggests we
can. Of $\valAlwaysLosers$ firms that bid without ever winning,
$\valFL$ participated in $\valThresholdStat$ or more
tender-items---a population we call \emph{frequent losers}. Their
behavior is hard to square with expected-profit competition and
reads naturally as the cover-bidder side of bid-rigging
arrangements. Within-item, within-year, within-procuring-unit
comparisons across $\valSampleN$ items associate frequent-loser
presence with a $\valHeadlineRange$ higher negotiated unit price
across estimators. The price gap shrinks as buyers grow---from
$\valOversightQonePct$ in the smallest procuring-unit-size
quartile to $\valOversightQfourPct$ in the largest---a
$\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ ratio that is the strongest
source of heterogeneity, larger than the preg\~ao--convite gap.
We read the pattern through a separating-equilibrium framework
with cover bidders and genuine entrants. Against CADE's
adjudicated cartel portfolio, the construct discriminates
cobidder firms at AUC $0.864$ under temporal holdout.
Identification is descriptive by design. Built from
contract-award records, the construct translates into a
three-stage administrative pathway---Screen, Triage,
Forensic---deployable where bid microdata are absent.
\end{abstract}
