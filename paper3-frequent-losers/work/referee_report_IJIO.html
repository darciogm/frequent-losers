Referee Report

Journal: International Journal of Industrial Organization (IJIO)
Paper: Frequent Losers as Cover Bidders in Public Procurement
Authors: Darcio Genicolo-Martins & Paulo Furquim de Azevedo (INSPER)
Date: 2026-03-14
Major Revision

1. Summary

This paper proposes “frequent losers” (FL)—firms with a zero win rate that participate in an abnormally large number of public procurement tenders—as a firm-level screening marker for cover bidding in procurement auctions. Using 4.5 million tender-items and 40 million bids from São Paulo’s BEC platform (2009–2019), the authors identify 2,735 FL firms via a median + 1.5×IQR threshold on always-loser participation counts. The paper tests two hypotheses: FL presence is a useful screening marker (H1) and FL firms are themselves cover bidders (H2).

The empirical toolkit includes: (i) OLS with high-dimensional FE showing 4–9% higher prices in FL-present tenders; (ii) a leave-one-out IV yielding a 21% price markup (F = 396); (iii) CADE cartel validation (FL firms 3.5× more likely to co-participate with convicted cartelists); (iv) Bajari–Ye exchangeability and conditional-independence tests; (v) co-bidding network heterogeneity; and (vi) a staggered Callaway–Sant’Anna DiD. A welfare analysis bounds losses at R$734M–R$2.4B.

2. Overall Assessment

This paper tackles a genuine gap: the absence of a firm-level screen operable without bid-level data. The FL concept is operationally simple and the dataset is exceptionally large. The intellectual ambition—deploying OLS, IV, Bajari–Ye, networks, and DiD—is commendable.

However, the paper’s most important problem is that none of its identification strategies conclusively establishes the causal mechanism (H2), and several produce results that contradict the cover bidding interpretation when examined closely. The LOO IV is fragile across FE structures (0.194 → 3.64). The Bajari–Ye tests, under tender FE, show FL pairs with lower residual correlation than non-FL pairs. The network split reveals zero price effect for “high-suspicion” FL firms. The DiD is underpowered with a near-zero ATT. Individually, each can be rationalized; collectively, they suggest H2 should be reframed as suggestive. The H1 contribution (FL as screening tool) is solid and sufficient for IJIO if properly framed.

3. Major Comments

[M1] The LOO instrument is fragile and the exclusion restriction is not credibly defended

Problem: The LOO instrument has a partial R² of 0.0002–0.0006 (Table 3). Despite F = 396, the instrument explains essentially none of FL-presence variation. When FE tighten to item-group×year (Table B.8, Panel C), 2SLS explodes from 0.194 to 3.64—an implausible 38× markup. The balance test (Table B.2) shows all four observables significantly predicted by the instrument (all p ≤ 0.013), suggesting systematic exclusion-restriction violation.

Location: Section 5.2, Eq. 3, Tables 3 and B.8.

Why it matters: An IV with near-zero partial R² that produces wildly different estimates across FE structures does not meet IJIO’s standard for credible identification.

Suggested fix: (a) Compute implied bias from balance-test imbalance per Goldsmith-Pinkham et al. (2020). (b) Bartik decomposition to identify driving product markets. (c) Time-lagged placebo instrument (FL supply in t−2). (d) Consider anchoring the paper on OLS + sensitivity analysis and relegating IV to supplementary evidence.

[M2] Bajari–Ye tender-FE results contradict the cover bidding interpretation

Problem: Under tender FE (Table B.12), FL pairwise product (0.38) is lower than non-FL (0.86)—the opposite of Prediction 5. The fake-groups placebo (5.80) exceeds the FL product (5.16) under baseline FE. The paper’s rationalization (“genuine bidders share cost information”) is post-hoc and untested.

Location: Table 5 (Panel C), Table B.12, Section 6.3.

Why it matters: The Bajari–Ye framework is a standard IJIO tool. If results don’t survive the correct specification, the paper cannot claim them as evidence for cover bidding.

Suggested fix: (a) Acknowledge that tender-FE results fail to support cover bidding. (b) Report both specifications side-by-side in the main text. (c) Discuss cost-heterogeneity as an alternative explanation for exchangeability failure.

[M3] Bajari–Ye first stage is underspecified

Problem: The auxiliary bid regression (Table B.10) uses only firm size (porte) plus item+year FE. No reference price, distance, firm age, capacity, or CNAE sector. R² = 0.77 is driven by item FE. Residuals capture cost heterogeneity, not strategic deviations.

Location: Table B.10, Section 5.4.

Suggested fix: (a) Add all available firm-level controls (CNAE, age, municipality). (b) Add reference price as regressor—its omission biases residuals. (c) Report R² with and without item FE. (d) Consider bid/reference-price as DV.

[M4] Network split produces internally inconsistent results

Problem: Table 7 shows “high-suspicion” FL firms (high HHI, repeat partners)—the profile most consistent with cover bidding—have a negative price coefficient (−0.018). The entire effect comes from “low-suspicion” FL (0.126). The paper relabels groups as “concentrated/competitive-market” but Table 7’s header still reads “FL Suspicion Level.”

Location: Table 7, Section 6.4, Table B.13.

Suggested fix: (a) Fix table header. (b) Acknowledge ambiguity: pattern is consistent with cover bidding in competitive markets and with non-collusive selection. (c) Test observable firm characteristics across the two FL subgroups. (d) Note that the continuous interaction (Table B.13, Col. 1) is not significant.

[M5] Welfare analysis is unreliable

Problem: OLS is labeled “lower bound” (valid only under measurement-error attenuation, not if OLS has positive OVB). IV is labeled “upper bound” (conflates attenuation with LATE heterogeneity). The “High-Suspicion FL” column shows negative welfare loss (R$−88M)—undiscussed in the text.

Location: Section 8.5, Table B.5.

Suggested fix: (a) Reframe as back-of-the-envelope. (b) Discuss the negative estimate. (c) Derive sensitivity-adjusted bound using Cinelli–Hazlett RV. (d) Acknowledge exclusion of GE effects.

[M6] DiD contributes nothing—move entirely to appendix

Problem: C&S ATT = 0.014 (SE = 0.040) for log prices—effectively zero. MDE (0.078) > OLS benchmark (0.064). Treatment timing is endogenous (cartels choose when to deploy FL).

Location: Section 8.4, Appendix C.

Suggested fix: One-sentence mention in main text; full details in online appendix only.

[M7] Massive selection problem in descriptive statistics not adequately addressed

Problem: FL-present tenders have mean price R$140,673 vs. R$12,465 for non-FL (11.3×). In logs: 4.92 vs. 2.49. Even with item FE, this gap raises concerns about within-item-group selection into fundamentally different tenders.

Location: Table 1, Section 4.3.

Suggested fix: (a) Report FE-residualized conditional means. (b) Show within-item overlap of FL and non-FL price distributions. (c) Give greater prominence to item×year FE spec (Table B.4, Col. 4: 0.074).

[M8] FL not benchmarked against existing screens

Problem: The paper claims FL complements bid-level screens but never compares detection performance. With 40M bids available, standard screen statistics (bid CV, variance, kurtosis) are computable.

Location: Section 2, Section 9.

Suggested fix: (a) Compute Imhof-style tender-level screens. (b) Report confusion matrix: FL-flagged vs. screen-flagged. (c) Horse-race regression: does FL persist after controlling for bid-level screen indicators?

4. Minor Comments

  1. Section 3, Eq. 1: Comparative static m* increasing in n requires unstated curvature assumptions on π(m,n).
  2. Table 1: Report within-item-FE conditional means alongside raw means. The unconditional 11× gap is misleading.
  3. Table 3: Column headers are unclear about FE structure. Clarify in headers, not just bottom rows.
  4. Section 4.2 footnote: The Tukey rule is designed for skewed distributions (uses Q3). Justify the median-based variant on economic, not statistical, grounds.
  5. Table 5: Report the number of tenders with ≥2 FL bids used in pairwise product calculations.
  6. Table 7 header: Still reads “FL Suspicion Level” but text uses “concentrated/competitive-market.” Reconcile.
  7. References: Missing IJIO-relevant citations: Wallimann, Huber & Imhof (2023); Clark, Houde & Kastl (2021); Schurter (2020).
  8. Section 7.2 (M2): Reference-price calibration could reflect item composition, not cartel strategy. Control for item-level price-to-reference dispersion.
  9. Source files: ~15 “% CO-AUTHOR EDIT:” LaTeX comments visible. Remove before submission.
  10. Section 8.1: Monotonically increasing coefficient with stricter thresholds is consistent with the screen working and with more extreme firms in more unusual markets. Discuss both.
  11. Figure 2: Annotate the threshold line with its numerical value (~14 participations).
  12. Table B.5: The negative “High-Suspicion FL” welfare estimate is not discussed anywhere.
  13. Section 5.2: Three threats to the exclusion restriction are compressed into one paragraph with no formal analysis. Each warrants its own subsection.
  14. Abstract: Clearly label “4–9%” as OLS and “21%” as IV/LATE to prevent reader conflation.

5. Specific Comments on Identification

5.1 OLS with High-Dimensional FE (H1)

The OLS baseline (Eq. 1, Table 2) is the paper’s most credible result. The coefficient of 0.064 with PBU FE represents within-item, within-year, within-PBU price differences.

Strengths: (a) Stable across specs (0.064–0.094). (b) Item×year FE yields 0.074. (c) Matching (CEM 0.077, IPW 0.055) brackets OLS. (d) Cinelli–Hazlett RVq=1 = 17.5% is stringent.

Weaknesses: (a) Reference-price control (Table B.4, Col. 3) produces −0.031. Calling reference price a “bad control” requires assuming FL manipulates it—testable but untested. (b) The 11× unconditional price gap means selection within item groups may not be fully absorbed.

Assessment: OLS credibly supports H1. Sufficient for the paper’s core contribution.

5.2 Leave-One-Out IV (H2)

First stage: Partial R² = 0.0002. The 2SLS is identified off <0.02% of residual variation. This is extremely thin.

Exclusion restriction concerns:

  1. Correlated cartel organization: Cartels deploying FL firms coordinate across PBUs. The LOO instrument captures coordination, not exogenous supply. All four balance-test observables are significantly predicted.
  2. Specification sensitivity: Panel A: 0.194; Panel C: 3.64 (18.7× increase). Identifying variation in Panel A comes from cross-market-year comparisons absorbed by Panel C.
  3. Placebo IV: Sub-threshold placebo has F = 33 (>10) yet produces implausible 2SLS of 5.24. If a “placebo” instrument above the weak-IV threshold produces implausible results, the main instrument’s 0.194 may also be amplified.

Assessment: IV results are fragile. Do not meet IJIO standard for credible causal identification.

5.3 Bajari–Ye Tests

Exchangeability: KS D = 0.15 (p < 0.001) with 28M observations. Modest effect; could reflect cost heterogeneity rather than strategic coordination.

Conditional independence: Baseline: FL = 5.16, non-FL = 2.21, fake-groups = 5.80. Fake groups > FL—random grouping produces more apparent coordination. Under tender FE: FL (0.38) < non-FL (0.86)—contradicts Prediction 5.

Assessment: Detects heterogeneity but does not establish strategic coordination. Under the correct specification, results contradict cover bidding.

5.4 Staggered DiD

C&S ATT = 0.014 (SE = 0.040). MDE = 0.078 > OLS benchmark 0.064. Treatment timing is endogenous. Uninformative.

5.5 Mechanism Tests

M1 (competitive displacement): positive genuine-bidder coefficient consistent with strategic selection or FL attraction to competitive tenders. M2 (reference-price calibration): could reflect item composition. M3 (reverse causality): negligible coefficient—convincing.

Missing alternative explanations: financial distress, learning-by-doing, geographic distance, minimum-bidder compliance (PBU officials inviting firms to meet convite threshold). None tested.

6. Assessment of Contribution

Contribution 1: FL as a Screening Marker (H1)

Assessment: Solid

Operationally simple, firm-level, data-parsimonious. Validated against CADE and robust across specifications. Genuine advance over tender-level screens in data requirements. However, incremental detection value over existing screens is asserted, not demonstrated (see M8). Conley & Decarolis (2016) detect bidder groups from co-bidding patterns without bid values—closer to FL than acknowledged.

Contribution 2: Causal Evidence for Cover Bidding (H2)

Assessment: Overstated

IV is fragile. Bajari–Ye reverses under tender FE. Network split shows zero effect for “most suspicious” FL firms. DiD is uninformative. The cumulative weight of evidence is weaker than the paper’s framing. Should be reframed as “suggestive evidence consistent with cover bidding, among other interpretations.”

Contribution 3: Bajari–Ye Applied to FL Classification

Assessment: Marginal

Reasonable application of an existing framework with a new partition. But the partition’s validity depends on FL firms being cover bidders—precisely what the tests are supposed to establish. Reasoning is circular unless independently validated (CADE partially provides this).

Overall Novelty

The FL concept is genuinely new as a named, operationalized screen. It is not a deep conceptual advance: it amounts to flagging zero-win, high-participation firms as outliers. The theoretical model does not derive FL from equilibrium. The contribution is primarily empirical and applied—appropriate for IJIO, but framing should be calibrated accordingly.

7. Conclusion & Recommendation

I recommend Major Revision. The paper introduces a novel and potentially useful screening tool, validated against a large dataset with rich institutional variation. The H1 contribution is solid and policy-relevant. However, the causal identification (H2) does not meet IJIO’s standard: the IV is fragile, the Bajari–Ye results are weaker than claimed, the network split is internally inconsistent, and the DiD is uninformative.

Three conditions for acceptance at IJIO:

  1. Reframe H2 as suggestive. Downgrade cover bidding mechanism from “substantial evidence” to “consistent evidence among multiple interpretations.” Move IV to supplementary analysis; anchor paper on OLS + sensitivity. Present Bajari–Ye tender-FE reversal in the main text.
  2. Benchmark FL against existing screens. Compute Imhof-style statistics. Show incremental predictive power of FL above bid-level screens, or demonstrate the data-constraint setting where FL is the only feasible tool.
  3. Address the network-split inconsistency. Provide a compelling theoretical account of why “most suspicious” FL firms show no price effect and “least suspicious” firms drive the entire effect, or acknowledge ambiguity and discuss alternative interpretations.

Meeting these conditions would make the paper a strong contribution to IJIO.
