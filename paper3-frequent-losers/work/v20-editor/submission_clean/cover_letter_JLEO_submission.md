Dear Editors,

We submit "Cheap Signals, Costly Proof: Award-Layer Evidence Triage in Cartel Enforcement" for consideration at the Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization. The paper studies an enforcement-design problem that arises before legal proof exists: how should an agency allocate costly forensic attention when routine contract-award records are available but bid-level evidence must be recovered through more expensive case-specific processes?

The paper's contribution is to frame procurement-cartel screening as evidence allocation under incomplete observability. Existing bid-distribution screens are valuable once bid microdata are available. We study the prior institutional stage: whether the cheaper award layer can rank where bid-layer forensics should begin. The distinction is legal as well as empirical. Award records can create forensic priority, but they cannot establish agreement, liability, or damages.

The empirical setting is Sao Paulo's BEC procurement platform from 2009 to 2019, linked to CADE procurement-cartel adjudications. We construct a frequent-loser ranking from award records alone: among zero-win firms, persistent participation ranks loser-side cartel-adjacency risk. The validation target is deliberately limited. We evaluate the ranking against adjudication-anchored cobidders, meaning always-loser firms observed alongside direct CADE defendants, rather than treating the statistic as a cartel-membership label.

The paper also treats validation as a set of threats rather than as a single performance statistic. The ranking is disciplined with participation-volume placebos, exposure adjustment over comparable opportunity sets, temporal holdouts, leakage checks, and a direct-defendant scope check. These exercises are designed to ask whether the statistic is useful for prioritizing forensic attention, not whether it can prove cartel participation.

The main operational result is a sequential gatekeeper. The award-layer ranking adds information to bid-distribution forensics, but the two layers play different institutional roles. Used before bid recovery, the award-layer ranking reduces the bid-microdata pool for the forensic stage by 83% while recovering 131 of 193 adjudicated cobidders. Legal liability remains in the richer evidentiary record, where bid-level conduct, communications, leniency material, and case-specific facts can be evaluated.

We believe the paper fits JLEO because its central object is not a classifier but an enforcement institution. The legal-economic issue is how suspicion, investigation, and proof should be sequenced when information is costly. The paper speaks to the organization of antitrust enforcement, the boundary between administrative suspicion and legal proof, and institutional design under costly observability.

The manuscript does not claim to identify cartel members, estimate damages, or replace bid-layer forensics. It proposes and evaluates a legally limited triage architecture: cheap administrative records discipline where proof-producing investigation should begin, while adjudication remains grounded in richer case evidence.
