Major Revision

Referee Report — RAND Journal of Economics

Paper: Frequent Losers as Cover Bidders in Public Procurement (v6)

Expertise: Cartels, bid rigging, procurement auctions, antitrust screening

Date: 2026-03-15

1. Summary

This paper proposes “frequent losers” (FL)—firms that never win yet participate in an abnormally large number of public procurement tenders—as a screening marker for cover bidding. Version 6 adds a structural model of cover-bidder deployment (Section 3), a formal detection validation with ROC analysis (AUC = 0.94), and a reorganized empirical strategy that elevates structural estimation and detection performance as primary contributions while demoting the LOO IV to a bracketing device. Using 4.5M tender-items and 40M bids from São Paulo’s BEC platform (2009–2019), the authors identify 2,735 FL firms. FL-present tenders exhibit 6.4% higher negotiated prices (OLS). The structural model derives equilibrium cover-bid distributions under two regimes, predicts that cover bidding concentrates in competitive markets, and provides a likelihood framework for separating cover bids from genuine bids. Bajari–Ye tests (corrected first stage) reject exchangeability and conditional independence for FL residuals. CADE validation yields AUC = 0.94.

2. Overall Assessment

The v6 reframe is a genuine improvement. The structural model derives the market-selection prediction endogenously. The ROC analysis (AUC = 0.94) demonstrates the FL screen’s effectiveness against actual enforcement outcomes. The corrected Bajari–Ye (excluding n_bids) and honest IV reframing are welcome.

However, significant gaps remain. The structural model is specified but not estimated: the Results section presents only v4 reduced-form estimates, not structural parameters. The paper has three identities—structural IO, reduced-form screening, detection methodology—and does not fully integrate them. A RAND paper must deliver on its structural promises.

Recommendation: Major Revision with a clear path to acceptance conditional on completing the structural estimation and integrating results throughout.

3. Major Comments

[M1] The structural model is not estimated

Problem: Section 3 develops a structural model; Section 5.1 describes MLE. But Section 7 reports only OLS/IV results—no structural parameter table, no BIC comparison, no implied markup, no bootstrap SEs. The structural model exists as theory and methodology but not as estimated output.

What would resolve it: Estimate the mixture model. Report σg, δ, σc, BIC(R1), BIC(R2), KS distances, implied markup (%), and bootstrap SEs. Verify the structural markup falls within OLS–IV range (6.4%–21%).

[M2] Results section disconnected from new empirical strategy

Problem: Section 7 follows the v4 ordering (OLS → IV → network → Bajari–Ye), not the v6 strategy (structural → reduced-form → detection → Bajari–Ye). No section presents structural estimation results or the ROC analysis (AUC = 0.94).

What would resolve it: Restructure Section 7: 7.1 Structural Results, 7.2 Reduced-Form, 7.3 Detection Performance, 7.4 Network Heterogeneity, 7.5 Bajari–Ye, 7.6 Regime Test. Each subsection should open with the relevant prediction from Section 3.

[M3] Structural proofs are heuristic, not formal

Problem: Proofs for Propositions 1–4 are economic arguments, not formal derivations. The profit function π(b*, m, n) is unspecified; comparative statics are asserted on an undefined function. The entropy argument for Regime 1 holds against entropy-based screens but not variance screens.

What would resolve it: Either specify π explicitly and derive formally, or rename Section 3 to “Framework” and relabel proofs as “Arguments.” Option (b) is faster and does not weaken the paper if the structural estimation delivers.

[M4] Supervised mixture ≠ structural model in the RAND sense

Problem: The mixture model conditions on known FL labels. It characterizes bid distributions conditional on a pre-assigned label, but does not identify cover bidders from bid data. RAND structural papers (Bajari–Ye, Chassang–Ortner) identify behavior without pre-labeled groups.

What would resolve it: Estimate without FL labels (latent mixture). If posterior classification correlates with FL, this validates the screen structurally. Alternatively, explicitly state the model characterizes distributions conditional on the FL screen and adjust claims.

[M5] ROC ground truth is imperfect

Problem: (a) Co-participation ≠ collusion. (b) Base rate 0.58% → precision 3.6% (96.4% false positives). (c) Temporal split described in Section 4.5 but not implemented in ROC script.

What would resolve it: Report precision-recall curve. Implement temporal split. Vary ground truth stringency (≥1, ≥3, ≥5 co-bids). Report confusion matrix.

[M6] Convite vs. pregão tension with structural prediction

Problem: Model predicts more cover bidding under convite (minimum-bidder constraint), but the price effect is larger under pregão (9.3% vs 3.8%). The model should distinguish quantity of cover bidders (higher under convite) from price impact per cover bidder (higher under pregão).

[M7] Welfare analysis does not use structural parameters

Problem: Welfare uses reduced-form coefficients, not the structural markup from the mixture model. After completing M1, compute structural welfare bounds.

5. Identification Deep-Dive

5.1 Structural Identification

The supervised mixture is identified by construction given FL labels. However, if some FL firms are genuinely incompetent, the estimated cover-bid distribution is contaminated, biasing the structural markup toward zero. The paper should estimate the structural model separately for competitive-market FL (significant price effect) and concentrated-market FL (zero effect).

5.2 OLS

The 0.064 estimate with RV = 17.5% (Cinelli–Hazlett) is the paper’s most credible result. Sufficient for H1 (screening marker).

5.3 IV

Reframing as a bracketing device is appropriate. Balance test acknowledgment is honest. No further work needed.

5.4 Bajari–Ye

n_bids exclusion confirmed correct in actual code. KS = 0.151 (p < 0.001). Fake-groups reframing is improved. Tender-FE reversal should be discussed in context of structural regime prediction.

5.5 Detection

AUC = 0.94 is excellent. Optimal threshold (1.45×) validates the 1.5× rule. Main limitations: ground truth imprecision and low precision (3.6%). Address per M5.

7. Literature Positioning

Bajari & Ye (2003): FL provides a principled partition for the Bajari–Ye tests (vs ad hoc groups). Corrected first stage (no n_bids) is a methodological contribution.

Conley & Decarolis (2016): FL is simpler (participation counts vs co-bidding matrices) but less general. Direct comparison on BEC data would strengthen the paper.

Chassang & Ortner (2022): FL is not “robust” in their sense. ROC analysis partially addresses this by characterizing operating characteristics.

Incremental contribution: Structural theory + validated detection (AUC=0.94) + market-selection insight. This combination is publishable at RAND if structural estimation is completed.

8. Conclusion and Conditions

The paper has the ingredients for RAND: structural model, rich data, AUC = 0.94 detection validation, novel market-selection finding. The v6 reframe is substantial. But the structural model must be estimated, the Results section must be restructured, and the detection validation must be strengthened.

Three conditions for acceptance:

  1. Complete structural estimation (M1): parameter table, BIC, implied markup, bootstrap SEs.
  2. Restructure Results (M2): align with Section 5 ordering; lead with structural results.
  3. Strengthen detection validation (M5): precision-recall, temporal split, confusion matrix.

Conditional acceptance probability: 50–60%.

If structural estimation fails: pivot to Detection Tool framing (AUC = 0.94 + market-selection heterogeneity). Lower but viable probability (~25–30%).
