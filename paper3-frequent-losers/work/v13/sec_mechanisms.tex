\section{Heterogeneity, Coherence Diagnostics, and Alternative Readings}
\label{sec:mechanisms}

The within-item association of Section~\ref{sec:results}
documents that loser-side concentration tracks higher
negotiated prices conditional on item, year, and procuring
unit. The descriptive association does not identify a
mechanism, and we do not claim it does. This section reports
five coherence diagnostics that constrain---rather than
discriminate---the readings the price gap leaves open. We
state up front that none of the diagnostics carries the load
of a structural mechanism test, that the heterogeneity pattern
across network primitives does not match the cell
configurations classical cartel-rotation models predict, and
that at least one cell returns a coefficient with the opposite
sign of the headline. The screen earns its place
operationally; the mechanism evidence below clarifies what
\emph{cannot} be claimed about what the screen flags.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity along network primitives.}
We split tender-items into cells defined by procuring unit
$\times$ item-group $\times$ year, and within each cell compute
two network primitives: winner-side concentration (HHI on
winner shares) and the count of repeated FL--winner pairs (the
same FL firm appearing alongside the same winner in $\geq 2$
items within the cell). Splitting on the medians of the two
primitives produces a $2 \times 2$ partition. Re-running the
within-item price specification of
Section~\ref{sec:results} separately on each cell, the
coefficient pattern is the following.\footnote{Specification:
$\log p = \alpha + \beta \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{loser-side concentrated}] + \gamma \cdot \text{convite} + \mu_{\text{item}} + \tau_{\text{year}} + \mu_{\text{PBU}} + \varepsilon$, item-clustered SEs.
Sample harmonized to the v13 main panel ($N \approx 1.65\text{M}$).
Full output in Online \ref{app:robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:unified_mechanism}.}
\emph{Low HHI $\times$ Low pairs} (the modal cell, $N =
\valMechQLLLN$): $+\valMechQLLLCoef\%$ ($p < 10^{-4}$). \emph{High HHI
$\times$ Low pairs} ($N = \valMechQLHLN$): $+\valMechQLHLCoef\%$ ($p =
0.075$). \emph{Low HHI $\times$ High pairs} ($N = \valMechQLLHN$):
$+\valMechQLLHCoef\%$ ($p = 0.18$, not statistically distinguishable from
zero). \emph{High HHI $\times$ High pairs} ($N = \valMechQLHHN$):
$\valMechQLHHCoef\%$ ($p = 0.002$).

The pattern does not discipline a single mechanism reading.
The cell that classical cartel-rotation models would tag as
the cleanest cartel signature---low winner concentration with
many repeated FL--winner pairs---returns a null coefficient.
The cell that delivers the largest premium is the modal cell
(low HHI, low pairs), in which neither concentration nor
pair persistence flags a coordination structure; this is also
the cell carrying $\valPctEighty$ of the sample, so its weight in
the headline coefficient is mechanical. The smallest cell
returns a \emph{negative} and statistically significant
coefficient: $\valMechQLHHCoef\%$ ($p = 0.002$). We read this anomaly
parsimoniously as selection rather than a substantive sign
flip. Cells with both high winner concentration and a
high count of repeated FL--winner pairs are the most visible
configuration to an oversight body; if such cells are over-represented
among environments already under CADE scrutiny or where firms
have updated bidding behavior in anticipation of investigation,
loser-side concentration would no longer covary with inflated
prices in those cells. The selection reading is consistent
with the absence of any positive coefficient in the high-HHI
$\times$ high-pairs cell across modalities (Online
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:unified_mechanism},
columns by convite/preg\~ao). We do not have an instrument
that distinguishes selection from a substantively negative
treatment effect in this cell, and we do not claim one. We
flag the anomaly because hiding it would be a referee's first
finding.

The remaining diagnostics (M1--M5) are coherence checks
against specific competing readings of the headline
association. They do not identify the mechanism; jointly with
the descriptive association, they narrow the set of readings
the data are consistent with.

\paragraph{M1: FL presence does not crowd out genuine
competitors.} If loser-side concentration reflected
displacement of real bidders, FL-present tender-items should
attract \emph{fewer} non-FL participants. They attract more:
$\valMechMOneCoef$ non-FL firms ($p < 0.01$), under the FE specification
of Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}. The pattern is consistent
with cover bidding (which adds participation without
displacing real competitors) and with FL self-selection into
inherently competitive items (which does not require any
coordinated reading). The diagnostic is one-sided: it rules
out one specific competing story (displacement of real
bidders), not the broader class of selection stories.

\paragraph{M2: Winners in FL-present items bid closer to the
public reference price.} BEC reference prices are publicly
posted before bidding opens. Regressing
$\log(p_{\text{negot}} / p_{\text{ref}})$ on FL presence
yields $\valMechMTwoCoef$ ($p < 0.01$). Winners in FL-present items bid
$\valPctFourRef$ closer to the visible anchor. The pattern is
consistent with calibrated coordination
\citep{marshall2012economics} and equally consistent with FL
participation being concentrated in items where reference
prices are unusually accurate (better-specified items, items
in markets with more competitive history, items where
reference-price audits are stricter). M2 is a coherence check;
it does not identify which channel is operative.

\paragraph{M3: Reverse causality through price-chasing is
quantitatively too small to account for the headline.}
Regressing FL entry on within-market lagged prices yields a
slope of $\valMechMThreeCoef$ (SE $\valMechMThreeSE$): a 10\% price increase raises
FL entry by 0.02 percentage points, against a \valCartelMarkup baseline.
For reverse causality to generate the headline associaton, the
elasticity would need to be roughly thirty times larger than
the estimate. M3 does not rule out selection on contemporaneous
market characteristics outside the FE structure; the
\citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity bound of $RV_{q=1} =
\valRVqOne$ (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) addresses that residual
threat without identifying it.

\paragraph{M4: FL firms form repeated winner pairings beyond
chance.} Under a stratified permutation test ($\valPermPilot$
iterations, preserving participation-count quartiles within
the always-loser pool), FL firms form $\valDyadicPairsObs$ unique pairs
with the same winner across $\geq 5$ shared items, against a
permuted mean of $\valDyadicPairsPerm$ ($p < 0.001$). Top-10 winner-FL
pairs average $\valDyadicTopTenObs$ co-occurrences against a permuted $\valDyadicTopTenPerm$
($p = 0.016$). The test rejects the null that FL--winner
adjacency is a random feature of always-loser participation.
What it cannot do: distinguish persistent coordination from
geographic or product-market co-location. The test is one of
several inputs to the broader risk-prioritization claim, not a
mechanism identifier
(Table~\ref{tab:dyadic_permutation}, \ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{M5: Genuine firms in FL-exposed markets exit BEC
\emph{less} frequently.} A Cox proportional-hazard model on
non-FL firm exit yields a hazard ratio of $\valMechMFiveHR$ ($p < 0.01$)
on FL-exposure intensity, after controlling for participation
volume and win rate
(Table~\ref{tab:cox_survival}, \ref{app:robustness}). Two
readings fit equally well: FL-exposed markets attract
sustained participation because they are more profitable, or
because they sustain a stable mixed pool of cover and
non-cover bidders. The PH assumption is rejected
($p < 0.001$), so the hazard estimate is descriptive rather
than structural; we report it because, jointly with M1, it
contradicts displacement-style readings of the price gap. It
does not identify the alternative.

\paragraph{First-tender behavioral test.} We attempted a
behavioral test in which we restrict the always-loser sample
to each firm's earliest valid-priced tender-item and ask
whether FL firms post systematically different bids than
non-FL always-losers at first appearance. On the unconditional
sample, FL firms bid $\approx 10\%$ above the item winner
($p = 0.02$, $N = \valFtFlNanalysis$).\footnote{Reported sample is
trimmed at $|\log(b/w)| \leq 5$ to drop bid-entry errors. On
the untrimmed sample the unconditional coefficient is
$\valFLpremTwentyPct$, dominated by a small number of extreme outliers
that survive in both treatment and control; we report the
trimmed sample throughout.} Coarsened-exact-matching on
item-group, year, modality, and log-winner-bid quartiles
attenuates the coefficient to $+\valFTcemCoef$ ($p = 0.076$);
propensity-score 1-NN matching on the same covariates
attenuates further to $+\valFTpsCoef$ ($p = 0.31$). We treat the
first-tender pattern as a sample-conditioned descriptive
statistic that does not survive matching on observable item
characteristics, and we do not interpret it as evidence of
behavioral selection by FL firms; we report it because the
attenuation pattern is itself informative for what the screen
identifies. Full output in Online
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}, Table~\ref{tab:matched_first_time}.

\paragraph{Rationality of sustained zero-win participation.}
\label{sec:rationality}
Even with no mechanism claim, the population the screen
identifies has a participation pattern that is not
self-evidently rational. With zero wins, expected profit per
bid is $E[\pi] = 0 \cdot \bar{V} \cdot m - c = -c < 0$ for any
bidding cost $c > 0$. BEC is electronic; $c$ captures staff
time for bid preparation and documentation compliance (1--2
hours at \valBidCostLow--\valBidCostLow/hour), yielding $c \in
[\text{R\$}50, \text{R\$}500]$. At the FL threshold (14
tenders), cumulative direct cost is R\$$700$--R\$$\valFLCostUp$,
conceivably explainable by persistent bad luck or learning. At
the 90th-percentile FL participation ($50+$ tenders),
cumulative direct cost reaches
R\$$\valFLCostMid$--R\$$\valFLCostMax$.\footnote{A Bayesian learner with
a uniform prior updates
$\Pr(\text{win}) = (0+1)/(n+2)$ after $n$ zero-win trials.
After $n = 50$, $\Pr(\text{win}) = 0.019$; with $\bar{V} =
$~R\$$\valDataItemAvgPay$ and $m = 0.10$, expected gain per bid is
$0.019 \times \valDataItemAvgPay \times 0.10 = $~R\$$163$, below the
lower-bound bidding cost. Exit is rational even for the most
optimistic learner.} Win rate zero in BEC does not preclude
wins outside the platform, so participation may be rational
for firms profitable elsewhere; what the rationality
calculation establishes is that BEC participation past the FL
threshold is unlikely to be standalone-optimal, which is the
condition the screen exploits
\citep{asker2010leniency, marshall2012economics}
(Table~\ref{tab:rationality}).

\paragraph{Strategic adaptation.}
If a cartel were to read the paper, the obvious responses---rotating
FL firms across years, allowing occasional wins, staying
below the IQR-derived threshold---would attenuate the screen.
The threshold sensitivity in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} confirms the construct's
fragility under such adaptations: the coefficient weakens as
the cutoff rises, and relaxing the zero-win condition to
low-win-rate firms attenuates the price association
substantially
(Table~\ref{tab:fl_lowwinrate}, \ref{app:online_robustness}).
The continuous loss-intensity score
($\log(1 + \text{tenders})$) is more robust than any specific
binary cutoff but inherits the same vulnerability to deliberate
participation-pattern adaptation. A deploying oversight body
would need to update the implementation periodically and
combine the screen with bid-level tools where microdata are
available---the cat-and-mouse pattern that applies to every
proactive screen \citep{harrington2008detecting}. We discuss
the implementation in Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.
