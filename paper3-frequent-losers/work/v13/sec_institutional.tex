\section{Legal and Institutional Framework}
\label{sec:institutional}

\paragraph{Procurement statutes.}
Brazilian public procurement during our 2009--2019 sample is
governed by Lei~\valLeiOriginal (the General Procurement Statute,
enacted to implement Article~37 of the 1988 Constitution) and
Lei \valLeiPregao (Pregão). Two modalities dominate BEC.
\emph{Convite} (Article~22 of Lei 8.666) is a sealed-bid
invitation procedure: the buyer invites at least three suppliers
from the qualified-supplier registry, all proposals are
submitted in writing within a 5-business-day window, and the
contract is awarded to the qualifying lowest bid. The statute
requires three valid proposals for the procedure to clear; if
fewer appear, the buyer must either re-issue the call or
document the absence of bidders. \emph{Pregão} (Lei 10.520) is
a reverse electronic auction: bidders submit progressively lower
offers in real time until no firm improves the standing best
offer. Pregão has no minimum-bidder requirement. Statutory value
caps (Lei 8.666 Article~23) restrict convite to procurement
below \valCapOldR for goods and services and \valCapMidR{,}000 for
engineering works during 2009--2017; Decreto \valDecreto raised
these caps to \valCapNewR and \valCapHighR{,}000 respectively. Above
the caps, only pregão (or higher modalities not used in BEC) is
permitted.

The structural difference between the two modalities is central
to our analysis. The convite minimum-bidder rule was introduced
to ensure procedural competition; in practice, when a buyer's
short-list yields fewer than three motivated bidders, additional
proposals must be obtained from the registry. The competition-economics
literature
\citep{marshall2012economics, asker2010leniency,
porter1993detection} identifies this configuration---a rule
mandating $\underline{n}$ bidders without a corresponding rule
that those bidders genuinely compete---as the canonical setting
for \emph{regulatory cover bidding}: designated firms submit
deliberately losing bids to satisfy the procedural requirement
while preserving the rents of the chosen winner. The
configuration is not unique to Brazil. Analogous minimum-bidder
provisions appear in EU public-procurement directives (Directive
2014/24/EU Article~26 on open and restricted procedures),
US small-business procurement (FAR 13.106-2 simplified
acquisition thresholds), and OECD procurement guidelines
\citep{oecd2019government}; the screen we develop applies
wherever the configuration recurs.

The 2021 Brazilian reform (Lei 14.133/2021) replaces Lei 8.666
for new contracts, restricts the convite modality, and
tightens habilitation requirements (mandatory federal
qualified-supplier registry, integrity programs above a value
threshold). The reform postdates our sample but shapes the
policy relevance of the FL screen: even where statutory
incentives for cover bidding are reduced, residual minimum-bidder
provisions in subnational systems and the analogous provisions
in OECD procurement regimes preserve the incentive structure
the screen exploits. Quantifying the reform's effect on FL
prevalence is an open empirical question we flag in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\paragraph{Enforcement architecture.}
Three institutions police BEC procurement.
\textbf{Tribunal de Contas do Estado de S\~ao Paulo} (TCE-SP)
audits purchasing units administratively, with binding power to
require correction of irregularities, fine officials, and report
findings to prosecutorial bodies; in 2023, TCE-SP processed
over \valPBUcount administrative procurement cases at first
instance. \textbf{Controladoria Geral do Estado} (CGE) oversees
executive-branch compliance with procurement law and integrity
programs, including the cadastros of debarred firms. The
\textbf{Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econ\^omica} (CADE)
is the federal competition authority; it investigates and
adjudicates cartel and bid-rigging cases under Lei 12.529/2011,
imposing administrative fines of \valAdminCost--\valPctTwenty of the
infringing firm's gross revenue in the year preceding the
investigation, and may refer cases to the Public Prosecutor's
Office for criminal prosecution under Lei 8.137/90 (Article~3,
prison sentences of two to five years for bid rigging).
CADE's leniency program (Lei 12.529/2011 Article~86) grants
immunity to the first cooperator and reduced fines to subsequent
ones, generating the disclosure trail on which much of the
cartel-detection literature relies for ground truth.

CADE's procurement-cartel portfolio during our window comprises
12 adjudicated cases covering 65 firm-defendants, with final
decisions spanning 2015--2025 (cases in our portfolio range
from \emph{Cartel dos trens-metr\^os} adjudicated 2019-07-08 to
\emph{Transporte escolar} adjudicated 2025-02-26). Eight of the
twelve cases were decided after our 2019 sample cut, so portions
of our external validation are prospective: 2009--2019 FL
classification anticipates firms whose cartel involvement
enforcement confirmed years later. The interval between
operational cartel activity and final CADE adjudication---the
cases in our portfolio span 4--16 years from process opening to
ruling---is the key benchmark for the policy claim that
screening at the buyer level can compress detection lead time.
A screen that flags suspicious environments in real time, even
imperfectly, dominates a process that takes a decade to deliver
verdicts.

\paragraph{The screening problem.}
Bid-level forensic tools---variance, kurtosis, and spread
analyses across submitted bids---detect coordinated bidding
when the required microdata are preserved
\citep{imhof2019detecting, huber2019machine,
wallimann2023machine}. The information-theoretic logic of these
tools is unimpeachable; their practical reach is bounded by
data infrastructure. Many electronic procurement systems,
including BEC during much of our sample, archive only winning
prices and participant lists at the contract-record layer:
bid-by-bid records may exist in operational logs but are not
always exported into the analytical warehouses oversight bodies
query.\footnote{In TCE-SP audits we examined, requesting
bid-by-bid microdata from the BEC operational system is a
multi-step administrative process that typically delays
investigations by weeks or months; the contract-record layer
queried directly produces results in minutes.} The data
asymmetry is not specific to Brazil. EU member-state platforms
preserve bid microdata heterogeneously; US federal procurement
(FPDS-NG) preserves contract awards but not bid history at the
contracting-officer-accessible layer; subnational systems are
typically thinner still. A screen that requires only
participation records lowers the data threshold for proactive
detection to the level virtually every system maintains. That
is the screen's primary contribution to the law-and-economics
of procurement enforcement: not a more powerful test, but a
test deployable where the more powerful tests are not.
