\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report results in five blocks. Section~\ref{sec:class_diagnostics}
verifies that the FL classification is not a threshold artifact.
Section~\ref{sec:results_ols} presents the conditional-association
baselines (OLS, cross-fit, matching) and the IV measurement-error
diagnostic. Section~\ref{sec:results_detection} reports detection
performance against CADE ground truth. Section~\ref{sec:cross_sector}
documents cross-sector replication. Section~\ref{sec:additional_id},
embedded after the supporting diagnostics, reports the
institutional-identification estimates. The headline conditional
range is 3.6--7.7\%; the institutional design (\S\ref{sec:additional_id})
identifies a 7.6\% premium under voluntary cover bidding within
convite, attenuating to negative under the binding constraint.

% ── 7.0 Classification Diagnostics ──────────────────────────────

\subsection{Classification Diagnostics}
\label{sec:class_diagnostics}

Three checks establish that the FL classification is not a
threshold artifact. \emph{Sharpness of the zero-win cutoff}: relaxing
the win-rate condition to 1--2\% attenuates the price coefficient
substantially (Table~\ref{tab:fl_lowwinrate},
\ref{app:online_robustness}); the bright line at zero is doing real
work. \emph{Continuous benchmark}: regressing log price on the log
of the maximum always-loser participation count in the tender
yields 0.022 (SE 0.005, $p < 0.01$) per log-point;\footnote{The
continuous measure correlates moderately with the number of
always-losers in the tender ($r = 0.33$); using the tender mean
rather than the max reduces $r$ to 0.15 and yields qualitatively
similar results.} at the FL threshold ($\log 14 \approx 2.64$) the
continuous prediction is $5.8\%$, close to the binary OLS of
$6.4\%$, indicating that the binary coefficient reflects the
underlying continuous relationship evaluated at the threshold
rather than a cutoff artifact. \emph{Permutation placebo}:
randomly reassigning the FL indicator across tenders while
preserving the 4.8\% treatment rate produces coefficients centered
at zero (mean 0.001, SD 0.003 across 20 replications); no
permutation reaches 0.064. The FL--price association reflects the
specific allocation of treatment, not chance.

% ── 7.1 Reduced-Form Evidence ──────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Conditional-Association Baselines}
\label{sec:results_ols}

\paragraph{OLS, cross-fit, matching, IV: a 3.6--7.7\% range.}
Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports the OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}. FL presence is associated with
significantly higher log negotiated prices across all four
specifications: $6.8\%$ general, $6.4\%$ with PBU fixed effects,
$9.3\%$ for preg\~ao, and $3.8\%$ for convite. PBU fixed effects
barely move the coefficient, ruling out the alternative that FL
presence proxies for high-cost purchasing units; the unrestricted
sample including items that never attract FL firms yields a
coefficient within 0.5 percentage points of the baseline,
ruling out the alternative that the item-type restriction
inflates the estimate.

\input{./output/tables/tab_prices}

FL-present tenders contain $0.19$ log points more non-FL firms
under the PBU FE specification, contradicting the crowding-out
prediction of legitimate-FL-as-competitor and consistent with
\emph{strategic selection}---FL firms gravitate toward more
competitive tenders, the regime-2 prediction of
\ref{app:proofs}. The pattern is also consistent with the
non-collusive alternative that FL firms self-select into
inherently competitive tenders for orthogonal reasons; the
institutional identification of \S\ref{sec:additional_id}
separates the two.

\paragraph{Cross-fit estimate.}
FL status is defined using the same 2009--2019 data on which the price
regression is estimated, potentially creating a mechanical link between
classification and outcomes. To address this, we define FL using only odd years and estimate on
even years, and vice versa (FL incidence is similar across odd and
even years---5.1\% vs.\ 4.5\%---so the temporal split is
approximately exchangeable). The cross-fit
average is $\hat{\beta}_{\text{CF}} = 0.036$ (3.6\%), attenuated by
44\% relative to full-sample OLS. The attenuation reflects a
combination of fewer FL firms per subsample
(1,885--2,153 vs.\ 2,735), classification noise near the threshold,
and---most importantly---the elimination of any within-sample
mechanical correlation between the FL rule and prices.

\paragraph{Cross-fit and decomposition.}
FL is defined on the same 2009--2019 data on which the price
regression runs, raising the concern of a mechanical
within-sample link between classification and outcomes. We
address it by splitting the sample by parity: define FL on odd
years, estimate on even years, and reverse. FL incidence is
$5.1\%$ in odd years and $4.5\%$ in even years, so the temporal
split is approximately exchangeable. The cross-fit average is
$\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{CF}} = 0.036$ ($3.6\%$), attenuated by $44\%$
relative to full-sample OLS. To decompose the attenuation, we
apply the odd-year FL list to the \emph{full} sample (all
years): the coefficient is $0.043$ (SE $0.019$), roughly midway
between full-sample OLS ($0.064$) and cross-fit ($0.036$). About
two-thirds of the drop reflects classification noise (the
odd-year list has fewer firms and is noisier near the threshold);
the remaining third comes from restricting estimation to the
half-sample. The decomposition is not statistically
distinguishable from baseline OLS ($p \approx 0.45$),\footnote{The
difference $0.064 - 0.043 = 0.021$ has approximate SE
$\sqrt{0.019^2 + 0.020^2} \approx 0.028$, $t \approx 0.75$.} so
the attenuation is largely noise. We retain the headline range
3.6--6.4\% with cross-fit removing the mechanical link and OLS
providing the full-sample baseline.

\paragraph{Matching and IV diagnostic.}
Coarsened exact matching (CEM: $0.077$, $N = 969{,}751$) and
inverse probability weighting (IPW: $0.055$, $N = 830{,}194$)
bracket the OLS and cross-fit estimates: the price gap holds
under non-parametric reweighting on observables. The
leave-one-out IV estimate ($0.194$, $p = 0.01$, $F = 396$)
exceeds OLS by a factor of three, as expected under
measurement-error attenuation in the binary FL indicator. The
instrument may, however, capture cartel intensity if cartels
operate across PBUs within the same product market; we treat
the IV as a measurement-error diagnostic indicating OLS is
conservative, not as a primary estimator
(Table~\ref{tab:iv_main}, \ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Summary.}
The four conditional-association approaches---cross-fit
($3.6\%$), IPW ($5.5\%$), OLS ($6.4\%$), CEM ($7.7\%$)---fall
in a $3.6$--$7.7\%$ range. All rest on selection on observables
plus fixed effects; the cross-fit additionally eliminates any
mechanical FL--price link. That estimates cluster rather than
scatter shows the association does not hinge on a particular
specification. The IV ($19.4\%$) is off the headline range
because exclusion-restriction concerns
(\S\ref{sec:reduced_form}) keep it diagnostic.
\citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity yields $RV_{q=1} = 17.5\%$:
an unobserved confounder would need to explain $17.5\%$ of
residual variation in both FL presence and log prices to drive
the coefficient to zero---a high bar given the FE structure.

% ── 7.2 Detection Performance (H1 validation) ───────────────────

\subsection{Detection Performance}
\label{sec:results_detection}

\paragraph{AUC = 0.94 against CADE; bid-level alternative is 0.79.}
The FL screen evaluated against the 193 BEC always-losers who
co-participate with CADE-defendants achieves AUC $= 0.9389$
(95\% DeLong CI $[0.932, 0.946]$; stratified percentile and BCa
bootstraps with $B = 5{,}000$ and $2{,}000$ agree to three
decimals; Figure~\ref{fig:roc}, Table~\ref{tab:roc_detection},
\ref{app:robustness}). The data-driven optimal threshold (Youden's
$J = 0.84$) corresponds to a multiplier of 1.45$\times$IQR, close
to the 1.5$\times$ baseline rule we set \emph{a priori},
achieving TPR $= 1.00$ at FPR $= 0.15$. The 15-point AUC gap
against an \citet{imhof2019detecting}-style bid-level composite
(within-tender CV, kurtosis, skewness, normalized spread; AUC
$= 0.79$, DeLong $p < 0.001$;
Table~\ref{tab:imhof_comparison}, Figure~\ref{fig:roc_comparison},
\ref{app:robustness}) is the headline detection finding.

Three caveats apply but do not erase the gap. \emph{(i)} The
firm-level CADE-co-bidding ground truth favors firm-level
screens that operate over many tenders over tender-level screens
that operate one tender at a time. \emph{(ii)} Our Imhof
implementation uses tender statistics aggregated to firm-level
means rather than the trained classifier in
\citet{huber2019machine}; the latter likely closes part of the
gap. \emph{(iii)} The participation-count score may
mechanically inflate co-participation opportunities; the
permutation test of \S\ref{sec:cade} controls for this by
stratifying on participation quartile and confirms the FL--cartel
association ($3.5\times$, $p < 0.001$). Precision is $7.1\%$
at a $1.1\%$ base rate---FL is a high-recall triage tool, not a
standalone classifier.

A logistic-regression combiner using both screens together
($z_{\mathrm{FL}}$ plus four Imhof $z$-features) reaches AUC
$= 0.84$ on held-out 5-fold cross-validation: above any
individual Imhof feature, below FL alone. Imhof features add
noise relative to the participation signal in this setting.
The two screens are complementary in the horse-race
specification of Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}: adding an
Imhof-style CV flag raises the FL coefficient from $0.064$ to
$0.084$ ($p < 0.01$), a suppression effect---Imhof-flagged
tenders have lower FL incidence, creating negative
omitted-variable bias in the FL-only regression. The Imhof
flag itself enters at $0.021$ ($p < 0.01$); the correlation
between indicators is $0.060$. The suppression is coherent
with Regime~2 coordinated cover bidding: low within-tender
dispersion evades CV-based screens while sustained
participation triggers FL (Table~\ref{tab:horse_race},
\ref{app:robustness}).

\subsection{Network-Split Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:results_network}

Table~\ref{tab:network_split} splits FL firms into
concentrated-market and competitive-market subgroups based on
co-bidding network metrics (Section~\ref{sec:empirical_network}).\footnote{Markets
above the winner-HHI sample median are classified as concentrated;
firms sharing at least two tenders are classified as repeat
co-participants.}
Competitive-market FL firms---operating in markets with diversified
winner patterns (low HHI)---drive the entire price effect: 0.126
($p < 0.01$, PBU FE). Concentrated-market FL firms show no significant
effect ($-0.018$, $p > 0.3$).

The pattern matches the standard cover-bidding prediction that the
returns to manufactured competition are highest where genuine
competition is otherwise active---but it is not the only reading. Cartels in
concentrated markets may already sustain high prices through market
power, making cover bidders redundant insurance rather than active
price inflators. Or the FL label may be noisier in concentrated
markets, where some flagged firms are genuinely unsuccessful entrants.
Or thin cells in concentrated item markets may simply absorb the
effect into the fixed effects. With cross-sectional data we cannot
tell these stories apart, and we regard the concentrated-market null as
an open puzzle. What is clear is that the FL marker bites hardest in
competitive markets---which, from a detection standpoint, is where it
is needed most.

\subsection{Bajari--Ye Tests}
\label{sec:results_bajari}

The price effect concentrates in competitive markets; the next
question is whether FL bids come from a different data-generating
process. The \citet{bajari2003deciding} framework provides two
tests on first-stage residuals from the bid equation (full setup
in \ref{app:structural_id}, Online):
\emph{exchangeability}, the KS test rejects the null that FL and
non-FL residuals share a distribution ($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$);
\emph{conditional independence}, the mean pairwise product of FL
residuals is $4.28$ ($t = 81.0$, $p < 0.001$), with bootstrap
FL--non-FL difference excluding zero. Both reject the null in the
direction predicted by coordinated bidding. Enriching the
first stage with firm age and CNAE-sector dummies alongside firm
size leaves first-stage $R^2$ unchanged at $0.770$ and does not
alter either test, reducing the concern that exchangeability
violations are driven by omitted firm-level cost shifters
(Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_corrected}, \ref{app:robustness}).

Under tender fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_tender_fe},
\ref{app:robustness}) both pairwise products drop sharply
(FL: $5.16 \to 0.38$; non-FL: $2.21 \to 0.86$), with the FL
product falling below non-FL---a reversal coherent with
Regime~2 since cover bids cluster near $b^* + \epsilon$ with
low variance and removing the tender mean eliminates the shared
focal-point component. The tender-FE specification absorbs the
variation cover bidding generates; the reversal is mechanically
expected but limits power to distinguish cover bidding from
common cost shocks.

\subsection{Cross-Sector Replication}
\label{sec:cross_sector}

A natural concern with single-setting estimates is that the
headline AUC and price coefficients aggregate over heterogeneous
product markets. We re-estimate both detection performance and
the price specification separately by BEC item sector
(two-digit prefix of the item code; 15 sectors with $N > 50{,}000$
tenders each; Table~\ref{tab:cross_sector} in
\ref{app:robustness}).

Detection generalizes uniformly. All ten sectors with at least
three CADE-defendant positives among their always-loser pool
yield AUC $\geq 0.90$, with mean $0.954$ (SD $0.034$). The
participation-based screen does not depend on a particular
product category: it discriminates CADE co-bidders from other
always-losers across food, paper, equipment, and services with
near-uniform accuracy.

The price coefficient is more heterogeneous. Of fifteen
within-sector OLS estimates, ten are positive (consistent with
the headline 6.4\%) and four are statistically significant at
the 5\% level. Two sectors (37 and 39) yield negative coefficients
significant at $5\%$ (around $-5\%$); we read these as boundary
cases where FL classification within a thin product market may
capture legitimately marginal entrants rather than cover bidders,
matching the environment-marker reading we develop in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The largest positive coefficient ($+25.5\%$, Sector 17) appears in
the BEC internal item classification, not the CNAE-economic
classification, and inverse mapping disconfirms a tight match to
any single CADE-adjudicated cartel.%
\footnote{The BEC item-code 2-digit prefix used to construct
Table~\ref{tab:cross_sector} is an internal classification, not
CNAE. We construct an alternative cross-sector regression using
the modal CNAE 2-digit division of an item's bidders---recovering
eleven CNAE divisions for seven cartels (46 atacado covers
medicamentos/trens/aquecedores; 47 varejo covers sacos-de-lixo,
Op.\ Colludium; 56 alimentação covers merenda-escolar and
cafeteria-aeroporto; 21 farmacêuticos; 27, 28, 30, 33, 42, 43, 81
miscellaneous industrial). The CNAE-conditional FL price
coefficient is not concentrated in CADE-matched divisions: in
divisions 46 and 47, where matched cartels operate, FL coefficients
are $-0.010$ and $-0.014$ respectively. The cross-sector
heterogeneity in Table~\ref{tab:cross_sector} therefore reflects
internal BEC item-classification heterogeneity, not a clean
sector-cartel mapping. Detection performance (AUC) does generalize
across both classifications; the price coefficient does not align
with the cartel-sector decomposition we attempted.}

The cross-sector evidence reinforces the screen's primary
contribution: detection performance is portable; the conditional
price gap is a property of specific procurement environments,
not a setting-wide constant.
