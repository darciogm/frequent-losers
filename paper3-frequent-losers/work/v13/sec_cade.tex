\section{External Consistency Check: CADE Cartel Convictions}
\label{sec:cade}

The institutional design of Section~\ref{sec:empirical} relies on
the assumption that FL classification is informative about
cartel-driven cover bidding. We test that informational content
directly against CADE's adjudication portfolio.

\paragraph{Sample and prospective design.}
CADE's procurement-cartel portfolio for our window comprises 12
adjudicated cases covering 65 firm-defendants, with final
decisions spanning 2015--2025. Eight of the twelve cases were
adjudicated after the BEC sample window closes, making the bulk
of the validation prospective: the screen is asked whether
2009--2019 participation patterns anticipate firms whose
collusive role enforcement confirmed years later. Of the 65
firm-defendants, 47 match BEC participants via CNPJ, including
six firm-defendants whose primary CNAE we recover from
\texttt{Firms\_final} for the inverse-mapping exercise of
Section~\ref{sec:cross_sector}.

\paragraph{Co-participation rate.}
Among 2,735 FL firms, 193 (7.1\%) co-participate with at
least one of the 47 BEC-active CADE-defendants in the same
tender-item. A permutation test (1{,}000 iterations, stratified
by participation-count quartile to absorb mechanical exposure
through high participation volume) yields a permutation mean of
2.0\% (SD $= 0.3$\%); the observed 7.1\% is 3.5 times the
permuted mean and rejects the null of random co-participation
at $p < 0.001$. The remaining 92.9\% of FL firms with no CADE
overlap reflects the low base rate of cartel prosecution: CADE
convictions cover a small fraction of operationally active
cartels \citep{connor2007price}; the 7.1\% figure is a lower
bound on FL--cartel overlap, not evidence that the residual
92.9\% are unaffected.

\paragraph{Within-firm validation.}
The 7.1\% co-participation rate measures market proximity.
A more demanding test asks how many of the 47 BEC-active
firm-defendants the FL rule would have flagged \emph{before} the
relevant CADE adjudication. Seven of the 47 are always-losers
in BEC---defendants more typically participate as winners, the
designated rent recipients of cartel rents. Three of those
seven (43\%) cross the FL threshold, against a population base
rate of 16\% (2,735/16,843)---a 2.6$\times$
firm-level enrichment. The three are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:cade_fl_firms}: \emph{Sol Tecnologia} (CADE
process 08012.001273/2010-24, solar water heaters; 84 tenders,
zero wins), \emph{Nova Esperan\c{c}a Locadora} (08700.005876/2019-85,
school transportation; 97 tenders, zero wins), and \emph{Jofran
Com\'ercio} (08700.005789/2015-02, trash bags, Operation
Colludium; 65 tenders, zero wins). All three exhibit the
defining FL pattern: sustained participation across item groups
and years with no wins, identified by the screen on 2009--2019
data. Because eight of the twelve cases were adjudicated after
2019, the enrichment is genuinely prospective.

\paragraph{Pre-2020 vs.\ post-2019 split.}
Three cases were adjudicated within the BEC sample window
(coleta de lixo 2015-01, aquecedores solares 2015-09, trens-metr\^os 2019-07;
20 firm-defendants); two of those defendants are FL. Nine cases
were adjudicated 2020--2025 (27 firm-defendants); one is FL. The
prospective FL rate (1/27 $= 3.7\%$) is lower in absolute terms
but operates on a smaller always-loser pool: 4 of the 27
post-2019 firm-defendants are always-losers, of which 1 is FL---
a within-firm rate of $25\%$, again above the $16\%$ population
baseline. Both subsamples show enrichment in the same direction;
neither is significant on its own at conventional levels given
the small N. The pooled enrichment is significant; we report it
as the headline.

\paragraph{What co-participation does and does not show.}
Sharing a tender-item with a CADE-defendant measures market
proximity, not cartel membership. An FL firm that co-participates
may be a cover bidder deployed by the cartel, an unwitting
competitor in a cartel-affected market, or a bystander.
Conditional on participation count, the FL--CADE overlap rate
becomes statistically indistinguishable from that of non-FL
always-losers ($p = 0.93$; Section~\ref{sec:ground_truth}).
The unconditional excess is therefore driven largely by high
participation volume rather than by the specific FL
classification. This is the screen's operating principle, not
its weakness: a participation-based triage tool flags firms
that participate heavily in suspicious markets, without
requiring the researcher to disentangle participation-driven
proximity from direct cartel involvement---a distinction that,
with observational data alone, may be impossible to draw
cleanly. The permutation test (3.5$\times$, stratified by
participation quartile) confirms that the unconditional excess
is real, not a mechanical artifact of participation volume.
What the CADE check does not establish is that any given FL
firm is a cartel member.

\paragraph{Robustness to known cartels.}
A natural concern is that the FL--price gap is driven entirely
by tenders CADE has already flagged. Dropping all 31{,}447
CADE-involved tender-items and re-estimating the baseline
yields $\hat{\beta} = 0.062$ ($N = 1{,}622{,}954$), virtually
unchanged from the full-sample 0.064 (Table~\ref{tab:excl_cade},
\ref{app:robustness}). The FL--price association does not
depend on the enforcement record. Either the residual sample
contains undetected cartels that the screen identifies before
enforcement does, or FL presence correlates with high-price
environments through channels orthogonal to documented
collusion. Either reading is consistent with the
diagnostic-screen claim of the paper; only the first justifies
the policy claim of Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

The bottom line is the conjunction of three checks: (i) the
permutation-stratified 3.5$\times$ FL--CADE overlap, (ii) the
within-firm 2.6$\times$ enrichment with prospective bite, (iii)
the price-gap insensitivity to the enforcement record. None
identifies the cover-bidding mechanism on its own. Together
they are the strongest external consistency check the available
ground truth supports.
