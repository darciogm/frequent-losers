\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Among 16{,}843 firms that participated in São Paulo's electronic
procurement platform between 2009 and 2019 without ever winning a
single contract, 2{,}735 placed bids in fourteen or more tenders.
At plausible bid-preparation costs of R\$50--500, sustained
zero-win participation past this threshold is rationally dominated
under any standard entry model: a Bayesian learner with a uniform
prior expects negative profit per bid. Why do thousands of firms
behave this way?

Brazilian procurement law (Lei 8.666/93, Article~22) requires the
sealed-bid \emph{convite} modality to receive at least three valid
proposals before a contract may be awarded. The rule was designed
to protect competition. In practice, when fewer than three
genuine bidders appear, the buyer (or the cartel that controls
the buyer's pool) must produce additional proposals---and those
proposals tend to be deliberately losing, the configuration that
competition law calls \emph{cover bidding}
\citep{marshall2012economics, asker2010leniency, porter1993detection}.
The minimum-bidder rule generates, by construction, a population
of firms whose participation pattern is the empirical signature
of regulatory cover bidding: persistent presence, zero wins. We
operationalize this signature into a screening rule and validate
it against the procurement-cartel adjudication portfolio of
Brazil's competition authority (CADE).

We call these firms \emph{frequent losers} (FL) and define them
in two steps: always-losers (zero win rate) above an outlier
threshold of 14 tenders (median + $1.5 \times$ IQR of the
participation distribution), set \emph{a priori} on behavioral
grounds. The screen requires only contract-award records---no bid
microdata, no structural assumptions, no enforcement records. It
is implementable by any procurement oversight body with a
participant log: the Tribunal de Contas do Estado, the
Controladoria Geral da Uni\~ao, comparable institutions in OECD
countries, and CADE itself when triaging tips for bid-rigging
investigations.

\paragraph{Findings.} We make two empirical claims, of unequal
strength. The \emph{detection} claim is robust: against
CADE-defendant co-bidding as ground truth, the FL screen achieves
AUC~$=0.94$ (95\% DeLong CI: $[0.932, 0.946]$), materially above
the $0.79$ obtained by an \citet{imhof2019detecting}-style
bid-level composite ($p < 0.001$). The result generalizes
within-platform: replicated separately across ten BEC product
sectors with at least three CADE positives, the AUC averages
$0.954$ (SD $0.034$); every sector exceeds $0.90$. Eight of the
twelve cartel cases were adjudicated \emph{after} the BEC sample
closes, so part of the validation is strictly prospective: the
2009--2019 FL classification anticipated firms whose
collusive involvement subsequent enforcement confirmed years
later. Three of the 47 BEC-active CADE-defendants are themselves
classified as FL, against a $16\%$ population base rate among
always-losers---a $2.6\times$ enrichment.

The \emph{price-gap} claim is weaker and we treat it as such.
FL-flagged tenders show 3.6--7.7\% higher negotiated prices in
within-item, within-year, within-purchasing-unit comparisons,
robust across cross-fit, OLS, and matching estimators. The price
gap concentrates where the regulatory cover-bidding incentive is
operative: under \emph{voluntary} cover bidding within convite
($n_{\text{genuine}} \geq 3$, where the rule does not force
entry), the FL premium is $7.6\%$; under binding constraint
($n_{\text{genuine}} < 3$, where FL participation is the
statutory requirement rather than a strategic choice), the
coefficient flips negative. Pregão (Lei 10.520/2002), which has
no minimum-bidder rule, shows a smaller premium than convite-voluntary.
The price gap is a conditional association, not a causal estimate;
\citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity analysis yields
$RV_{q=1} = 17.5\%$, bounding the scope for omitted confounders
without identifying them. Pre-event coefficients in the FL-entry
event study at $t = -2,-3$ preclude a fully causal reading.

\paragraph{Mechanism evidence.} We complement the price regression
with five diagnostics that constrain the interpretation. FL-present
tenders have $0.143$ \emph{more} non-FL firms (no crowding-out, contradicting
the prediction of legitimate FL competition); winners in
FL-present tenders bid $4\%$ closer to publicly posted reference
prices (calibrated cover bidding); FL entry has elasticity
$0.0021$ with respect to lagged prices (price-chasing too weak to
explain the main result); FL firms form $4{,}603$ unique winner
pairs with $\geq 5$ shared tenders against a permuted mean of
$3{,}271$ ($p < 0.001$); genuine firms in FL-exposed markets
exit BEC \emph{less} frequently, not more, consistent with a
stable cartel ecosystem rather than displacement. Each
diagnostic is individually consistent with non-collusive
alternatives; jointly with the institutional identification, they
constrain the alternative readings.

\paragraph{Contributions.} \emph{First}, we close the partitioning
gap in the bid-coordination literature. \citet{bajari2003deciding},
\citet{conley2016detecting}, and subsequent work require an
\emph{ex post} partition of bidders into suspected colluders and
a competitive fringe. The FL classifier provides the
\emph{ex ante} firm-level partition these tests need. \emph{Second},
we close an operational gap in the proactive-screen literature.
\citet{imhof2019detecting}, \citet{huber2019machine}, and
\citet{wallimann2023machine} require granular bid microdata that
many electronic procurement systems do not preserve. The FL
screen runs on the contract-award records virtually every system
maintains, lowering the data threshold for proactive enforcement
to participation tallies and win/loss flags. \emph{Third}, we
develop the law-and-economics framing of the screen: the
convite minimum-bidder rule, intended to protect competition,
generates a decoy-firm population that procurement authorities
can detect at virtually no marginal cost, and we translate the
detection capability into a three-stage implementation pathway
(Screen $\rightarrow$ Triage $\rightarrow$ Investigate; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion})
deployable by Brazilian and OECD oversight bodies.

We are explicit about what the design does not deliver. We do not
identify cover bidders firm-by-firm: FL-defendant overlap with
CADE conditional on participation count is statistically
indistinguishable from non-FL always-losers ($p = 0.93$), making
the screen an environment-level marker rather than a stable firm
type. We do not estimate causal overcharges: the price gap is a
conditional association. We do not measure welfare losses
beyond illustrative back-of-the-envelope calculations
(\ref{app:extensions}, Online). The screen is a flag, not a
verdict.
