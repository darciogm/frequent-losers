\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Brazilian procurement law (Lei 8.666/93, Article 22) requires the
sealed-bid \emph{convite} modality to receive at least three valid
proposals before a contract may be awarded. The rule was designed
to protect competition. Its operational effect, documented in
multiple Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econ\^omica (CADE)
investigations, has been the opposite: when fewer than three genuine
bidders appear, the buyer (or the cartel that controls the buyer's
pool) must produce additional bids, and those bids tend to be
deliberately losing---a pattern competition law calls \emph{cover
bidding} \citep{marshall2012economics, asker2010leniency}. The
minimum-bidder rule thus creates, by design, a population of firms
whose participation pattern is anomalous: persistent presence,
zero wins.

This paper proposes a screening rule that uses precisely that
anomaly. Define a \emph{frequent loser} (FL) as a firm that bids on
many tenders without ever winning. The screen requires only
participation records and win/loss outcomes---no bid microdata, no
structural assumptions, no enforcement records. It is implementable
by any procurement oversight body that maintains contract registries:
the Tribunal de Contas do Estado (TCE), the Controladoria Geral da
Uni\~ao, comparable institutions in OECD countries, and CADE itself
when triaging tips for bid-rigging investigations. The screen does
not identify cartelists. It flags procurement environments
systematically associated with patterns the cover-bidding literature
documents in prosecuted cases.

We apply the screen to S\~ao Paulo's Bolsa Eletr\^onica de Compras
(BEC), the centralized procurement platform handling 1{,}308
purchasing units across 4.5 million tender-items between 2009 and
2019, and validate it against CADE's portfolio of cartel
adjudications. Among 2{,}735 FL firms identified in BEC,
193 (7.1\%) co-participate with at least one CADE-defendant in the
same tender-item---3.5$\times$ the participation-stratified
baseline ($p < 0.001$). Three of the 47 BEC-active CADE-defendants
are themselves classified as FL by the screen, against a population
base rate of 6.6\%. The ROC curve against CADE co-bidding achieves
AUC~$= 0.94$, with the data-driven optimal threshold (Youden's
$J = 0.84$) nearly identical to the rule we set \emph{a priori} on
behavioral grounds. Eight of the twelve cartel cases were
adjudicated after the BEC sample window closes, so the validation
is partly prospective: 2009--2019 participation patterns
anticipate firms whose involvement enforcement confirmed years
later.

FL-flagged tenders show 3.6--7.7\% higher negotiated prices in
within-item, within-year, within-purchasing-unit comparisons
(temporal cross-fitting, OLS with high-dimensional fixed effects,
and matching estimators bracket this range). After absorbing item
fixed effects the raw FL--non-FL gap collapses from 2.43 to 0.050
log points (overlap 0.978; Table~\ref{tab:conditional_descstats},
\ref{app:competition})---the regressions compare similar tenders
within tight product categories. Comparability, however, is not
identification. The price gap is a conditional association, not a
causal estimate. \citet{cinelli2020making} sensitivity analysis
yields $RV_{q=1} = 17.5\%$, bounding the scope for omitted
confounders, but bounding is not identification. We are explicit
about what the design does and does not deliver.

The legal and economic stakes of getting screening right are
considerable. Brazilian federal and subnational procurement
spending exceeds 12\% of GDP \citep{oecd2019government}; even
modest cartel overcharges aggregate to material public losses.
Existing screens require bid-level microdata that incomplete
electronic systems do not always preserve
\citep{imhof2019detecting, huber2019machine}; the FL screen lowers
the data threshold for proactive enforcement to participation
records that virtually every electronic system records. The 2021
Brazilian procurement reform (Lei 14.133/2021, replacing Lei
8.666/93) tightens habilitation rules in ways that should reduce
the regulatory cover-bidding incentive over time, but does not
eliminate it; the screen we develop applies under both regimes
and across other settings where minimum-participant rules or
formal-competition requirements create analogous incentives.

\subsection{Contributions}

\emph{First}, we operationalize the regulatory-cover-bidding
phenomenon with a participation-only screen that requires no bid
microdata. The screen is firm-level, computable from
contract-award records alone, and deployable at scale by oversight
bodies whose data infrastructure cannot support tender-level
forensic analysis. Against CADE-defendant co-bidding as ground
truth, the screen achieves AUC~$= 0.94$, dominating
\citet{imhof2019detecting}-style bid-level composites
($0.79$; DeLong $p < 0.001$).

\emph{Second}, we document that FL-flagged environments exhibit
3.6--7.7\% higher negotiated prices in within-item comparisons.
This is a conditional association, not a causal estimate; we are
explicit about identification limits and the scope of plausible
omitted-variable bias.

\emph{Third}, we develop the law-and-economics framing of the
screen: the convite minimum-bidder rule, intended to protect
competition, generates a decoy-firm population that procurement
authorities can detect at virtually no marginal cost. Our policy
discussion (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) translates the screen
into an implementation pathway: triage flagging firms, then
prioritize by network features (winner concentration,
co-bidding partners), then deploy bid-level forensic tools on
the triaged set.

We do not claim to identify cover bidders, estimate causal
overcharges, or measure welfare losses. The screen is a
diagnostic, not a verdict.
