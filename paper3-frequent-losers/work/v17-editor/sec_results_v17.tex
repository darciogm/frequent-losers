\section{Pricing Imprint as Descriptive Corroboration}
\label{sec:results}

Do environments the screen flags also carry a price imprint?
This section reports the conditional association between
frequent-loser presence and log negotiated unit price as
descriptive corroboration of the discrimination evidence in
\S\ref{sec:cade}. The paper does not rest on this section. The
screening contribution is carried by the discrimination evidence
in \S\ref{sec:cade} and the architectural test in
\S\ref{sec:forensic}; the price imprint is a third piece of
evidence on whether the construct also tracks an outcome the
collusion literature would expect cover bidding to leave on
prices. We report it briefly across three steps:
the broad-sample conditional association $\beta$
(\S\ref{sec:results_ols}); the overlap-restricted
$\beta^{\text{ov}}$ and the segment-level decomposition of
the resulting sign reversal (\S\ref{sec:results_overlap}); and
the heterogeneity of the signal across detection regimes
(\S\ref{sec:results_oversight}). The screening-value
formalisation that motivates why the broad-sample $\beta$
remains an economic object under coarsened observability is in
Online Appendix~A
(Proposition~\ref{prop:screening_value}); we do not lean on it
in the body of the paper.

\subsection{Broad-Sample Conditional Association \texorpdfstring{($\beta$)}{(beta)}}
\label{sec:results_ols}

Frequent-loser presence associates with a higher log negotiated
unit price in all four specifications of
Equation~\eqref{eq:ols_main}: $\valPctSixeight$ within item and
year, $\valPctSixfour$ adding procuring-unit fixed effects, and
$\valPctNinethree$ in pregão against $\valPctThreeeight$ in
convite (Table~\ref{tab:prices}). Cross-fit estimation yields
$\valPctThreeSix$, CEM $\valBidCoefMean$, IPW $\valBidNonCV$, and
a leave-one-out IV returns $\valBidPHigh$ ($p<0.05$, first-stage
$F=396$). The four estimators jointly bound the conditional range
$\valHeadlineRange$.

The pregão--convite asymmetry falsifies a quorum-filler reading.
Lei~$8.666/93$ Art.~$22$ \S\,$3$ requires a three-bidder minimum
in convite; if the construct's signal were mechanically driven by
firms entering to satisfy that quorum, the imprint would be
sharper in convite. The data show the opposite
($\valFalPregBin$ in pregão vs.\ $\valFalConvBin$ in convite;
Table~\ref{tab:falsification_modal}).

$\beta$ is a conditional association across items where
frequent losers deploy. Whether it is also a treatment effect
on prices is a separate question that the available
identification cannot settle. \S\ref{sec:results_overlap}
shows that overlap restriction reverses the sign and
decomposes the reversal across tender-value segments.

\subsection{Overlap Restriction, Sign Reversal, and Segment-Level Decomposition}
\label{sec:results_overlap}

Restricting comparisons to cells where frequent-loser-present
and -absent items genuinely overlap on observables and
reweighting to the average treatment effect on the treated
produces a reversed coefficient: overlap-cell ATT
$\valMatchOverlapCoef$, propensity-score-trimmed ATT
$\valMatchPSCoef$ (Online Appendix~B,
Table~\ref{tab:item_level_scope_match}). The reversal is real;
the cell-dropping decomposition reported in the same appendix
table (Table~\ref{tab:sign_reversal_decomp}) shows it is a
reweighting result rather than a dropped-counterfactual result:
only $\valDroppedItemsShare$ of treated items lack a within-cell
counterfactual and are strictly dropped, the remaining
$99\%$ participate in both estimates, and what changes is
which untreated counterfactuals each estimator up-weights.
The paper does not rest on either sign. The segment-level
decomposition that follows gives the sharper structure. Within
tender-value quintiles, the broad-sample coefficient is already
negative in Q1--Q3 ($\valSRQoneBroad$, $\valSRQtwoBroad$,
$\valSRQthreeBroad$, $p<10^{-15}$ in each) and positive in Q4
($\valSRQfourBroad$, $p=0.012$). Both the overlap restriction and
the ATT reweighting leave each within-quintile estimate
essentially unchanged: the Q4 positive survives all three
specifications at $\valSRQfourATT$ ($p=\valSRQfourATTp$ under ATT
weights), and the Q1--Q3 negatives survive at quantitatively
similar magnitudes. The aggregate $\widehat\beta=\valBetaBroad$
is therefore the population-weighted average of three negative
within-quintile coefficients and one positive Q4 coefficient,
with Q4's $40{,}878$ treated items dominating the volume-weighted
mean; $\widehat\beta^{\text{ov}}$ is the same average under
cell-size weights that down-weight Q4's volume
(Table~\ref{tab:sign_reversal_segment}, Panel~A). Trimming
the most heavily ATT-weighted cells \emph{strengthens} the
negative estimate---from $\valSRTrimNone$ at no trim to
$\valSRTrimTen$ at the top decile and $\valSRTrimFifty$ at the
median---so the negative is structural in the bulk of the overlap
sample, not extreme leverage on cells with thin counterfactuals
(Panel~C). Inside the overlap sample, two further wedges echo the
segment reading: $\widehat\beta^{\text{ov}}$ is statistically null
among items containing direct CADE defendants ($\valBetaCADEDirect$,
$p=\valBetaCADEDirectP$, against $\valBetaCADENonDirect$ in
non-direct-CADE items, $p<0.001$), and positive in the highest
tender-value quartile ($\valBetaQfour$, $p=\valBetaQfourP$).

What the segment decomposition supports is narrower than a
treatment-effect identification. In Q4---the segment where
deployment value is most plausible \emph{a priori} on contract
size---the imprint is positive and robust to design choice
($\valSRQfourBroad$ broad, $\valSRQfourATT$ under ATT). In
Q1--Q3 the imprint is negative, consistent with several
non-exclusive readings (thin-market price formation, selection
on item characteristics, composition heterogeneity in the firm
pool) that the available identification cannot disentangle.
The paper does not rest on either sign. The discrimination
evidence in \S\ref{sec:cade} and the architectural test in
\S\ref{sec:forensic} carry the contribution; the broad-sample
imprint is bracketed by \citet{cinelli2020making}'s
$RV_{q=1}=\valRVqOne$ and
\citet{oster2019unobservable}'s $\hat\delta=\valOsterDelta$
against selection on observables already absorbed by the fixed
effects. Figure~\ref{fig:two_objects} traces the coefficient
across the four anchor specifications. The conditions under
which $\beta$ remains an economic object under coarsened
observability are formalised in Online Appendix~A
(Proposition~\ref{prop:screening_value}); the body of the
paper does not lean on that formalisation.

\subsection{Heterogeneity Across Detection Regimes}
\label{sec:results_oversight}

Where the screening signal varies tells us something the level
does not. Splitting the sample into quartiles of procuring-unit
size produces a sharp extreme-quartile contrast:
$\valOversightQonePct$ at the smallest-buyer quartile (Q1) and
$\valOversightQfourPct$ at the largest (Q4), a
$\valPBUgradientRatio\!\!\times$ gradient preserved across
alternative size measures (annual contract volume, cumulative
item count, headcount of distinct purchasers).
The intermediate quartiles are imprecisely estimated and not
statistically distinguishable from each other or from Q4
(Table~\ref{tab:regime_oversight}).

The framework predicts the contrast direction through the
detection-cost comparative static $\partial m^*/\partial
\theta_k < 0$: cover-bidder deployment is more aggressive where
the principal cost of detection is lower. Buyer size proxies for
several correlated institutional features---procurement-officer
tenure \citep{coviello2017tenure}, internal-audit infrastructure,
item composition, and discretionary procedure use
\citep{decarolis2020rules,decarolis2020bureaucratic}---none
separable with the variation we have. We read the
extreme-quartile contrast as heterogeneity in the screening object
across the monitoring environment, in the framework's predicted
direction, with the magnitude concentrated at the extremes.
