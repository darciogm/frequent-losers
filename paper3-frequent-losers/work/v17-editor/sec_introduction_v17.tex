\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Can a cartel-detection screen built on the contract-award
envelope identify cartel-adjacent firms when per-bidder bid
amounts are not routinely observable? The empirical
industrial-organization literature reads collusion off the
distribution of submitted bids
\citep{porter1993detection,bajari2003deciding,imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine}.
Yet most enforcement environments expose that distribution only
through case-specific administrative requests. Electronic
procurement systems around the world archive the award envelope
at an analytical-warehouse interface that returns query results
in minutes---winner identity, participant identity, item code,
negotiated price---while per-bidder bid amounts, where retained
at all, sit in operational logs reachable only after weeks of
administrative back-and-forth (\S\ref{sec:institutional}).
Methods designed for the bid layer simply do not run on the
layer that survives.

We take this asymmetry as a constraint on enforcement design and
ask three connected questions. What collusion-relevant information
remains in award-record data alone? Does it separate
cartel-adjacent populations from competitive ones at discrimination
accuracy comparable to bid-distribution methods? And does the
answer reorganize how an enforcement architecture under incomplete
observability should be sequenced?

A simple separating-equilibrium framework with cover bidders and
genuine entrants makes the first two questions tractable
(Online Appendix~A). Cover bidders in equilibrium satisfy
$\Pr(\text{win}\mid C)=0$: the $\text{wins}=0$ filter is a
separating choice, not a descriptive coincidence. Given
award-record data, $\log(1+\text{tenders\_count})$ on
$\text{wins}=0$ firms ranks firms by their posterior probability
of being type $C$, and endogenous sorting into cartel-affected
items is part of what the screening object measures rather than
a confound to clean. The binary
$\text{median}+1.5\!\times\!\text{IQR}$ rule coarsens this
continuous primitive, and we call firms above the threshold
\emph{frequent losers}.

The third question is informational and architectural. Under
incomplete operational observability, the appropriate enforcement
design separates an \emph{award-layer screening stage} that
prioritises candidate environments from a \emph{bid-layer
forensic stage} that adjudicates suspected coordination on the
bid distribution
\citep{becker1968crime,stigler1970optimum,harrington2008detecting,kawai2022detecting}.
The screening stage need not wait for the bid layer to be
recovered.

We test the framework on São Paulo's Bolsa Eletr\^onica de
Compras (BEC, $\valYearStart$--$\valYearEnd$), an electronic
platform that preserves the award envelope across $\valSampleN$
tender-items. Three discrimination results lead. The first is the
within-stratum result. Against the cobidder population inside
the always-loser stratum---the $\valCobidders$ firms that
participated alongside adjudicated CADE direct defendants---the
flag yields firm-level AUC $\valAUCFLfirmTemp$ under temporal
holdout (train $2009$--$2016$, test $2017$--$2019$) and
$\valAUCprePost$ on a strict pre-$2020$ benchmark with
participation-stratified permutation null
($\valMultThreeTwo\!\!\times$ excess over the random-matching
baseline, $p<0.001$). An out-of-fold and temporal-holdout
audit of the corresponding item-level AUC decomposes the raw
in-sample $0.995$ into a structural component ($\approx
0.86$--$0.89$) and a pure-leakage component ($0.10$--$0.13$);
the structural component is what the screening interpretation
rests on (Table~\ref{tab:leakage_audit}), and operational
metrics throughout this paper use the temporal-holdout column
as the headline reference.

The second is the architectural result. Against the seven-feature
Imhof--Wallimann bid-distribution pipeline trained on the
forensic-recoverable bid-microdata layer (\S\ref{sec:forensic}),
the award-layer flag matches AUC on a thinner envelope and adds
non-redundant signal in same-sample combination
($+\valImhofIncFL$ AUC, DeLong $p=\valImhofIncFLp$). A sequential
rule that uses the flag to filter which firms enter the forensic
stage catches $\valArchTPThouSeqK$ of $\valArchNPos$ adjudicated
cobidders in the top-$1{,}000$ flag list while interrogating bid
microdata for $\valArchSeqKone$ firms instead of
$\valArchPoolSize$, an $\valArchFootprintReduction$ data-envelope
reduction whose relative recall robustness survives the
temporal-holdout audit.

The third is the design-signature result. Against the
$\valDirectCADE$ direct CADE defendants in the broader BEC firm
universe, AUC is $\valAUCdirectStd$. The flag recovers loser-side
participation, not winner-side identity, and the asymmetry is
not a failure but the empirical fingerprint of what a screen
built on the loser side can and cannot do.

The cobidder population behaves like the modelled type. They
deploy at higher intensity than FL non-cobidders (Cohen's
$d=\valBridgeUniqWinD$ on unique winners faced), they bid
proximally to direct CADE defendants
($d=\valBridgeDirectD$), and they concentrate their participation
in narrower markets ($d=\valBridgeHHID$ on portfolio item-group
HHI). At the bid level, they bid plausibly close to winners
($d=\valTBBLMedGapD$ on per-firm median gap, $p<10^{-6}$) with
elevated within-firm cross-bid dispersion ($p<10^{-3}$ in a
multivariate logit holding participation intensity constant).
The signature matches credible cover bidding---bids low enough
to be plausible, dispersed enough to track rotation across cover
roles---rather than the textbook deliberately-uncompetitive cover
bid the early literature described.

Frequent-loser presence also leaves a $\valHeadlineRange$ higher
conditional log negotiated unit price across four estimators on
the broad sample. The segment-level decomposition concentrates
the positive sign in the largest-tender-value stratum
($\valSRQfourBroad$ in Q4, robust to overlap restriction and to
trimming the most heavily ATT-weighted cells); we report it as
descriptive corroboration, not as a causal estimate.

The contribution sits at the intersection of four literatures.
Bid-coordination tests
\citep{bajari2003deciding,porter1993detection,conley2016detecting}
require a researcher-supplied partition between colluders and a
competitive fringe; the construct supplies a candidate
\emph{ex ante} firm-level flag computable from award records.
Bid-distribution screens
\citep{imhof2019detecting,wallimann2023machine,huber2019machine}
require bid microdata at the analytical-warehouse layer; the
construct operates on the operational layer and integrates with
bid-distribution methods at the forensic stage where microdata are
recoverable. Empirical cover-bidding work identifies cover bidders
\emph{ex post}, through leniency testimony or judicial discovery
\citep{porter1999ohio,pesendorfer2000study,asker2010leniency,kawai2022detecting};
we are not aware of a published prospective identification of
cover-bidder candidates from public award-record data alone. The
enforcement-design literature
\citep{becker1968crime,stigler1970optimum,harrington2008detecting,baker2002case}
prescribes how detection technology and information acquisition
interact under costly observation; the screening-then-forensic
sequencing this paper proposes is a particular structure of that
information chain.

The cumulative claim is structural and informational. Under
incomplete operational observability of the bid layer, an
award-layer screening stage prioritises environments at
discrimination accuracy comparable to bid-distribution methods
on a substantially thinner data envelope, with non-redundant
signal in combination and a sequential architecture whose
data-envelope wedge survives temporal holdout. The bid-layer
forensic stage need not be the universal entry point for cartel
enforcement. Jurisdictions whose operational layer carries award
records but not per-bidder bid amounts can deploy the screening
stage on data they already maintain, and reforms that mandate
bid archival expand the forensic stage rather than substitute
for it.

Four claims the paper does not make. The pricing imprint is not
a causal estimate of cover bidding's effect on prices. The
buyer-size gradient is scope information about where the
screening signal varies across detection regimes, not
identification of an institutional channel. The
preg\~ao--convite modal asymmetry is scope information about
where the screening object discriminates better, not a
positive test of the minimum-bidder-rule mechanism. And the
construct does not adjudicate cartel membership: cobidders are
the validation object the data layer supports, and the AUC
asymmetry against direct defendants is the design's empirical
signature, not a failure of the screening logic.
