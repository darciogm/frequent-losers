\subsection{OLS Baseline}
\label{sec:results_ols}

Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports the baseline OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:main} for log negotiated prices. Across all four
specifications, FL presence is associated with significantly higher
prices: 6.8\% in the general specification (column~1), 6.4\% with PBU
fixed effects (column~2), 9.3\% for preg\~ao (column~3), and 3.8\% for
convite (column~4). The inclusion of PBU fixed effects in column~2
absorbs purchasing-unit-specific price levels yet barely changes the
coefficient, suggesting that FL presence does not simply proxy for
high-cost PBUs.

\input{../output/tables/tab_iv_main}

The preferred IV price estimate (Panel~A: 0.194, $p = 0.01$) exceeds
the OLS estimate (0.064) by a factor of three. The implied
signal-to-total-variance ratio is % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: expanded λ interpretation with plausibility + bounds
$\hat{\lambda} = \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} / \hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}}
= 0.064 / 0.194 \approx 0.33$.
Given that the FL dummy is a deliberately coarse screen defined by a
distributional threshold, a signal-to-noise ratio of one-third is
entirely plausible: the threshold sensitivity exercises
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) show that the coefficient increases
monotonically with stricter thresholds, consistent with a finer screen
recovering more of the true effect. The implied markup of 21\% falls
within the 10--50\% range typically found in cartel overcharge studies
\citep{connor2007price}.

Moreover, if treatment effects are heterogeneous and concentrated in
markets where FL supply variation is most consequential, the
IV---which identifies a LATE---will exceed OLS even absent measurement
error. We cannot fully disentangle attenuation from LATE heterogeneity.
Accordingly, we treat the OLS estimate as a conservative lower bound and
the IV estimate as an upper bound on the average price effect, consistent
with the welfare calculations in Section~\ref{sec:welfare}.

\paragraph{LATE interpretation.}
The IV estimate represents a Local Average Treatment Effect
(LATE) for tenders whose FL participation is driven by supply-side
availability rather than demand-side selection. Comparing the IV
estimate (0.194) to the network-based competitive-market coefficient
(0.126) yields a ratio of 1.54, consistent with the IV identifying
complier tenders where FL supply shifts are most consequential.

\paragraph{IV robustness.}
Table~\ref{tab:iv_panelc} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} reports the % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: reframed Panel C as "for completeness"
specification with item-group $\times$ year fixed effects for
completeness; the heavier FE structure increases the risk of
weak-instrument bias and we do not use it for inference. The IV estimate
is substantially larger, consistent with weak-instrument amplification as
the more demanding FE structure reduces the first-stage $F$-statistic.
We retain Panel~A as the preferred specification. Balance tests (Table~\ref{tab:iv_balance}
in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}) regress pre-determined observables on
the LOO instrument with item, year, and PBU FE. Even with $N > 1.5$ % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: added economic-grounds framing for balance
million observations, all standardized differences are below
0.03$\sigma$---well inside what is typically considered negligible
covariate imbalance. We therefore evaluate balance on economic grounds
rather than statistical significance, which is uninformative at this
sample size.

A placebo IV exercise replaces FL firms with sub-threshold always-losers
(firms that never win but participate in fewer than 14 tenders). As
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} (Table~\ref{tab:iv_placebo}),
the placebo first stage is weak: the sub-threshold instrument's
coefficient is an order of magnitude smaller than the main instrument's
(0.00004 vs.\ 0.0003), and the resulting 2SLS estimate is implausibly
large---a hallmark of near-weak-instrument bias. This contrast supports
the view that the main LOO instrument captures genuine variation in FL
supply rather than common geographic shocks.

\subsection{Network-Split Results}
\label{sec:results_network}

Table~\ref{tab:network_split} splits FL firms into % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: relabeled high/low-suspicion → market-structure labels
concentrated-market and competitive-market subgroups based on the
co-bidding network metrics described in
Section~\ref{sec:empirical_network}.\footnote{We label these groups by
market structure rather than suspicion level: high-HHI markets with
repeated winners may reflect natural monopoly or structural concentration
rather than cartel behavior, precisely the environments where cover
bidding yields less competitive benefit.}
The results reveal an informative pattern of heterogeneity.
Competitive-market FL firms---those operating in markets with
\emph{diversified} winner patterns (low winner HHI)---drive the entire
price effect, with a coefficient of 0.126 ($p < 0.01$) in the PBU FE
specification. Concentrated-market FL firms, operating in markets with
repeated winners, show no significant price effect ($-0.018$, $p > 0.3$).

The absence of a price effect among FL firms in highly concentrated
markets is itself informative: cover bidding is most valuable where
genuine competitive threat exists. In markets already dominated by one or
two firms, the marginal value of manufacturing the appearance of
competition is lower---the dominant firm already controls pricing through
market power. The finding speaks to the optimal deployment of the FL
screen: it is most informative in competitive markets, precisely where
procurement agencies have the greatest scope for intervention.

We formalize this intuition using continuous market concentration.
Table~\ref{tab:network_interactions} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}
reports two interaction specifications. Column~(1) interacts FL presence
with winner HHI at the item-group $\times$ PBU $\times$ year level. The
interaction coefficient is negative ($-0.024$) but not statistically
significant (SE~$= 0.067$), directionally consistent with the
network-split finding that the FL price effect is smaller in concentrated
markets, though the continuous specification lacks power to detect the
interaction. Column~(2)
interacts FL presence with the number of genuine (non-FL) bidders,
providing a complementary perspective: the FL effect is modulated by the
level of genuine competition. FL-present tenders attract substantially
more genuine bidders than FL-absent tenders (mean $n_{\text{genuine}}$
of approximately 4--5 vs.\ 3), further supporting strategic selection
rather than competitive displacement.

\subsection{Bajari--Ye Tests}
\label{sec:results_bajari}

We apply the \citet{bajari2003deciding} framework to test three
sequential claims: (i) FL bid residuals violate exchangeability,
(ii) FL bid residuals violate conditional independence, and
(iii) these violations survive absorption of tender-level confounds.
Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye} reports the results.

\input{../output/tables/tab_bajari_ye}

\paragraph{Claim 1: Exchangeability fails (Panel~A).}
The Kolmogorov--Smirnov test rejects the null that FL and
non-FL bid residuals are drawn from the same distribution
($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$), violating the exchangeability condition
required under competitive bidding (Prediction~\ref{pred:exchange}).

\paragraph{Claim 2: Conditional independence fails (Panel~B).}
The mean product of pairwise FL residuals is 5.16 ($t = 157.4$),
larger than the non-FL pairwise product of 2.21 ($t = 71.5$). The
bootstrap difference of 2.95 (95\% CI: $[2.57, 5.21]$) excludes zero,
indicating that FL bid residuals exhibit stronger within-tender
correlation than non-FL residuals
(Prediction~\ref{pred:independence}).

\paragraph{Fake-groups placebo (Panel~C).}
When non-FL firms are randomly split into two groups within each tender,
the pairwise product is 5.80---also significantly different from zero.
This reflects common tender-level shocks (e.g., commodity prices,
PBU-specific factors) that induce positive residual correlations among
\emph{all} bidders in the same tender, regardless of FL status. The
relevant comparison is therefore not whether the pairwise product differs
from zero, but whether FL pairs exhibit \emph{stronger} correlation than
non-FL pairs. The bootstrap difference between FL and non-FL products
(2.95, 95\% CI: $[2.57, 5.21]$) indicates that FL bid residuals are
more correlated than non-FL residuals, net of common tender-level shocks.

\paragraph{Claim 3: Tender FE do not absorb the signal.}
As a more demanding check, we re-estimate the first-stage bid equation
with tender $\times$ item fixed effects, which absorb \emph{all}
tender-level shocks (reference price, commodity prices, PBU-specific
factors). Only within-tender, cross-firm variation in residuals remains.
Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_tender_fe} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}
compares the baseline (item + year FE) and tender-FE pairwise products.
Under tender FE, the fake-groups product drops from 5.80 to 0.51 and
the non-FL product from 2.21 to 0.86, indicating that most of the
baseline correlation reflects common tender-level shocks. The FL pairwise
product drops from 5.16 to 0.38---significantly positive ($p < 0.001$),
but smaller in magnitude than the non-FL product (0.86). This ordering
is not inconsistent with the cover bidding hypothesis: genuine bidders
in the same tender share cost information (input prices, local market
conditions) that induces residual correlation, while cover bidders
submit bids without such shared cost signals. The key finding is that FL
residual correlation remains significantly positive after absorbing all
tender-level confounds, suggesting a common signal among cover bids
beyond the tender-level mean. We interpret this contrast with caution: % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: added explicit hedge on FL vs non-FL magnitude
non-FL bidders operating in the same markets may share cost information
or face correlated signals, which would mechanically generate positive
residual correlation even absent coordination. The relevant diagnostic is
not the magnitude ranking but the failure of exchangeability and
conditional independence for FL bidders specifically.

Taken together, the Bajari--Ye diagnostics indicate that FL bids are % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: added closing paragraph linking to cover-bidding interpretation
generated by a process that violates both exchangeability and conditional
independence, even after absorbing tender-level shocks. We do not rely on
the magnitude comparison between groups as a standalone argument. Rather,
we treat the Bajari--Ye results as non-structural evidence that is
consistent with, and reinforces, the cover-bidding interpretation
developed in Section~\ref{sec:results_iv}.

\subsection{Regime Test}
\label{sec:results_regime}

To discriminate between Regime~1 (complementary) and Regime~2
(coordinated) cover bidding (Prediction~\ref{pred:regime}), we analyze
the bid spread distribution of FL firms.

Among FL bids with observable spread, 91\% exceed the winning bid, with
a median spread of approximately 55\%. FL bid dispersion (measured by
log standard deviation) is significantly higher than non-FL dispersion,
consistent with Regime~1: cover bidders submit bids from a wide
distribution above the winning price without precise calibration near it.

Figure~\ref{fig:regime_densities} compares simulated bid densities under
each regime with the empirical FL distribution. The empirical pattern
most closely resembles Regime~1 (uniform above the winning bid), though
with a heavier right tail than the simple theoretical model predicts.
