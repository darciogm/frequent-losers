\section{External Validation: CADE Cartel Convictions}
\label{sec:cade}

Before presenting the main empirical strategy and results, we validate
the FL screen against an external benchmark: firms convicted of
bid-rigging by CADE (Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econ\^omica),
Brazil's competition authority. If FL firms are disproportionately
associated with known cartel participants, this supports the screen's
credibility as a detection tool (H1) regardless of the causal mechanism.

\subsection{CADE--BEC Cross-Match}

We match CADE cartel conviction records (2009--2019) to BEC firm
identifiers using CNPJ (the Brazilian federal tax identifier). Of 65
CADE cartel cases involving procurement, 47 convicted firms are matched
to BEC, collectively participating in approximately 12,500 tender-items.

\subsection{FL--CADE Co-Participation}

We define ``co-participation'' as an FL firm and a CADE-convicted firm
both submitting bids in the same tender-item. Among 2,735 FL firms, 193
(7.1\%) co-participate with at least one CADE-convicted firm. Among
38,709 non-FL firms, the unconditional co-participation rate is 5.9\%
($\chi^2 = 5.7$, $p = 0.017$, odds ratio $= 1.21$).

\paragraph{Permutation test.}
To assess whether the 7.1\% co-participation rate could arise by chance,
we conduct 1,000 permutations: in each iteration, we randomly select
2,735 firms from the pool of always-losers, matching the participation
count distribution of FL firms via stratified resampling by
participation quartile. We compute the co-participation rate with
CADE-convicted firms for each random draw. The observed 7.1\% exceeds
all 1,000 permutation draws ($p < 0.001$), indicating that FL--CADE % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: "confirming" → "indicating"
overlap is not driven by high participation counts alone.

\paragraph{Permutation-adjusted comparison.}
The unconditional odds ratio of 1.21 understates the FL--CADE
association because FL firms participate in many more tenders than
typical firms, mechanically increasing their co-participation
opportunity. The permutation test controls for this by drawing 2,735
random firms from the always-loser pool, \emph{stratified by
participation quartile}, and computing their co-participation rate. The
permutation mean is 2.0\% (SD~=~0.3\%), implying that the observed
7.1\% FL rate is 3.5 times higher than expected after controlling for
participation intensity ($p < 0.001$).

\paragraph{Addressing the 92.9\% non-overlap.}
That 92.9\% of FL firms have no CADE overlap should not be interpreted
as evidence against the FL screen. CADE prosecutes only a small fraction
of actual cartels---estimates suggest 1--5\% of cartel activity
results in prosecution \citep{connor2007price}. The relevant comparison
is the \emph{participation-adjusted} rate: FL firms are 3.5 times more
likely than comparable always-losers to co-participate with known
cartelists, against a very low base rate of detection. If CADE captures
only $\sim$2\% of actual cartels, the observed 7.1\% FL overlap is
consistent with a much larger fraction of FL firms being involved in
undetected collusion.

\subsection{Regressions Excluding CADE Markets}

A concern is that the FL--price association in Section~\ref{sec:results}
might be driven entirely by the known cartel cases. To address this, we
drop all 31,447 tender-items involving any CADE-convicted firm and
re-estimate the baseline price regression on the remaining 1,622,954
observations. Table~\ref{tab:excl_cade} shows that the FL coefficient
(0.062) remains significant and virtually identical to the full-sample
estimate (0.064), demonstrating that FL detects price anomalies
\emph{beyond} known cartels.
