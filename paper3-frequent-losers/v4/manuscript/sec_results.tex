\subsection{OLS Baseline}

Table~\ref{tab:prices} reports the baseline OLS estimates of
Equation~\eqref{eq:main} for log negotiated prices. Across all four

high-cost PBUs.

\subsection{Instrumental Variable Results}
\label{sec:results_iv}

Table~\ref{tab:iv_first_stage} reports the first-stage diagnostics of
the leave-one-out instrument (Equation~\ref{eq:iv}). The instrument is a
strong predictor of FL presence, with $F$-statistics well above the
conventional threshold of 10. Table~\ref{tab:iv_main} presents the
second-stage 2SLS estimates.

\input{../output/tables/tab_iv_first_stage}

\input{../output/tables/tab_iv_main}

The IV price estimate (0.194, $p = 0.01$) exceeds the OLS estimate % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: t=2.56, p≈0.01 is more precise than p<0.05
(0.064) by a factor of three, consistent with measurement-error
attenuation: the binary FL indicator misclassifies some cover bidders
below the IQR threshold (attenuation bias) and potentially some
non-cover-bidder FL firms above it (contamination). The IV corrects for
this by exploiting exogenous variation in FL availability. The implied
markup of 21\% falls within the 10--50\% range typically found in cartel
overcharge studies \citep{connor2007price}.

Adding item-group $\times$ year fixed effects (Panel~C) absorbs
product-market-year shocks, restricting identification to cross-PBU
variation within narrow product-year cells. The IV estimate in this
specification is substantially larger, suggesting that the more demanding
FE structure weakens the instrument (the first-stage $F$-statistic
declines), leading to weak-instrument amplification of the second-stage
coefficient. The preferred specification with item + year + PBU FE
(Panel~A) balances instrument strength with adequate control for
confounds. Balance tests (Table~\ref{tab:iv_balance} in
Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}) regress pre-determined observables on the
LOO instrument with item, year, and PBU FE. The point estimates are
statistically significant but economically negligible (all $< 0.001$),
reflecting the very large sample size ($N > 1.5$ million) rather than
meaningful imbalance. In standardized terms, a one-SD increase in the
instrument shifts each observable by less than 0.03~SD, well below
conventional thresholds for meaningful covariate imbalance.

\paragraph{IV magnitude interpretation.}
The ratio $\lambda = \hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}} / \hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}} % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: added LATE/heterogeneity caveat
\approx 0.33$ implies that approximately 67\% of the variation in the
binary FL indicator is noise---i.e., the threshold-based screen
misclassifies roughly two-thirds of its assignments. This degree of
misclassification is plausible: the IQR threshold is a statistical
screen, not a diagnostic tool, and the threshold sensitivity exercises
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) confirm that the coefficient increases
monotonically with stricter thresholds. However, $\lambda < 1$ can also
reflect treatment effect heterogeneity: if the IV identifies complier
tenders with larger-than-average treatment effects
($\text{LATE} > \text{ATE}$), the IV would exceed OLS even without
measurement error. We cannot disentangle these two channels, but note
that both are consistent with the data. The IV estimate represents a
Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) for tenders whose FL participation
is driven by supply-side availability rather than demand-side selection.
Comparing the IV estimate (0.194) to the network-based low-suspicion
coefficient (0.126) yields a ratio of 1.54, consistent with the IV
identifying an even more extreme subpopulation of complier tenders where
FL supply shifts are most consequential.

A placebo IV exercise replaces FL firms with sub-threshold always-losers
(firms that never win but participate in fewer than 14 tenders). As
reported in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} (Table~\ref{tab:iv_placebo}),
the placebo first stage is weak: the sub-threshold instrument's
coefficient is an order of magnitude smaller than the main instrument's
(0.00004 vs.\ 0.0003), and the resulting 2SLS estimate is implausibly
large---a hallmark of near-weak-instrument bias. This contrast supports
the view that the main LOO instrument captures genuine variation in FL
supply rather than common geographic shocks.

\subsection{Network-Split Results}
\label{sec:results_network}

Table~\ref{tab:network_split} splits FL firms into high-suspicion and
low-suspicion subgroups based on the co-bidding network metrics described
in Section~\ref{sec:empirical_network}. The results reveal an
informative pattern of heterogeneity. FL firms classified as
low-suspicion---those operating in markets with \emph{diversified}
winner patterns (low winner HHI)---drive the entire price effect, with a
coefficient of 0.126 ($p < 0.01$) in the PBU FE specification.
High-suspicion FL firms, operating in \emph{concentrated} markets with
repeated winners, show no significant price effect ($-0.018$, $p > 0.3$).

This pattern is consistent with the economics of cover bidding: the
marginal value of a cover bidder is highest in competitive markets where
genuine competition threatens the cartel's preferred outcome. In
concentrated markets where one firm dominates regardless, cover bidding
is redundant---the dominant firm already controls pricing through market
power. The finding also speaks to the optimal deployment of the FL
screen: it is most informative in competitive markets, precisely where
procurement agencies have the greatest scope for intervention.

We formalize this intuition using continuous market concentration.
Table~\ref{tab:network_interactions} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}
reports two interaction specifications. Column~(1) interacts FL presence
with winner HHI at the item-group $\times$ PBU $\times$ year level. The % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: "confirms" → directionally consistent; coef is insignificant (t=-0.36)
interaction coefficient is negative ($-0.024$) but not statistically
significant (SE~$= 0.067$), directionally consistent with the
network-split finding that the FL price effect is smaller in concentrated
markets, though the continuous specification lacks power to detect the
interaction. Column~(2)
interacts FL presence with the number of genuine (non-FL) bidders,
providing a complementary perspective: the FL effect is modulated by the
level of genuine competition. FL-present tenders attract substantially
more genuine bidders than FL-absent tenders (mean $n_{\text{genuine}}$
of approximately 4--5 vs.\ 3), further supporting strategic selection
rather than competitive displacement.

\subsection{Bajari--Ye Tests}
\label{sec:results_bajari}

Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye} reports the Bajari--Ye test results.

\input{../output/tables/tab_bajari_ye}

\paragraph{Exchangeability (Panel~A).}
The Kolmogorov--Smirnov test strongly rejects the null that FL and
non-FL bid residuals are drawn from the same distribution
($D = 0.15$, $p < 0.001$), violating the exchangeability condition
required under competitive bidding (Prediction~\ref{pred:exchange}).

\paragraph{Conditional independence (Panel~B).}
The mean product of pairwise FL residuals is 5.16 ($t = 157.4$),
substantially larger than the non-FL pairwise product of 2.21
($t = 71.5$). The bootstrap difference of 2.95 has a 95\% confidence
interval that excludes zero, confirming that FL bid residuals exhibit
significantly stronger within-tender correlation than non-FL residuals
(Prediction~\ref{pred:independence}).

\paragraph{Fake-groups placebo (Panel~C).}
When non-FL firms are randomly split into two groups within each tender,
the pairwise product is 5.80---also significantly different from zero.
This reflects common tender-level shocks (e.g., commodity prices,
PBU-specific factors) that induce positive residual correlations among
\emph{all} bidders in the same tender, regardless of FL status. The
relevant comparison is therefore not whether the pairwise product differs
from zero, but whether FL pairs exhibit \emph{stronger} correlation than
non-FL pairs. The FL pairwise product (5.16) is significantly smaller
than the fake-group product (5.80), while both exceed the non-FL product
(2.21). The bootstrap difference between FL and non-FL products (2.95,
95\% CI: $[2.57, 5.21]$) confirms that FL bid residuals are
significantly more correlated than non-FL residuals, net of common
tender-level shocks.

\paragraph{Tender fixed effects refinement.}
As an additional check, we re-estimate the first-stage bid equation with
tender $\times$ item fixed effects, which absorb \emph{all} tender-level
shocks (reference price, commodity prices, PBU-specific factors). Only
within-tender, cross-firm variation in residuals remains.
Table~\ref{tab:bajari_ye_tender_fe} in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}
compares the baseline (item + year FE) and tender-FE pairwise products.
Under tender FE, the fake-groups product drops from 5.80 to 0.51 and % CO-AUTHOR EDIT: expanded tender-FE discussion to address FL < non-FL reversal
the non-FL product from 2.21 to 0.86, confirming that most of the
baseline correlation reflects common tender-level shocks. The FL pairwise
product drops from 5.16 to 0.38---significantly positive ($p < 0.001$),
but smaller in magnitude than the non-FL product (0.86). This reversal
likely reflects the fact that genuine bidders in the same tender share
cost information (input prices, local market conditions) that induces
residual correlation, while cover bidders submit bids without such
shared cost signals. The relevant finding is that FL residual correlation
remains significantly positive after absorbing all tender-level confounds,
indicating a common signal among cover bids (the designated winning bid)
beyond the tender-level mean.

\subsection{Regime Test}
\label{sec:results_regime}

To discriminate between Regime~1 (complementary) and Regime~2
(coordinated) cover bidding (Prediction~\ref{pred:regime}), we analyze
the bid spread distribution of FL firms.

Among FL bids with observable spread, 91\% exceed the winning bid, with
a median spread of approximately 55\%. FL bid dispersion (measured by
log standard deviation) is significantly higher than non-FL dispersion,
consistent with Regime~1: cover bidders submit bids from a wide
distribution above the winning price without precise calibration near it.

Figure~\ref{fig:regime_densities} compares simulated bid densities under
each regime with the empirical FL distribution. The empirical pattern
most closely resembles Regime~1 (uniform above the winning bid), though
with a heavier right tail than the simple theoretical model predicts.
