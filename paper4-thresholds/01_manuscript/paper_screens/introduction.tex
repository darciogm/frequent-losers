\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Convicted bid-rigging cartels in S\~{a}o Paulo exchange workers with each other at a rate that has nothing to do with chance. In the six cartels that Brazil's federal competition authority prosecuted between 1999 and 2016 and that we can match to the universe of formal employment, any two members of the same cartel were bridged, on average, by 74 workers moving across firm boundaries over the eight years for which we have matched employer--employee data. A random pair of non-cartel firms drawn from the same bidder universe was bridged by, on average, $0.003$ such workers. This is a factor of 24{,}000. It is not the kind of difference a screen should miss.

This paper builds a cartel screen around that fact. Our premise is that bid-rigging cartels are structurally integrated sets of firms, and that this integration leaves fingerprints outside the bid stream --- specifically in two places where cartel-detection research has not systematically looked. The first is the pair-level labor-mobility pipeline: which workers move between which firms, and how often. The second is the pattern of items on which firms co-participate in auctions, independent of what they bid. Both features are pair-level, both are symmetric, and both are computable from data that competition authorities routinely hold: a matched employer--employee register linked to a procurement bidder registry. Neither requires observation of bid values, reference prices, or auction-format details.

Why has nobody tried this? The applied detection literature has been dominated by signals extracted directly from the bids. Variance screens flag auctions with unusually tight bid clustering \citep{abrantesmetz2006screening, harrington2008detecting_effects}. Rotation and complementary-bidding screens look for patterns predicted by classical cartel theory \citep{porter1993detection, porter1999detection, bajari2003deciding}. More recent work constructs non-parametric tests of competitive behavior based on the shape of the bid distribution \citep{chassang2022robust, kawai2022detecting}, and supervised-learning papers treat bid-distribution statistics as features for classifier training \citep{huber2019detection, kawai2023bidrotation}. What these approaches share is that the signal lives in the bids themselves. But a cartel that carefully shapes its bids to look competitive can fool a bid-based screen; it cannot as easily fool a screen that reads firm structure. And --- as we show below --- bid-based screens sometimes fail by construction: in the Brazilian \emph{preg\~{a}o eletr\^{o}nico}, a descending electronic auction in which bidders observe each other's bids in real time and iteratively compress toward the lowest-so-far price, the classical variance screen cannot distinguish cartel from competitive auctions at all.

\subsection*{What we do}

We operationalize two firm-pair fingerprints and build them into a composite classifier. For each unordered pair $\{i, j\}$ of firms in the universe of BEC bidders, we compute:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pair-level worker flow} $\mathrm{wf}_{ij}$: the symmetric count of workers observed at firm $i$ in year $t$ and at firm $j$ in year $t+1$ (or the reverse), summed over 2009--2016. Our regression feature is $\ln(1 + \mathrm{wf}_{ij})$. This operationalizes the inter-firm labor-mobility pipeline documented in the labor-economics literature by \citet{stoyanov2012worker}, \citet{jarosch2021learning}, and \citet{sorkin2018ranking}: workers who move between firms transmit productive knowledge, strategic information, and human capital, and the rate at which two firms exchange workers is therefore a measurable proxy for their pair-level structural linkage.

\item \textbf{Product-market overlap} $\mathrm{ov}_{ij}$: the logarithm of the number of distinct BEC item codes on which both firm $i$ and firm $j$ submitted at least one bid over 2009--2017. This measures how much of the two firms' procurement universes overlap, independent of who won. It is a direct operationalization of the classical cartel-theory observation \citep{stigler1964theory, green1984noncooperative} that cartels must coordinate over a shared product scope.
\end{itemize}

We validate the composite against the ground truth provided by Brazil's federal competition authority (CADE): six procurement cartels convicted between 1999 and 2016 that operated in S\~{a}o Paulo and whose member firms are identifiable in the BEC bidder universe. After deduplicating one firm that appears in two cartel sectors, the six cartels involve 33 distinct firms and exactly 100 unordered within-cartel pairs. The negative class is a sample of approximately 100{,}000 pairs drawn from non-cartel BEC-active firms. All inference is performed under leave-one-CADE-cartel-out cross-validation.

\subsection*{What we find}

Three findings define the paper's contribution.

\emph{First}, the composite classifier achieves a leave-one-cartel-out macro AUC of $\mathbf{0.845}$ and a pooled AUC of $\mathbf{0.809}$. The macro average --- our preferred cross-cartel generalization metric --- beats product-market overlap alone by $+0.11$ AUC points, beats the three trivial firm-pair baselines by $+0.04$ to $+0.27$, and beats every alternative specification we test. A label-permutation test on the pooled AUC rejects the no-signal null at the 2\% level. The composite captures a real detection signal that bid-based screens miss.

\emph{Second}, the magnitude of the incremental contribution of worker flow beyond product-market overlap is economically meaningful ($+0.11$ macro AUC) but statistically imprecise. A cluster bootstrap over the six CADE cartels gives a 95\% confidence interval on that incremental contribution of approximately $[-0.07, +0.27]$ and an empirical $p$-value of $0.10$. Under a strict 5\% threshold, we cannot reject the hypothesis that worker flow's marginal contribution is zero. We report this precision limitation prominently, and interpret the point estimate as the best available evidence that a pair-level labor-mobility fingerprint is present in field cartel data. The precision limit is a property of the five-effective-cartel validation sample, not of the feature's informativeness.

\emph{Third}, the composite generalizes cleanly on four of the six cartels --- the three largest (pharmaceuticals, school meals, metropolitan rail) and one mid-sized (trash bags) --- with per-fold AUCs between 0.84 and 1.00. It generalizes weakly on the two shortest-lived cartels (solar water heaters, 2009--2010; airport cafeterias, 2014), where fold AUCs are approximately 0.66. We read this as an honest boundary condition: worker flow and product-market overlap both accumulate over time, so a cartel with only a single year of documented conduct has not had the opportunity to leave either fingerprint. This defines the methodology's operating envelope --- mature, multi-year cartels with consolidated product scope --- and clarifies that it is not a substitute for reactive enforcement against short-lived price-fixing episodes.

\subsection*{Contributions}

To the detection literature, we (i)~introduce a new class of screens that read firm-pair structure rather than bid distributions, connecting applied antitrust empirics to the labor-economics literature on inter-firm knowledge transmission; (ii)~document that the classical variance-screen of \citet{abrantesmetz2006screening} produces AUCs below chance on preg\~{a}o data, which we attribute to the descending-auction format mechanically compressing within-auction bid dispersion and thereby eliminating the variance-screen's identifying signal regardless of cartel status; and (iii)~characterize failure modes in terms of cartel age, size, and product-market specialization, giving authorities a clear operating envelope for deployment.

To the empirical industrial organization of procurement, we (iv)~provide the first pair-level linkage of CADE-convicted procurement cartels to both the bid-level BEC panel and the universe-level RAIS matched employer--employee register, establishing reusable data infrastructure for any subsequent work that wants to study cartel firms at the labor-market margin; and (v)~document an empirical fact of independent interest --- the 24{,}000-fold difference in pair-level worker flow between convicted cartel pairs and random non-cartel pairs --- that is, to our knowledge, the first systematic measurement of this quantity in any field dataset.

\subsection*{Related literature}

The paper sits at the intersection of four literatures. The \textbf{cartel-detection} literature pioneered by \citet{porter1993detection, porter1999detection} and \citet{bajari2003deciding}, synthesized for policy by \citet{harrington2008detecting_effects}, and extended recently by \citet{kawai2022detecting} and \citet{chassang2022robust}, has focused almost exclusively on bid-based signals. None of these papers uses pair-level labor or product-market features as screen inputs. Our contribution is additive to this literature, not substitute.

The \textbf{labor-mobility and knowledge-transfer} literature, building on \citet{cardheiningkline2013}, \citet{sorkin2018ranking}, \citet{jarosch2021learning}, and \citet{stoyanov2012worker}, establishes that inter-firm worker flows transmit productive and strategic knowledge. We provide the first systematic test of whether the pair-level labor-mobility pipeline documented in that literature leaves a detectable fingerprint on ground-truth cartel data.

The \textbf{Brazilian procurement} literature \citep{ferrazfinan2008, ferrazfinan2011, barbosa2020revolving, szerman2023employee, colonelli2022corruption} has established BEC and RAIS as credible data sources for public-procurement empirics but has not previously combined them with CADE ground truth for detection purposes. To our knowledge we are the first to do so.

Finally, the \textbf{machine-learning-for-antitrust} literature \citep{huber2019detection, chassang2022robust, kawai2023bidrotation} has explored supervised learning and structural tests for detection but has focused exclusively on bid-distribution features and bidding-pattern features as inputs. Our contribution is to introduce two orthogonal input classes --- pair-level worker flow and pair-level product-market participation --- that expand the feature space in a direction previous work has not considered.
