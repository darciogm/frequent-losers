When antitrust enforcement dismantles a procurement cartel, the convicted firms' workers disperse to competitors. Does this labor reallocation transmit collusive know-how to the receiving firms---or does it simply redistribute operational capacity? We test this by linking six convicted cartels in S\~{a}o Paulo to millions of public auctions and the universe of matched employer--employee records. Using a stacked difference-in-differences design, we find that firms absorbing ex-cartel workers increase their win rates by up to 5--6 percentage points in the same product markets---but do not raise their prices. Three robustness checks---a Bartik shift-share instrument, a conditional-on-hiring placebo, and the imputation estimator of Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess---sharpen and discipline this headline. The stacked-DiD estimate is an upper bound that bundles capacity transfer with a hiring-decision selection margin; conditioning on whether the receiving firm was hiring into a cartel-relevant occupation at all, the cartel-source effect attenuates to roughly $+1.7$~pp. Characterizing the movers sharpens the mechanism: the workers who transfer are predominantly operational staff in service and sales roles, not the managers or professionals who might carry coordination strategies. The combined evidence is consistent with capacity transfer---a pro-competitive side effect of enforcement-driven labor reallocation---rather than collusion contagion, though the precision of our robustness tests leaves moderate contagion effects statistically possible. Enforcement dismantles the cartel and, through labor reallocation, appears to strengthen the cartel's former rivals without transmitting the ability to collude.
