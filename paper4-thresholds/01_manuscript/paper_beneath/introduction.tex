\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Between 2009 and 2019, Brazil's federal antitrust authority---the \emph{Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econ\^{o}mica} (CADE)---secured administrative convictions against 35 firms across seven distinct procurement cartels operating in the state of S\~{a}o Paulo, with case files delineating the specific product markets and time windows over which each ring was active. We match these convicted firms and tender windows to the universe of 3.8~million bids placed on S\~{a}o Paulo's electronic procurement platform, the \emph{Bolsa Eletr\^{o}nica de Compras} (BEC), and to the universe of formal-sector employment relationships recorded in the Rela\c{c}\~{a}o Anual de Informa\c{c}\~{o}es Sociais (RAIS). On the resulting benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions in which at least one convicted firm bid during the active period of its cartel, the canonical variance-based screens of \citet{porter1993detecting} and \citet{bajari2003detecting} behave in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model: bid dispersion, the coefficient of variation of bids, and the number of active bidders are all compressed at cartel-tainted auctions. Yet the areas under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUCs) for each of these screens lie between 0.46 and 0.48---statistically indistinguishable from chance as discriminators between cartel and non-cartel auctions. A close-margin regression discontinuity in the spirit of \citet{kawai2023using} likewise produces a null. A single alternative screen, constructed from inter-firm worker flows in the matched employer--employee data, recovers an AUC of 0.75 on the same benchmark. A specialization placebo restricted to same-market non-cartel firm pairs absorbs approximately half of this signal, leaving a cartel-specific residual that indicates cartels both inherit and reinforce the personnel flows natural to thin specialized-labor markets. This paper documents these facts, interrogates their mechanism, and draws out the implications for the design of cartel-detection screens in such markets.

A growing literature uses statistical regularities in bidding behavior to infer collusion that is never documented directly in the administrative record. Classical screens exploit the compression induced by bid-rotation agreements: colluding firms submit prearranged prices that cluster tightly around the designated winner, yielding low dispersion, few active bidders, and predictable rotation patterns \citep{porter1993detecting, pesendorfer2000study, bajari2003detecting, conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening}. A complementary and influential approach, recently advanced by \citet{kawai2023using}, focuses on the winning--losing threshold: under competition, the observable characteristics of narrowly winning and narrowly losing firms should be continuous at a bid margin of zero, and any statistically sharp discontinuity at the cutoff identifies an anti-competitive wedge. Both approaches have been validated primarily against cases in high-income economies with deep formal labor markets, dense bidder pools, and strong institutional capacity for antitrust enforcement; their extension to thinner institutional environments rests on a small but growing empirical base. \citet{wachs2019network} apply a co-bidding network approach to roughly 150{,}000 Georgian procurement notices and show that group-level cohesion and exclusivity flag candidate cartels in the absence of criminal ground truth. \citet{signor2020detection} exploit the dataset released by the Brazilian Federal Police's \emph{Operação Lava Jato} investigation to build a probabilistic detector of collusive tenders in the Petrobras construction-cartel case, reporting accuracy between 81 and 96 percent. \citet{huber2019machine} combine classical statistical screens with supervised machine-learning classifiers and correctly predict 84 percent of Swiss tenders out of sample, demonstrating the gains from combining bid-distribution screens with flexible functional forms. What has been missing is a direct test against an independent criminal ground truth in a setting with \emph{both} structurally different institutional primitives \emph{and} access to administrative employer--employee microdata. The S\~{a}o Paulo--CADE--RAIS linkage we build offers exactly that test, and is, to our knowledge, the first cartel-detection benchmark to integrate auction-level behavioral data, employer--employee matched labor records, and formal antitrust convictions. The policy stakes are first-order: middle-income competition authorities increasingly rely on off-the-shelf screens developed and validated abroad to triage investigations \citep{oecd2015, bosio2022public}, and if those screens systematically mislabel cartels under local conditions, existing enforcement pipelines may be misfiring in precisely the settings where deterrence capacity is weakest.

Our primary benchmark is a \emph{cartel-active} sample of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin pregão auctions (with absolute bid margin $|\text{MV}| \le 0.10$) in which at least one convicted firm participated during the active period of its cartel. This is the sample on which classical, regression-discontinuity, and network-based screens can all be estimated with enough statistical power to discriminate cartel auctions from a clean control. We supplement it with a \emph{strict pair-matched} sample of 14 auctions in which both the winning and the runner-up firms belong to the same convicted cartel during its prosecuted conduct window. The strict sample is informative about cartel mechanics in head-to-head competition in principle, but is severely power-limited. For transparency we also report results on an \emph{anytime-listed} sensitivity sample of 107 auctions in which both the winner and the runner-up appear in a CADE conviction for the same cartel process but the auction year may fall outside the prosecuted conduct window---of which 93 are out-of-window auctions and 100 of the 107 come from a single cartel (the medicamentos hub-and-spoke case, CADE process 08012.002222/2011-09). Because whether out-of-window bidding by firms that were cartelists in another year should be treated as cartel behavior depends on assumptions this paper does not attempt to resolve, the anytime sample is reported only as a sensitivity and is not the basis for any headline claim. We refer to the three samples collectively as \emph{conviction-anchored detection benchmarks}.

Our first set of results documents a striking failure of canonical screens in this institutional setting. On the cartel-active benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions, classical variance screens fire in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model: bid dispersion, the coefficient of variation of bids, and the number of active bidders are all \emph{compressed} at cartel-tainted auctions relative to clean controls, with $t$-statistics that are statistically overwhelming given the sample size ($p < 10^{-7}$ for each). Yet the areas under the receiver-operating-characteristic curves (AUCs) for these screens lie between 0.46 and 0.48---statistically indistinguishable from chance, and in the cases of the coefficient of variation and bid spread marginally \emph{below} 0.5. Classical screens are therefore directionally consistent with theory but operationally useless as cartel detectors in this setting: the distributional overlap between cartel and clean auctions is nearly total, and a triage tool built on these statistics would misclassify cartel auctions at essentially the same rate as random assignment. The same qualitative pattern holds on the strict pair-matched sample of 14 auctions---canonical direction, low discriminative power, and mostly non-significant individual $t$-tests owing to small-$N$ constraints. A direct replication of the Kawai close-margin RD on the cartel-active subsample yields no statistically detectable discontinuity in incumbency or last-bid behavior at the cutoff, and a density test in the spirit of \citet{imhof2018screening} detects no manipulation of the running variable. The anytime-listed sensitivity sample of 107 auctions does produce positive $\Delta$ estimates on variance screens with $p<0.02$, but we interpret this as a mechanical consequence of the 93 out-of-window auctions in that sample---auctions in which firms that had been cartelists in another year are bidding against each other under competitive conditions---rather than as evidence that cartel auctions exhibit elevated dispersion. In short, neither the canonical variance-based screens nor the close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} successfully detect the cartels that CADE has formally convicted.

Our second set of results shows that network-based screens constructed from administrative labor data recover strong and statistically robust cartel detection on the cartel-active benchmark. For each auction, we construct two network screens from the five-year window of RAIS records preceding the tender: the number of workers who have moved between the winner and the runner-up firms, and the Jaccard similarity of the two firms' historical workforces. On the cartel-active sample of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions, these screens yield AUCs of 0.750 and 0.739 respectively---outperforming every classical screen by a wide margin and representing, to our knowledge, the first robust AUCs reported for procurement cartel detection against a formal criminal ground truth. A third screen, built from the co-bidding network of shared tender participations, yields an AUC of 0.695, providing independent corroboration through a distinct information channel. Critically, the worker-flow signal is neither an artifact of sectoral composition nor a mechanical re-expression of co-bidding intensity. Restricting attention to firm pairs that share the same 2-digit sectoral classification reduces the worker-flow AUC only marginally, from 0.739 to 0.730, and the residual of worker-flow after partialling out the log co-bidding density of each pair achieves an AUC of 0.749---essentially unchanged from the raw baseline, indicating that worker mobility carries detection information above and beyond what co-bidding patterns alone can reveal. A more demanding test separates cartel-specific coordination from passive same-market labor circulation: we construct a \emph{specialization placebo} of non-cartel firm pairs bidding on the same BEC item code in the same year as each cartel-active auction, and compare worker-flow distributions across cartel, placebo, and global clean samples. Cartel pairs share at least one worker in 57.9\% of cases, compared with 31.1\% for same-market placebo pairs and 9.9\% for pairs in other markets. In a decomposition of the worker-flow AUC gain relative to the global clean baseline, roughly half is absorbed by the same-market specialization component and roughly half remains as a cartel-specific residual. Both components are consistent with our institutional-thinness mechanism: cartels inherit the labor-circulation patterns natural to thin specialized-labor markets and additionally reinforce those flows to sustain coordination. On the strict pair-matched sample ($N=10$ after close-margin filtering), worker-flow AUCs decline to near chance; we attribute this to power constraints at small sample sizes rather than to any breakdown in the underlying signal, and we emphasize that the cartel-active benchmark is where the discriminative power of any screen---classical or network-based---can be meaningfully evaluated. The conviction-anchored benchmark does not merely declare cartels detectable in S\~{a}o Paulo; it pinpoints the channel through which the detection signal propagates---worker mobility, not bid dispersion.

We interpret these findings through the lens of institutional thinness. In settings where the universe of firms capable of bidding on a given product is small and the formal-sector labor market for the specialized workers that serve those bids is shallow, coordination among colluding firms generates naturally detectable traces in worker flows: personnel rotations, key-employee transfers, and shared specialized labor become vehicles for, and therefore signatures of, the collusive communication that substitutes for the statistical-pattern coordination captured by canonical screens in thicker markets. This mechanism---which we develop formally in a simple model in Section~\ref{coll}---rationalizes both the low discriminative power of bid-dispersion screens and the success of network-based screens in the Brazilian setting. Critically, it also predicts the two-component decomposition of the worker-flow signal documented by our specialization placebo: because thin markets generate spontaneous labor circulation among same-market firms whether or not they are colluding, a non-cartel placebo restricted to same-market firm pairs should already exhibit elevated worker-flow overlap relative to random pairs; and cartel pairs should exhibit still greater overlap on top of that baseline. Both predictions are borne out in the data. The mechanism further predicts that classical screen-based enforcement strategies will systematically under-detect cartels in precisely the institutional environments where their deterrence value would be highest.

This paper makes three contributions. \emph{First}, methodologically, we deliver the first stress test of two complementary cartel-detection frameworks against an independent criminal ground truth, and we document a common failure mode: low discriminative power. On the cartel-active benchmark, canonical variance-based screens \citep{porter1993detecting, bajari2003detecting} fire in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model---compression of bid dispersion and bidder counts---but yield AUCs near chance, offering no operational basis for triaging investigations. The close-margin RD design of \citet{kawai2023using} similarly produces a null. Neither result repudiates the underlying designs, both of which remain internally valid; rather, both warn that the \emph{operational discriminating power} of screens developed against cartels in thick-labor environments is not invariant when these tools are redeployed to settings in which cartel coordination is suspected to rely on alternative technologies such as labor mobility. \emph{Second}, substantively, we introduce conviction-anchored detection benchmarks by linking CADE rulings, BEC bid-level records, and RAIS employer--employee data, and we show that worker-flow and co-bidding network screens constructed from these sources substantially outperform classical screens in detecting convicted cartels. Because such data are increasingly available to national competition authorities in middle-income economies, the screens we propose are directly deployable at negligible marginal cost. \emph{Third}, conceptually, we advance the view that collusion leaves institution-specific signatures, and that the design of an effective detection screen should depend on which coordination technology is cheapest for cartel members to operate in the relevant environment---a framing that organizes and partially reconciles the growing patchwork of contradictory empirical findings in the detection literature \citep{conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening, kawai2023using}.
