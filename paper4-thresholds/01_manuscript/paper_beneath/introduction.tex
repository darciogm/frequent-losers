\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Can the canonical tools for detecting procurement cartels distinguish collusion from noise when they leave the thick-market, high-enforcement environments in which they were built? And when a new screen appears to succeed where they fail, is the signal genuine---or an artifact of how the benchmark was constructed? We address both questions by linking seven cartels convicted by Brazil's antitrust authority to 2.6~million competitive auctions and the full universe of formal employment relationships in S\~{a}o Paulo. The answer to the first question is nuanced: classical variance screens and a close-margin regression discontinuity yield AUCs of $0.46$--$0.56$ in the aggregate, but disaggregating by coordination technology reveals that they detect bid rigging at operationally useful levels (AUC $\approx 0.65$) while missing hub-and-spoke cartels entirely. The answer to the second is a warning: worker-flow screens constructed from the same data appear to achieve an AUC of $0.75$, but temporal conditioning reveals that the entire signal comes from labor reallocation \emph{after} cartel conduct ends---not from pre-existing personnel connections that might reflect coordination. The traces that employment records capture are not those of collusion operating, but of collusion dissolving.

A growing literature uses statistical regularities in bidding behavior to infer collusion that is never documented directly. Classical screens exploit the compression that bid-rotation agreements impose on the bid distribution: prearranged prices cluster around the designated winner, yielding low dispersion, few active bidders, and predictable rotation \citep{porter1993detecting, pesendorfer2000study, bajari2003detecting, conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening}. The complementary approach of \citet{kawai2023using} focuses on the winning--losing threshold: under genuine competition, firms that barely win and barely lose should look alike, and any sharp discontinuity at the cutoff identifies a non-competitive wedge. Both families of tests have been validated primarily against cartels in high-income economies with deep bidder pools and strong institutional capacity for enforcement; their track record in thinner settings rests on a small and growing empirical base.

Network-based methods have pushed the frontier in two directions. Within the procurement data itself, co-bidding networks flag candidate cartels through group-level cohesion and exclusivity \citep{wachs2019network, signor2020detection, huber2019machine, clark2018bid}. Outside the bid record, a recent wave exploits external network layers: \citet{asai2026ownership} show that common-ownership links predict bid rigging in Singapore, and \citet{villamil2024collusion} build multiplex ownership--co-bidding networks for Swedish procurement. What none of these studies does---and what our benchmark uniquely enables---is test whether the detection signal from a network layer survives temporal conditioning: restricting the network measure to information available \emph{before} the auction being screened. This distinction matters because network panels built from administrative registries (employment, ownership, board membership) mechanically include post-event observations, and any correlation between the network measure and the ground-truth label may reflect the consequences of detection and enforcement rather than the coordination the screen is meant to identify.

Our primary evaluation sample is a \emph{cartel-active} benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin preg\~{a}o auctions in which at least one convicted firm bid during the active period of its cartel (details of sample construction in Section~\ref{data}). On that benchmark, classical screens disappoint. Bid dispersion and the coefficient of variation are weakly compressed at cartel-tainted auctions (Cohen's $d$ between $-0.07$ and $-0.24$), and Welch $t$-statistics are significant thanks to the enormous control sample---but the aggregate AUCs top out at $0.56$, far short of what a triage tool would require (though, as Section~\ref{sec:coord_type} shows, disaggregating by coordination technology recovers operationally useful detection for bid rigging). The close-margin RD produces a null. In thin markets---where the median number of bidders falls below five---classical screens actually fire in the \emph{wrong} direction (AUC $0.40$--$0.45$), consistent with phantom-bidding mechanisms that inflate rather than compress the bid distribution.

Worker-flow screens initially appear to resolve this failure. Counting the employees who have worked at both the winning and the runner-up firm over the 2009--2017 RAIS panel yields an AUC of $0.75$. But this headline rests on the full nine-year employment panel, which includes worker movements that occurred years \emph{after} the auction being screened. When we condition on temporal precedence---counting only workers who had appeared at both firms \emph{before} the auction year---the AUC falls to $0.52$, indistinguishable from chance. A three-year rolling window produces an essentially identical result ($0.54$). The prospective detection power of worker-flow screens is, on this benchmark, zero.

The temporal decomposition reveals where the signal actually lives. We classify each shared worker by when the inter-firm connection was first observable, relative to the cartel's conduct period. Pre-conduct worker flows produce an AUC of exactly $0.50$. During-conduct flows produce $0.62$. Post-conduct flows---worker movements that occurred \emph{after} the cartel period ended---produce $0.77$, accounting for nearly all of the headline result. The flows are directional: workers move predominantly \emph{from} convicted cartel firms \emph{to} their market rivals, at a ratio that rises from $1.4{:}1$ in 2010 to $4{:}1$ by 2016--2017. This pattern is consistent with enforcement-driven labor reallocation: as investigations proceed and conduct periods end, cartel firms shed employees who are absorbed by competitors in the same thin labor markets.

The paper makes four contributions. The first is a \textbf{positive detection result}. Although classical screens fail in the aggregate, disaggregating by coordination technology reveals that they detect bid rigging at operationally useful levels (AUC $\approx 0.65$). The aggregate null is a composition effect: the hub-and-spoke cartel that dominates the sample ($85\%$) leaves no trace in bid patterns and dilutes the bid-rigging signal. Matching the screen to the coordination technology restores detection power for the cartel types that classical screens are designed to catch.

The second is \textbf{methodological}. We document that detection benchmarks built from administrative panel data are vulnerable to look-ahead bias. When a network screen (worker-flow, ownership, or board-interlock) is evaluated against conviction-anchored ground truth using the full panel, post-enforcement labor reallocation inflates the apparent AUC. Temporal conditioning---restricting the network measure to pre-auction information---is necessary to distinguish prospective detection power from retrospective correlation. This caveat applies broadly: any study that evaluates a panel-based network screen against ground truth without conditioning on temporal precedence risks overstating detection performance.

The third contribution is \textbf{substantive}. The temporal pattern we document---post-conduct worker flows from cartel firms to rivals, intensifying over time and strongly directional---is evidence that enforcement reshapes the labor-market structure of thin product markets. \citet{szerman2023employee} showed that debarred procurement firms lose nearly half their employees; we show where those employees go: to competitors operating in the same specialized product markets. The matched employer--employee record thus captures not the coordination channel of collusion but its labor-market aftermath.

The fourth contribution is \textbf{empirical}. We deliver the first stress test of canonical detection frameworks against criminal ground truth outside a high-income setting. Classical screens and the Kawai RD yield near-null AUCs; worker-flow screens yield a null after temporal conditioning. The benchmark itself---linking CADE rulings, BEC bid-level records, and RAIS employer--employee data---is a reusable infrastructure for future detection research.
