\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Do the canonical tools for detecting procurement cartels work when they leave the thick-market, high-enforcement environments in which they were built? We put this question to a new test. Linking seven cartels convicted by Brazil's antitrust authority to 3.8~million bids and the full universe of formal employment relationships in S\~{a}o Paulo, we assemble the first detection benchmark that combines auction data, employer--employee records, and criminal ground truth. Classical variance screens and a close-margin regression discontinuity yield AUCs of $0.52$--$0.56$---directionally right but operationally inadequate. Screens built from inter-firm worker flows reach $0.75$, and the gap widens sharply in thin labor markets where collusion coordinates through people rather than prices. This paper documents these facts, traces them to an institutional mechanism, and draws out the consequences for enforcement design.

A growing literature uses statistical regularities in bidding behavior to infer collusion that is never documented directly. Classical screens exploit the compression that bid-rotation agreements impose on the bid distribution: prearranged prices cluster around the designated winner, yielding low dispersion, few active bidders, and predictable rotation \citep{porter1993detecting, pesendorfer2000study, bajari2003detecting, conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening}. The complementary approach of \citet{kawai2023using} focuses on the winning--losing threshold: under genuine competition, firms that barely win and barely lose should look alike, and any sharp discontinuity at the cutoff identifies a non-competitive wedge. Both families of tests have been validated primarily against cartels in high-income economies with deep bidder pools and strong institutional capacity for enforcement; their track record in thinner settings rests on a small and growing empirical base.

Network-based methods have pushed the frontier in two directions. Within the procurement data itself, co-bidding networks flag candidate cartels through group-level cohesion and exclusivity \citep{wachs2019network, signor2020detection, huber2019machine, clark2018bid}. Outside the bid record, a recent wave exploits external network layers: \citet{asai2026ownership} show that common-ownership links predict bid rigging in Singapore, and \citet{villamil2024collusion} build multiplex ownership--co-bidding networks for Swedish procurement. What remains missing is a network layer that captures \emph{operational} coordination---the personnel flows, knowledge transfers, and shared specialized labor through which cartels in thin markets actually run---and a validation of such a layer against criminal ground truth in a middle-income setting. The S\~{a}o Paulo--CADE--RAIS linkage we construct offers precisely that test, and is the first cartel-detection benchmark to integrate auction-level data, matched employer--employee records, and formal antitrust convictions. The policy stakes are immediate: middle-income competition authorities increasingly rely on screens developed and validated abroad to triage investigations \citep{oecd2015, bosio2022public}, and if those screens systematically miss cartels under local conditions, enforcement pipelines may be misfiring where deterrence is needed most.

Our primary evaluation sample is a \emph{cartel-active} benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin pregão auctions in which at least one convicted firm bid during the active period of its cartel (details of sample construction in Section~\ref{data}).

On that benchmark, canonical screens disappoint. Bid dispersion and the coefficient of variation are weakly compressed at cartel-tainted auctions (Cohen's $d$ between $-0.07$ and $-0.24$), and Welch $t$-statistics are significant thanks to the enormous control sample---but the AUCs top out at $0.56$, far short of what a triage tool would require. The close-margin RD produces a null on the cartel subsample. In short, the screens pick up a whisper of signal, but not enough to act on.

Worker-flow screens recover what bid screens cannot. Counting the employees who have worked at both the winning and the runner-up firm over the 2009--2017 panel yields an AUC of $0.75$; the Jaccard similarity of the two firms' workforces reaches $0.74$; a co-bidding density measure adds $0.70$. A specialization placebo decomposes the worker-flow signal into a same-market component---reflecting the natural labor circulation of thin product markets---and a cartel-specific residual. Roughly half the signal comes from each channel, consistent with cartels both inheriting and reinforcing the personnel flows already present in the markets where they operate.

We read these findings through the lens of \emph{institutional thinness}. When the universe of firms capable of bidding on a given product is small and the formal-sector labor pool that serves those firms is shallow, coordination through personnel becomes the cheapest technology available to a cartel. The bid distribution, in such markets, carries little trace of the collusion---but the employment records do. A simple model in Section~\ref{sec:model} formalizes this logic and delivers three testable predictions: classical screens should be weakest in thin markets; worker-flow screens should be strongest there; and a same-market placebo should absorb part, but not all, of the worker-flow signal. All three predictions are borne out. The sharpest confirmation comes from splitting the benchmark by market thickness: the gap between the best classical screen and the worker-flow screen jumps from 10~percentage points in thick markets to 33~percentage points in thin ones.

The paper makes three contributions. The first is conceptual. Collusion leaves \emph{institution-specific} signatures: the screen that detects a cartel depends on which coordination technology is cheapest in the relevant environment. In thick labor markets, collusion channels through bids and classical screens fire; in thin ones, it channels through people and worker-flow screens fire. This framing organizes---and partially reconciles---the contradictory findings that have accumulated as detection methods developed in one setting are deployed in another \citep{conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening, kawai2023using}. The second contribution is substantive. We build conviction-anchored benchmarks by linking CADE rulings, BEC bid-level records, and RAIS employer--employee data, and show that worker-flow and co-bidding screens constructed from these sources substantially outperform classical screens. Because matched employer--employee registries are increasingly available to national competition authorities in middle-income economies, the screens we propose are directly deployable at negligible marginal cost. The third contribution is methodological: we deliver the first stress test of canonical detection frameworks against criminal ground truth outside a high-income setting, documenting that classical screens and the Kawai RD yield AUCs in the low $0.50$s while network screens reach $0.75$.
