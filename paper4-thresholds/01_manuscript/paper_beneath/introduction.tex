\section{Introduction}\label{intro}

Do the canonical tools for detecting procurement cartels work when they are deployed outside the thick-market, high-enforcement environments in which they were developed? We assemble the first conviction-anchored detection benchmark that integrates auction-level bid data, matched employer--employee records, and formal antitrust rulings---linking seven convicted cartels in S\~{a}o Paulo to 3.8~million bids and the universe of formal-sector employment relationships. On this benchmark, classical variance-based screens and a close-margin regression discontinuity design yield AUCs of $0.52$--$0.56$: directionally correct but operationally useless. By contrast, screens constructed from inter-firm worker flows in the employer--employee data achieve AUCs of $0.75$---and the gap widens sharply in thin labor markets where collusion coordinates through personnel rather than bid patterns. This paper documents these facts, proposes a mechanism linking screen effectiveness to institutional primitives, and draws out the implications for enforcement design.

A growing literature uses statistical regularities in bidding behavior to infer collusion that is never documented directly in the administrative record. Classical screens exploit the compression induced by bid-rotation agreements: colluding firms submit prearranged prices that cluster tightly around the designated winner, yielding low dispersion, few active bidders, and predictable rotation patterns \citep{porter1993detecting, pesendorfer2000study, bajari2003detecting, conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening}. A complementary and influential approach, recently advanced by \citet{kawai2023using}, focuses on the winning--losing threshold: under competition, the observable characteristics of narrowly winning and narrowly losing firms should be continuous at a bid margin of zero, and any statistically sharp discontinuity at the cutoff identifies an anti-competitive wedge. Both approaches have been validated primarily against cases in high-income economies with deep formal labor markets, dense bidder pools, and strong institutional capacity for antitrust enforcement; their extension to thinner institutional environments rests on a small but growing empirical base. \citet{wachs2019network} apply a co-bidding network approach to roughly 150{,}000 Georgian procurement notices and show that group-level cohesion and exclusivity flag candidate cartels in the absence of criminal ground truth. \citet{signor2020detection} exploit the dataset released by the Brazilian Federal Police's \emph{Operação Lava Jato} investigation to build a probabilistic detector of collusive tenders in the Petrobras construction-cartel case, reporting accuracy between 81 and 96 percent. \citet{huber2019machine} combine classical statistical screens with supervised machine-learning classifiers and correctly predict 84 percent of Swiss tenders out of sample, demonstrating the gains from combining bid-distribution screens with flexible functional forms. Recent work has begun extending detection beyond co-bidding patterns to exploit external network layers: \citet{asai2026ownership} use ownership networks to detect bid rigging in Singapore, and \citet{villamil2024collusion} build multiplex ownership--co-bidding networks for Swedish procurement. What has been missing is a network layer that captures \emph{operational} coordination---personnel flows, knowledge transfer, shared specialized labor---and a direct validation against criminal ground truth in a middle-income setting with structurally different institutional primitives. The S\~{a}o Paulo--CADE--RAIS linkage we build offers exactly that test, and is, to our knowledge, the first cartel-detection benchmark to integrate auction-level behavioral data, employer--employee matched labor records, and formal antitrust convictions. The policy stakes are first-order: middle-income competition authorities increasingly rely on off-the-shelf screens developed and validated abroad to triage investigations \citep{oecd2015, bosio2022public}, and if those screens systematically mislabel cartels under local conditions, existing enforcement pipelines may be misfiring in precisely the settings where deterrence capacity is weakest.

Our primary evaluation sample is a \emph{cartel-active} benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin pregão auctions in which at least one convicted firm participated during the active period of its cartel (details of sample construction in Section~\ref{data}).

Our first set of results documents the limited discriminative power of canonical screens in this institutional setting. On the unified cartel-active benchmark of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin pregão auctions ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$), classical variance screens fire in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model: bid dispersion and the coefficient of variation are weakly compressed at cartel-tainted auctions relative to clean controls (Cohen's $d \approx -0.07$ to $-0.10$), and the number of active bidders is modestly lower ($d \approx -0.24$), with Welch $t$-statistics that are statistically significant given the large control sample. Yet the effect sizes are small and the distributional overlap between cartel and clean auctions is near-total: the areas under the receiver-operating-characteristic curves (AUCs) for these screens range from $0.52$ (coefficient of variation) to $0.56$ (bidder count)---above chance but far too low for investigative triage. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirms the weak rank-order separation (rank-sum $p < 10^{-4}$ for each, aligning with the AUC direction). Classical screens are therefore directionally consistent with theory but operationally inadequate as cartel detectors in this setting: a triage tool built on these statistics would generate an unacceptable rate of false positives and false negatives relative to the conviction-anchored ground truth. The same qualitative pattern holds on the strict pair-matched sample of 14 auctions---canonical direction, low discriminative power, and mostly non-significant individual $t$-tests owing to small-$N$ constraints. A direct replication of the Kawai close-margin RD on the cartel-active subsample yields no statistically detectable discontinuity in incumbency or last-bid behavior at the cutoff, and a density test in the spirit of \citet{imhof2018screening} detects no manipulation of the running variable. The anytime-listed sensitivity sample of 107 auctions does produce positive $\Delta$ estimates on variance screens with $p<0.02$, but we interpret this as a mechanical consequence of the 93 out-of-window auctions in that sample---auctions in which firms that had been cartelists in another year are bidding against each other under competitive conditions---rather than as evidence that cartel auctions exhibit elevated dispersion. In short, while canonical variance-based screens exhibit weak directional signal, neither they nor the close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} achieve the discriminative power required to operationally detect the cartels that CADE has formally convicted.

Our second set of results shows that network-based screens constructed from administrative labor data recover strong and statistically robust cartel detection on the cartel-active benchmark. For each auction, we construct two network screens from the five-year window of RAIS records preceding the tender: the number of workers who have moved between the winner and the runner-up firms, and the Jaccard similarity of the two firms' historical workforces. On the cartel-active sample of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions, these screens yield AUCs of 0.750 and 0.739 respectively---outperforming the best classical screen by roughly 20 percentage points and representing, to our knowledge, the first robust AUCs reported for procurement cartel detection against a formal criminal ground truth. A third screen, built from the co-bidding network of shared tender participations, yields an AUC of 0.695, providing independent corroboration through a distinct information channel. Critically, the worker-flow signal is neither an artifact of sectoral composition nor a mechanical re-expression of co-bidding intensity. Restricting attention to firm pairs that share the same 2-digit sectoral classification reduces the worker-flow AUC only marginally, from 0.739 to 0.730, and the residual of worker-flow after partialling out the log co-bidding density of each pair achieves an AUC of 0.749---essentially unchanged from the raw baseline, indicating that worker mobility carries detection information above and beyond what co-bidding patterns alone can reveal. A more demanding test separates cartel-specific coordination from passive same-market labor circulation: we construct a \emph{specialization placebo} of non-cartel firm pairs bidding on the same BEC item code in the same year as each cartel-active auction, and compare worker-flow distributions across cartel, placebo, and global clean samples. Cartel pairs share at least one worker in 57.9\% of cases, compared with 31.1\% for same-market placebo pairs and 9.9\% for pairs in other markets. In a decomposition of the worker-flow AUC gain relative to the global clean baseline, roughly half is absorbed by the same-market specialization component and roughly half remains as a cartel-specific residual. Both components are consistent with our institutional-thinness mechanism: cartels inherit the labor-circulation patterns natural to thin specialized-labor markets and additionally reinforce those flows to sustain coordination. On the strict pair-matched sample ($N=10$ after close-margin filtering), worker-flow AUCs decline to near chance; we attribute this to power constraints at small sample sizes rather than to any breakdown in the underlying signal, and we emphasize that the cartel-active benchmark is where the discriminative power of any screen---classical or network-based---can be meaningfully evaluated. The conviction-anchored benchmark does not merely declare cartels detectable in S\~{a}o Paulo; it pinpoints the channel through which the detection signal propagates---worker mobility, not bid dispersion.

We interpret these findings through the lens of institutional thinness. In settings where the universe of firms capable of bidding on a given product is small and the formal-sector labor market for the specialized workers that serve those bids is shallow, coordination among colluding firms generates naturally detectable traces in worker flows: personnel rotations, key-employee transfers, and shared specialized labor become vehicles for, and therefore signatures of, the collusive communication that substitutes for the statistical-pattern coordination captured by canonical screens in thicker markets. This mechanism---which we develop formally in a simple model in Section~\ref{coll}---rationalizes both the low discriminative power of bid-dispersion screens and the success of network-based screens in the Brazilian setting. Critically, it also predicts the two-component decomposition of the worker-flow signal documented by our specialization placebo: because thin markets generate spontaneous labor circulation among same-market firms whether or not they are colluding, a non-cartel placebo restricted to same-market firm pairs should already exhibit elevated worker-flow overlap relative to random pairs; and cartel pairs should exhibit still greater overlap on top of that baseline. Both predictions are borne out in the data. The mechanism further predicts that classical screen-based enforcement strategies will systematically under-detect cartels in precisely the institutional environments where their deterrence value would be highest.

This paper makes three contributions. \emph{First}, conceptually, we advance the view that collusion leaves \emph{institution-specific} signatures: the effectiveness of a detection screen depends on which coordination technology is cheapest for cartel members to operate in the relevant institutional environment. In thick labor markets, collusion coordinates through bid patterns and classical screens fire; in thin specialized-labor markets, coordination piggybacks on personnel flows that classical screens cannot see. We formalize this in a simple model (Section~\ref{sec:model}) and confirm its central prediction empirically: the gap between classical and worker-flow screens widens from 10~percentage points in thick markets to 33~percentage points in thin markets. This framing organizes and partially reconciles the growing patchwork of contradictory findings in the detection literature \citep{conley2016detecting, imhof2018screening, kawai2023using}. \emph{Second}, substantively, we introduce conviction-anchored detection benchmarks by linking CADE rulings, BEC bid-level records, and RAIS employer--employee data, and we show that worker-flow and co-bidding network screens constructed from these sources substantially outperform classical screens in detecting convicted cartels. Because such data are increasingly available to national competition authorities in middle-income economies, the screens we propose are directly deployable at negligible marginal cost. \emph{Third}, methodologically, we deliver the first stress test of canonical cartel-detection frameworks against an independent criminal ground truth in a middle-income setting, documenting that classical variance-based screens \citep{porter1993detecting, bajari2003detecting} and the close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} yield AUCs of only $0.52$--$0.56$---directionally correct but operationally inadequate---while network screens achieve $0.75$.
