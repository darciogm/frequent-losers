\title{\textbf{Beneath the Surface: Catching Cartels in the Act in Brazilian Procurement\tnoteref{tfoot}}}

\begin{abstract}
Brazil's antitrust authority has secured administrative convictions against 35 firms in seven distinct procurement cartels operating in the state of S\~{a}o Paulo between 2009 and 2019. We match these firms and the product markets and time windows delineated in the administrative case files to 3.8~million bids placed on the state's electronic procurement platform and to the universe of matched employer--employee records, assembling the first conviction-anchored benchmark that combines auction behavior, labor microdata, and antitrust rulings. On the benchmark sample of roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions in which at least one convicted firm participated during the active period of its cartel, the canonical variance-based screens of \citet{porter1993detecting} and \citet{bajari2003detecting} fire in the direction predicted by the classical bid-rotation model---bid dispersion, the coefficient of variation of bids, and the number of active bidders are compressed at cartel-tainted auctions---yet their areas under the ROC curve lie between 0.46 and 0.48, statistically indistinguishable from chance as discriminators between cartel and non-cartel auctions. A close-margin regression discontinuity in the spirit of \citet{kawai2023using} likewise detects no discontinuity in incumbency at the cutoff. By contrast, a single alternative screen, constructed from inter-firm worker flows in the matched employer--employee data, achieves an area under the ROC curve of 0.75 on the same benchmark; the signal survives conditioning on sector and on co-bidding intensity. A specialization placebo restricted to non-cartel firm pairs bidding on the same BEC item code in the same year as each cartel-active auction absorbs approximately half of the worker-flow signal, leaving a statistically robust cartel-specific residual---consistent with a view in which cartels both inherit and reinforce the personnel flows natural to thin specialized-labor markets. We rationalize these findings through a simple model in which the cheapest coordination technology available to cartel members depends on institutional primitives: in markets with thin specialized-labor pools, collusion leaves traces in personnel flows rather than in bid patterns. Canonical variance screens are directionally consistent with theory but operationally useless in this setting; worker-flow data recover the signal cleanly. For competition authorities with access to employer--employee microdata, worker-flow screens offer a deployable complement to canonical bid-pattern tests.
\end{abstract}
\end{frontmatter}

%In this study, we adopt a Regression Discontinuity design, following the methodology of \cite{kawai2023using}, to investigate 3.8~million public procurement auctions in São Paulo, Brazil. We aim to detect bid rotation and incumbency trends as indicators of anti-competitive behavior by analyzing auctions with narrowly winning and narrowly losing bids. Our findings reveal an incumbency advantage but no definitive evidence of bid rotation. This indicates that political favoritism or corruption, rather than firm collusion, may influence the auction outcomes.
