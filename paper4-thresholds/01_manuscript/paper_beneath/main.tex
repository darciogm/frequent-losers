\begin{abstract}
Brazil's antitrust authority convicted 35 firms across seven procurement cartels in S\~{a}o Paulo between 2009 and 2019. We link these firms to 3.8~million bids on the state's electronic platform and to the universe of matched employer--employee records, assembling what is, to our knowledge, the first detection benchmark to combine auction behavior, labor microdata, and formal antitrust rulings.

The benchmark exposes a sharp divide. On roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions involving at least one convicted firm, canonical variance screens behave as bid-rotation theory predicts---dispersion and bidder counts are compressed---yet the areas under the ROC curve ($0.52$--$0.56$) are too low to be operationally useful. The close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} likewise yields a null. Worker-flow screens tell a different story: counting the employees who have moved between the winning and runner-up firms produces AUCs of $0.75$ and $0.74$, outperforming every classical screen by roughly 20~percentage points. A co-bidding density measure reaches $0.70$. Crucially, the worker-flow advantage is not uniform: in thin labor markets the gap reaches 33~percentage points; in thick markets it narrows to 10. A specialization placebo shows that about half the signal reflects the natural personnel circulation of thin markets, while the other half is a cartel-specific residual.

The pattern points to an institutional logic. Where specialized-labor pools are shallow, the cheapest way to coordinate a cartel runs through people, not through prices---and the traces show up in employment records that canonical bid screens never see. For competition authorities with access to employer--employee microdata, worker-flow screens offer a practical, low-cost complement to the toolkit they already have.
\end{abstract}
\end{frontmatter}

%In this study, we adopt a Regression Discontinuity design, following the methodology of \cite{kawai2023using}, to investigate 3.8~million public procurement auctions in São Paulo, Brazil. We aim to detect bid rotation and incumbency trends as indicators of anti-competitive behavior by analyzing auctions with narrowly winning and narrowly losing bids. Our findings reveal an incumbency advantage but no definitive evidence of bid rotation. This indicates that political favoritism or corruption, rather than firm collusion, may influence the auction outcomes.

\subsection{Bandwidth sensitivity}\label{app:bandwidth}

Table~\ref{tab:bw} reports AUCs for all screens across six bandwidth choices for the close-margin envelope. The benchmark bandwidth of $\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$ used throughout the paper is neither the most nor the least favorable for the worker-flow screens. At narrower bandwidths ($0.02$, $0.05$), worker-flow AUCs are marginally higher ($0.76$, $0.75$) but sample sizes are smaller; at wider bandwidths ($0.20$, $0.50$), AUCs decline modestly ($0.74$, $0.72$) as the sample dilutes toward the full population. Classical screen AUCs follow the same monotonic pattern. The gap between the best classical screen (number of bidders) and the worker-flow screens remains stable at approximately 18--20 percentage points across all bandwidths.
