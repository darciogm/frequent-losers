\begin{abstract}
Brazil's antitrust authority convicted 35 firms across seven procurement cartels in S\~{a}o Paulo between 2009 and 2019. We link these firms to 2.6~million competitive auctions on the state's electronic platform and to the universe of matched employer--employee records, assembling the first detection benchmark to combine auction data, labor microdata, and formal antitrust rulings. Six of the seven cartels have auctions in our close-margin evaluation sample.

On roughly 2{,}300 close-margin auctions involving at least one convicted firm, canonical variance screens yield AUCs of $0.46$--$0.56$ in the aggregate---but this null conceals a sharp split by coordination technology. On bid-rigging cartels, classical screens achieve AUCs of $0.65$; on the hub-and-spoke cartel that dominates the sample ($85\%$), they are at chance. The close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} produces a null on both types. Worker-flow screens---counting employees who have worked at both the winning and runner-up firms---appear far more powerful, with an AUC of $0.75$.

That headline result does not survive temporal conditioning. When we restrict worker flows to those observed \emph{before} each auction, the AUC falls to $0.50$---indistinguishable from chance. A temporal decomposition reveals where the signal actually lives: not in pre-existing personnel connections that might reflect coordination, but in post-conduct labor reallocation. After cartel conduct periods end, workers flow from convicted firms to their rivals at a ratio that rises from $1.4{:}1$ to $4{:}1$ over the panel, consistent with enforcement-driven displacement rather than collusive coordination. The post-conduct component alone produces an AUC of $0.77$; the pre-conduct component produces exactly $0.50$.

The temporal finding carries two broader implications. First, detection benchmarks built from panel data are vulnerable to look-ahead bias: network screens that use the full employment history mechanically absorb enforcement aftermaths and overstate prospective detection power. This caveat applies to any screen---worker-flow, ownership, or board-interlock---evaluated against conviction-anchored ground truth without temporal conditioning. Second, the temporal pattern is itself informative: cartel enforcement in thin labor markets triggers a measurable reallocation of human capital from convicted firms to competitors, a real effect that matched employer--employee records are uniquely positioned to document.
\end{abstract}
\end{frontmatter}

%In this study, we adopt a Regression Discontinuity design, following the methodology of \cite{kawai2023using}, to investigate 3.8~million public procurement auctions in São Paulo, Brazil. We aim to detect bid rotation and incumbency trends as indicators of anti-competitive behavior by analyzing auctions with narrowly winning and narrowly losing bids. Our findings reveal an incumbency advantage but no definitive evidence of bid rotation. This indicates that political favoritism or corruption, rather than firm collusion, may influence the auction outcomes.

\subsection{Bandwidth sensitivity}\label{app:bandwidth}

Table~\ref{tab:bw} reports AUCs for all screens across six bandwidth choices for the close-margin envelope. The benchmark bandwidth of $\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$ used throughout the paper is neither the most nor the least favorable for the worker-flow screens. At narrower bandwidths ($0.02$, $0.05$), worker-flow AUCs are marginally higher ($0.76$, $0.75$) but sample sizes are smaller; at wider bandwidths ($0.20$, $0.50$), AUCs decline modestly ($0.74$, $0.72$) as the sample dilutes toward the full population. Classical screen AUCs follow the same monotonic pattern. Note that all worker-flow AUCs in this table use the full 2009--2017 RAIS panel; as shown in Section~\ref{sec:temporal}, these AUCs do not survive temporal conditioning.
