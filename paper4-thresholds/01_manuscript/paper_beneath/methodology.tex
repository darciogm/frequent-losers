\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{coll}

We bring four detection approaches to the conviction-anchored benchmark: a close-margin regression discontinuity (Section~\ref{sec:rdd}), classical bid-distribution screens (Section~\ref{sec:classical}), network screens built from worker-flow and co-bidding data (Section~\ref{sec:network}), and a specialization placebo that separates market-level labor circulation from cartel-specific coordination (Section~\ref{sec:placebo}). Every screen is evaluated by its area under the ROC curve (Section~\ref{sec:auc}). A simple model in Section~\ref{sec:model} ties the pieces together.

\subsection{Close-margin regression discontinuity}\label{sec:rdd}

Following \citet{kawai2023using}, we focus on auctions decided by a hair. Define the margin of victory in auction $a$ as
\[
MV_a \;=\; \frac{b^{\text{runner-up}}_a - b^{\text{win}}_a}{b^{\text{win}}_a}\,.
\]
When $MV_a$ is close to zero, the winner barely underbid the nearest rival. Under genuine competition, narrowly winning and narrowly losing firms should look alike on pre-determined characteristics; a sharp discontinuity at the cutoff signals something non-competitive.

We estimate the jump using local-polynomial regression with a data-driven bandwidth. Two outcomes carry interpretive weight. \emph{Incumbency}---whether the firm won the previous auction in the same market---tests for bid rotation: a jump would indicate that designated winners are systematically past winners. \emph{Last bidder}---whether the firm submitted the final bid---tests for strategic timing. A density test on the running variable checks whether the margin itself is being manipulated.

\subsection{Classical bid-distribution screens}\label{sec:classical}

We compute four screens on firm-best bids (one bid per firm per auction, keeping the lowest when multiple rounds exist):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Coefficient of variation} ($CV_a$): standard deviation divided by mean. Bid rotation predicts lower $CV$ because cover bids cluster near the designated winner \citep{porter1993detecting, abrantesmetz2006variance}.

\item \textbf{Bid spread} ($S_a$): $(\max - \min)/\text{mean}$. Like $CV$, compressed under cover bidding.

\item \textbf{Margin of victory} ($MV_a$): the designated winner should undercut cover bids by just enough to win, yielding a small margin.

\item \textbf{Number of bidders} ($N_a$): entry deterrence or market division should reduce the count \citep{marshall2012economics}.
\end{enumerate}

For each, we compare cartel-tainted auctions to clean controls on the close-margin benchmark ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$), reporting the Welch $t$-statistic, Cohen's $d$, the Wilcoxon rank-sum $p$-value, and the AUC.

\subsection{Network-based screens}\label{sec:network}

We step outside the bid data entirely and build three screens from administrative sources that CADE analysts do not routinely examine.

\noindent\textbf{Worker-flow screens.} For each (winner, runner-up) pair we measure labor connectivity from the full 2009--2017 RAIS panel:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shared workers} ($W_{ij}$): the count of distinct PIS numbers appearing at both firms during the nine-year window.

\item \textbf{Jaccard similarity} ($J_{ij} = W_{ij} / (N_i + N_j - W_{ij})$): normalizes the count by firm size, so that large employers are not mechanically flagged.
\end{itemize}

The logic is straightforward. If cartel coordination in thin labor markets runs through personnel---managers carrying pricing information, technicians rotating between member firms---then the employment records should show it \citep{stoyanov2012worker, serafinelli2019good}.

\noindent\textbf{Co-bidding screen.} For each pair we count the auctions in which both firms submitted bids ($C_{ij}$). This provides a network channel built entirely within the procurement data, serving as a benchmark against which the value-added of the external worker-flow layer can be measured.

\subsection{Detection evaluation: AUC}\label{sec:auc}

We use the area under the ROC curve to compare screens. The AUC is simply the probability that a randomly chosen cartel auction scores more ``cartel-like'' than a randomly chosen clean auction. For screens where lower is more suspicious ($CV$, $S$, $MV$, $N$), we negate scores so that AUC $> 0.5$ always means detection in the predicted direction. We compute the AUC via the Mann--Whitney $U$ statistic with average-rank tie-handling:
\[
\text{AUC} \;=\; \frac{U_1}{n_1 \times n_0}\,,
\]
where $n_1$ and $n_0$ are the cartel and clean sample sizes. All screens are evaluated on the same unified close-margin benchmark, so comparisons are apples-to-apples. Appendix~\ref{app:bandwidth} shows that results are stable across bandwidths from $0.02$ to $0.50$.

\subsection{Specialization placebo}\label{sec:placebo}

Elevated worker flows between a cartel firm and its rival could mean two very different things: that the firms collude through personnel, or that they simply draw from the same thin labor pool. We need to tell them apart.

We build a three-way decomposition. The \textbf{cartel} sample consists of auction-level (winner, runner-up) pairs in which at least one firm is cartel-active ($N \approx 2{,}300$ for preg\~{a}o). The \textbf{placebo} consists of non-cartel pairs bidding on the same BEC item code in the same year---firms facing the same product-market labor pool but not colluding. The \textbf{clean} baseline is every other non-cartel pair.

If the entire worker-flow signal were market specialization, the placebo should look like the cartel sample. If there is a cartel-specific coordination channel on top, cartel pairs should show still more worker overlap than same-market non-cartel pairs. We measure the split by comparing AUCs: the fraction of the total gain (cartel vs.\ clean, minus $0.5$) absorbed by the placebo is the specialization share; the remainder is the cartel residual.

\subsection{A simple model of institution-dependent coordination}\label{sec:model}

Why should screen effectiveness depend on the market? Consider $n \ge 2$ firms that can form a cartel. They need to coordinate on a winning bid for each auction. Two technologies are available:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bid-pattern coordination} (cost $c_b$): arrange cover bids around the designated winner's price. Leaves traces in $CV$, spread, and bidder counts---exactly what classical screens are designed to catch.

\item \textbf{Personnel-flow coordination} (cost $c_w(L)$, decreasing in the thickness $L$ of the specialized-labor pool): coordinate through transfers, rotations, or shared employment of key staff. Leaves traces in worker-flow statistics but not necessarily in the bid distribution.
\end{itemize}

Rational cartel members pick the cheaper channel. In thick markets (large $L$), $c_w > c_b$: collusion runs through bids, and classical screens fire. In thin markets (small $L$), $c_w < c_b$: collusion runs through people, and the bid distribution looks clean.

Three testable predictions follow:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Thickness gradient.} In thin markets, classical AUCs should be near chance while worker-flow AUCs should be above chance. In thick markets, the gap should narrow as classical screens recover.

\item \textbf{Placebo decomposition.} Thin labor pools produce spontaneous inter-firm worker circulation even without collusion. A same-market placebo should show elevated worker overlap relative to random pairs (specialization), and cartel pairs should show more still (coordination). Both components should be present.

\item \textbf{Symmetry.} Because personnel-flow coordination operates through the shared labor pool of the market---not through the outcome of any particular auction---the worker-flow signal should not depend on whether the cartel firm wins or loses.
\end{enumerate}

The model is deliberately spare. It abstracts from repeated-game dynamics, leniency, the possibility that firms use both channels simultaneously, and heterogeneity in $c_b$ across auction formats. We offer it not as a structure to be estimated but as a lens for reading the results and for specifying when the classical-vs.-network comparison is most informative.
