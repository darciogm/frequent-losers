\section{Empirical Strategy}\label{coll}

We deploy four complementary detection approaches on the conviction-anchored benchmark described in Section~\ref{data}: a close-margin regression discontinuity (Section~\ref{sec:rdd}), classical bid-distribution screens (Section~\ref{sec:classical}), network-based screens constructed from worker-flow and co-bidding data (Section~\ref{sec:network}), and a specialization placebo that decomposes the network signal into market-specialization and cartel-specific components (Section~\ref{sec:placebo}). We evaluate each screen's discriminative power using the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (Section~\ref{sec:auc}). Section~\ref{sec:model} sketches a simple model linking the informativeness of each screen family to institutional primitives.

\subsection{Close-margin regression discontinuity}\label{sec:rdd}

Following \citet{kawai2023using}, we exploit the winning--losing threshold in close-margin pregão auctions. Let $MV_{a}$ denote the margin of victory in auction $a$, defined as the percentage difference between the runner-up bid and the winning bid:
\[
MV_a \;=\; \frac{b^{\text{runner-up}}_a - b^{\text{win}}_a}{b^{\text{win}}_a}\,.
\]
The running variable is $MV_a$, with a cutoff at zero: auctions with $MV_a \approx 0$ are those in which the winner barely underbid the closest rival. Under competitive conditions, the observable pre-determined characteristics of narrowly winning and narrowly losing firms should be continuous at $MV_a = 0$. Any statistically sharp discontinuity at the cutoff identifies a non-competitive wedge---either collusion or external favoritism.

We estimate the discontinuity using local-polynomial regression with the bandwidth selector of \citet{imhof2018screening}. The two primary outcomes are \emph{incumbency}---an indicator equal to one if the firm won the immediately preceding auction in the same market---and \emph{last bidder}---an indicator for whether the firm submitted the final bid in the auction. A positive jump in incumbency at $MV = 0$ is consistent with bid rotation (the designated winner is systematically a past winner); a jump in last-bidder status is consistent with strategic bid timing. We supplement the RD estimates with a density test on the running variable, following \citet{imhof2018screening}, to assess whether there is manipulation of the bid margin itself---a prerequisite for interpreting discontinuities causally.

\subsection{Classical bid-distribution screens}\label{sec:classical}

We implement four classical screens computed at the auction level on firm-best bids (one bid per firm, retaining the firm's lowest bid when multiple rounds exist):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Coefficient of variation} ($CV_a$): the standard deviation of firm-best bids divided by their mean. Under bid rotation with cover bidding, $CV_a$ is predicted to be lower at cartel-tainted auctions because bids cluster around the designated winner's price \citep{porter1993detecting, abrantesmetz2006variance}.

\item \textbf{Bid spread} ($S_a$): the range of firm-best bids normalized by their mean, $S_a = (\max - \min)/\text{mean}$. Like $CV_a$, the spread is predicted to be compressed under cover bidding.

\item \textbf{Margin of victory} ($MV_a$): defined above. Under bid rotation, the winning margin is predicted to be \emph{small} because the designated winner undercuts cover bids by only the minimum necessary to win.

\item \textbf{Number of active bidders} ($N_a$): the count of distinct firms submitting bids. Collusive entry deterrence or market-division agreements predict \emph{fewer} bidders at cartel-tainted auctions \citep{marshall2012economics}.
\end{enumerate}

Each screen is evaluated on the cartel-active benchmark ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$), comparing the distribution of cartel-tainted auctions to clean controls. We report the Welch $t$-statistic for the mean difference, Cohen's $d$ for effect-size calibration, the Wilcoxon rank-sum $p$-value for rank-order separation, and the AUC (Section~\ref{sec:auc}).

\subsection{Network-based screens}\label{sec:network}

We construct three screens from administrative data sources external to the bid records themselves.

\noindent\textbf{Worker-flow screens.} For each auction-level (winner, runner-up) firm pair, we measure inter-firm labor connectivity using the five-year window of RAIS employer--employee records preceding the tender year. Two variants capture different aspects of labor proximity:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shared workers} ($W_{ij}$): the number of distinct workers (identified by their PIS number) who appear in the employment records of both firm $i$ and firm $j$ at any point during the five-year window. A higher count indicates denser personnel flows between the two firms.

\item \textbf{Jaccard similarity} ($J_{ij}$): the ratio $J_{ij} = W_{ij} / (N_i + N_j - W_{ij})$, where $N_i$ and $N_j$ are the total number of distinct workers employed by each firm over the window. The Jaccard index normalizes the shared-worker count by the size of each firm's workforce, ensuring that the screen is not mechanically driven by firm size.
\end{itemize}

The hypothesis is that cartel coordination leaves detectable traces in personnel flows: managers, salespeople, or technical staff who rotate between colluding firms serve as informal communication channels \citep{stoyanov2012worker, serafinelli2019good}. Under the institutional-thinness mechanism we develop below, the worker-flow signal should be strongest in markets with thin specialized-labor pools.

\noindent\textbf{Co-bidding screen.} For each (winner, runner-up) pair, we count the number of auctions in which both firms submitted bids over the full sample period ($C_{ij}$). Firms that co-bid frequently may be coordinating market presence. The co-bidding screen provides an independent network channel constructed entirely within the procurement data, serving as a comparator for the worker-flow screens, which are constructed from a wholly external source.

\subsection{Detection evaluation: AUC}\label{sec:auc}

For each screen $s$, we evaluate discriminative power by computing the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC). The AUC is the probability that a randomly drawn cartel-tainted auction scores higher (more cartel-like) on screen $s$ than a randomly drawn clean auction. For screens where lower values are predicted under collusion ($CV$, $S$, $MV$, $N$), we negate the scores before computing the AUC, so that a value above 0.5 always indicates detection in the predicted direction. An AUC of 0.5 corresponds to chance (no separation), and an AUC of 1.0 to perfect separation.

We compute the AUC via the Mann--Whitney $U$ statistic with average-rank tie-handling:
\[
\text{AUC} \;=\; \frac{U_1}{n_1 \times n_0}\,,
\]
where $U_1$ is the sum of ranks of cartel-tainted auctions minus $n_1(n_1+1)/2$, and $n_1$ and $n_0$ are the cartel and clean sample sizes, respectively. All screens---classical and network-based---are evaluated on the same unified close-margin benchmark ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$), ensuring apples-to-apples comparability. Appendix~\ref{app:bandwidth} reports AUCs at six alternative bandwidths ($0.02$--$0.50$); results are stable across the full range.

\subsection{Specialization placebo}\label{sec:placebo}

The worker-flow AUC may reflect two distinct channels: (i)~\emph{market specialization}---firms operating in the same thin product market share workers because the pool of specialized labor is small, regardless of whether they collude---and (ii)~\emph{cartel-specific coordination}---colluding firms engage in additional personnel transfers to sustain communication and monitoring. To disentangle these channels, we construct a three-way sample decomposition:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cartel}: auction-level (winner, runner-up) pairs in which at least one firm is cartel-active, drawn from the close-margin benchmark ($N \approx 2{,}300$ for pregão).
\item \textbf{Placebo (same market)}: non-cartel firm pairs bidding on the same BEC item code in the same year as each cartel-active auction. These pairs face the same product-market labor pool as cartel pairs but are not colluding (by the ground-truth definition).
\item \textbf{Clean (global)}: all remaining non-cartel pairs in the close-margin benchmark.
\end{enumerate}

If the entire worker-flow signal were driven by market specialization, the placebo pairs should exhibit the same AUC as cartel pairs (relative to the global clean baseline). If instead there is a cartel-specific residual, cartel pairs should exhibit \emph{higher} worker-flow overlap than same-market placebo pairs. In a linear decomposition of the total AUC gain (cartel vs.\ clean minus 0.5), we attribute the fraction absorbed by the placebo to market specialization and the remainder to cartel-specific coordination.

\subsection{A simple model of institution-dependent coordination}\label{sec:model}

We sketch a parsimonious framework to organize the empirical predictions. Consider a market with $n \ge 2$ firms that can form a cartel. The cartel must coordinate on a winning bid for each auction. Two coordination technologies are available:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Bid-pattern coordination} (cost $c_b$): the cartel arranges bids so that cover bids cluster around the designated winner's price. This technology leaves traces in bid-distribution statistics ($CV$, spread, bidder counts) and is the mechanism assumed by classical screens.

\item \textbf{Personnel-flow coordination} (cost $c_w(L)$, decreasing in the thickness $L$ of the specialized-labor pool): the cartel coordinates through the transfer or rotation of key personnel---managers, salespeople, or technical staff---between member firms. This technology leaves traces in worker-flow statistics but not necessarily in bid distributions.
\end{itemize}

Each cartel chooses the cheapest available technology. In thick markets (large $L$), $c_w(L) > c_b$, and the cartel coordinates through bids: classical screens fire. In thin markets (small $L$), $c_w(L) < c_b$, and the cartel coordinates through personnel: worker-flow screens fire, but classical screens may not, because the bid distribution is not distorted by the coordination mechanism.

This framework generates three testable predictions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Direction}: In thin-labor markets, classical screen AUCs should be near chance; worker-flow AUCs should be above chance. The reverse holds in thick-labor markets.

\item \textbf{Placebo decomposition}: Because thin specialized-labor pools generate spontaneous inter-firm labor circulation even among non-colluding firms, a same-market placebo should exhibit elevated worker-flow overlap relative to random pairs (the specialization component). Cartel pairs should exhibit still greater overlap on top of that baseline (the coordination component). Both components should be present in thin-market data.

\item \textbf{Policy implication}: Enforcement strategies that rely exclusively on classical bid-pattern screens will systematically under-detect cartels in thin-labor environments, precisely where deterrence capacity may be weakest.
\end{enumerate}

The model is deliberately stylized: it abstracts from dynamic considerations (repeated games, leniency incentives), from the possibility that firms use both technologies simultaneously, and from heterogeneity in $c_b$ across auction formats. The framework is offered not as a structural model to be estimated but as an organizing device for interpreting our empirical findings and for specifying the conditions under which the classical-vs.-network screen comparison is most informative.
