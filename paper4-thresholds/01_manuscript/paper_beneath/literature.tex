\section{Related Literature}\label{literature}

Our paper sits at the intersection of three literatures: the design and validation of cartel-detection screens, the use of network structure to identify collusion, and the economics of inter-firm worker mobility.

\subsection{Detection through bid-pattern screens}

The idea that bid-rotation agreements leave statistical footprints is now well established. \citet{porter1993detecting} showed that collusive phases of the 1880s railroad freight cartel exhibit systematically lower bid dispersion than competitive phases, exploiting the cartel's own internal records as an exogenous regime switch. \citet{pesendorfer2000study} documented bid rotation in Florida school-milk procurement; \citet{bajari2003detecting} proposed exchangeability tests for bidder costs in Midwestern highway paving; \citet{conley2016detecting} extended pair-level correlation tests to Italian procurement. \citet{imhof2018screening} demonstrated that combining multiple screens in a portfolio outperforms any single indicator in Swiss data, and \citet{abrantesmetz2006variance} offered practical guidance on implementing variance screens while cautioning against single-indicator reliance. \citet{harrington2008detecting} synthesized the design principles, emphasizing a point our findings will make concrete: the informativeness of any screen depends on the competitive counterfactual \emph{and} the coordination technology the cartel actually uses.

A complementary strand focuses on the bid margin itself. \citet{kawai2023using} proposed a regression discontinuity centered on the winning--losing threshold in Japanese construction procurement: under genuine competition, narrowly winning and narrowly losing firms should look alike, and any sharp discontinuity identifies a non-competitive wedge. \citet{chassang2019collusion} developed a theoretical framework in which bid constraints endogenously shape the collusive technology---predicting, among other things, that format-specific screens may outperform generic variance tests, a prediction consistent with the format heterogeneity we document below.

A common thread across these contributions is that nearly all validation exercises rely on ground truth from high-income economies with deep bidder pools and well-resourced enforcement agencies. \citet{levenstein2006determines} documented across 81 international cartels that market structure, transparency, and enforcement capacity jointly determine cartel success---suggesting that screens validated in one institutional regime need not travel well to another. Direct validation against criminal ground truth in a middle-income setting with structurally different labor markets remains scarce, and that is the gap our conviction-anchored benchmark is designed to fill.

\subsection{Network-based and machine-learning approaches}

A parallel literature has moved beyond univariate bid statistics to exploit the relational structure of procurement markets. \citet{wachs2019network} showed that co-bidding networks in Georgian procurement flag candidate cartels through group-level cohesion and exclusivity, even without criminal ground truth. \citet{signor2020detection} built a probabilistic detector from the data released by Brazil's \emph{Operação Lava Jato}, reporting 81--96~percent accuracy on the Petrobras construction cartel. \citet{huber2019machine} combined classical screens with supervised machine-learning classifiers in Swiss data, correctly predicting 84~percent of collusive tenders out of sample. \citet{clark2018bid} documented how collusive networks in Québec construction co-evolve with political connections, illustrating the interplay between bid rigging and corruption.

A more recent wave extends the network logic to data sources \emph{outside} the bid record. \citet{asai2026ownership} link shareholder registries to procurement auctions in Singapore and show that common-ownership links predict identical bids; removing the ownership-network effect would cut government spending by 4--6~percent. \citet{villamil2024collusion} build temporal multiplex networks that combine ownership ties and co-bidding patterns in Swedish procurement, finding that denser ownership connections predict higher single-bidding and more missing bidders. Together, these papers establish that external network layers---corporate ownership, in their case---carry detection signal that co-bidding statistics alone cannot reach.

We add a third layer: labor-market connectivity, constructed from matched employer--employee records. Worker-flow networks differ from ownership networks in two ways that matter for detection. They capture \emph{operational} coordination---personnel rotation, knowledge transfer, shared technical staff---rather than financial ties, and thus speak to a distinct coordination channel. And they are available wherever governments maintain compulsory employer--employee registries (RAIS in Brazil, DADS in France, IAB in Germany), making the approach portable without new data collection. Because worker-flow data are informationally independent of both the bid record and the corporate registry, the risk of circularity between the screen and the behavior it targets is lower than for co-bidding or ownership-based approaches.

\subsection{Worker mobility as an inter-firm channel}

Labor economists have long recognized that workers carry knowledge between firms. \citet{stoyanov2012worker} showed that productivity gains spill across Danish firms through the mobility of their employees, with larger spillovers when the productivity gap between sender and receiver is wide. \citet{serafinelli2019good} documented a similar pattern in Italy's Veneto region: workers arriving from high-productivity firms raise wages and output at their new employers. \citet{jarosch2021learning} developed an equilibrium model in which team composition and worker reallocation jointly shape firm-level human capital. The upshot is that worker flows are not just a labor-market phenomenon---they are an active conduit for inter-firm information transmission.

Three recent papers connect this conduit to collusion. \citet{lamichhane2024collusion} provide experimental evidence that, in Bertrand markets, worker mobility between firms facilitates collusive pricing: workers carry pricing-strategy information, and firms that exchange employees coordinate on higher prices and more market sharing, especially in concentrated markets. \citet{herrera2025collusion} study product-market collusion facilitated by shared directors and executives. They find that common leadership raises the probability of collusion by 11~percentage points---and, strikingly, that inter-firm worker flows \emph{fall} by roughly 20\% once a board interlock is established. The substitution is telling: when collusion runs through leadership ties, personnel-flow coordination becomes redundant. Our institutional-thinness model predicts the mirror image: when leadership ties are absent and coordination relies on operational personnel exchange, worker-flow screens should carry the signal that ownership and board-interlock screens miss.

On the data-infrastructure side, two papers have already linked RAIS to Brazilian procurement---though not for detection. \citet{barbosa2020revolving} track worker movements between public agencies and private suppliers, showing that supplier-to-administration revolving doors are detrimental to procurement efficiency. \citet{szerman2023employee} exploits a debarment-policy change to estimate the worker-level costs of anticorruption enforcement, finding that blacklisted firms lose nearly half their employees. Both papers prove the RAIS--procurement linkage is feasible and productive; neither uses it as a cartel screen.

We bring these threads together. \citet{lamichhane2024collusion} establish the mechanism---worker mobility as a collusion channel. \citet{asai2026ownership} and \citet{villamil2024collusion} show that external network layers carry detection signal. \citet{barbosa2020revolving} and \citet{szerman2023employee} demonstrate the data infrastructure. What has been missing is the step from mechanism to screen: constructing a labor-network detection tool, validating it against formal convictions, and embedding it in a framework that predicts \emph{when} worker-flow screens should outperform the alternatives. That is what this paper provides.
