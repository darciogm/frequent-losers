\section{Institutional Background}\label{hist}

\subsection{Public procurement in S\~{a}o Paulo: the BEC platform}

S\~{a}o Paulo's state government purchases common goods and services through a decentralized network of roughly 1{,}300 public buyer units (PBUs), all of which have been required since 2007 to transact on the state's electronic platform, the \emph{Bolsa Eletr\^{o}nica de Compras} (BEC). The scale is substantial: in 2018 alone, BEC handled approximately US\$1.7~billion in procurement, and over the study period the platform processed more than R\$7.4~billion for the State Health Secretariat and other agencies.

The BEC catalog organizes items by group and class---from pharmaceuticals to office supplies---totaling about 6{,}350 distinct product codes. Each tender, called a purchase offer (PO), may contain multiple items; each item is auctioned separately, so a unique procurement event is defined by a (PO $\times$ item code) pair, which we label POI. The platform records everything a researcher needs: the reference price set by the buyer, every bid submitted, the identities of bidders and the winning firm, and the auction format.

Two formats predominate. In the \emph{convite} (sealed-bid tender), firms submit confidential bids by a deadline; the lowest qualified bid wins. This format is typically used for purchases below R\$176{,}000 (about US\$35{,}000). In the \emph{preg\~{a}o eletr\^{o}nico} (descending auction), firms first submit sealed proposals, after which an open phase begins: bidders observe the current lowest price in real time and can underbid for an initial 20~minutes, with automatic 4-minute extensions whenever a late bid arrives. The auction closes after 4~consecutive minutes of silence. The preg\~{a}o carries no value ceiling and accounts for the majority of BEC spending.

The distinction matters for collusion. The preg\~{a}o's iterative, anonymous bidding gives cartel members the ability to monitor compliance in real time but also exposes deviations; the convite's single-shot format makes monitoring harder but bid coordination simpler. Our primary analysis focuses on preg\~{a}o auctions, for which the close-margin RD framework is most naturally motivated; convite results are reported as a complementary check.

\subsection{Antitrust enforcement: CADE and the ground truth}

Brazil's federal competition authority, the \emph{Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econ\^{o}mica} (CADE), is an autonomous agency within the Ministry of Justice. Its Tribunal renders administrative decisions---binding fines and behavioral remedies, not criminal sentences in the judicial sense. Cases typically begin with a complaint or a leniency application (\emph{Termo de Compromisso de Cessa\c{c}\~{a}o}, TCC), proceed through a formal investigation by the \emph{Superintend\^{e}ncia-Geral}, and conclude with a Tribunal vote.

Our ground truth draws on CADE proceedings in which the Tribunal convicted firms of cartel conduct in S\~{a}o Paulo procurement markets. We searched CADE's public repository for proceedings with final decisions that (i)~name specific firms with identifiable CNPJs, (ii)~specify the product market, and (iii)~delineate a conduct period. Seven cartels meet these criteria, spanning sectors as varied as pharmaceuticals, school meals, trash bags, solar water heaters, and metropolitan rail, and involving 35 firms whose CNPJ roots we can match to BEC records.

What makes these case files particularly useful is that they specify not just \emph{who} colluded but \emph{when} and \emph{in which market}. We exploit this granularity to label individual auctions as cartel-tainted---matching each convicted firm's CNPJ root, the CADE-specified conduct window, and the relevant product code to BEC tenders. The result is a benchmark that captures both the identity and timing dimensions of collusion, rather than treating every auction involving a flagged firm as equally suspect.
