\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{concl}

This paper assembles a conviction-anchored benchmark---seven CADE-convicted procurement cartels linked to 3.8~million bids and the universe of formal employment in S\~{a}o Paulo---and uses it to ask which detection tools actually work. Three findings stand out.

The first is a warning. Canonical variance screens behave as bid-rotation theory predicts---dispersion and bidder counts are compressed at cartel-tainted auctions---but the effect sizes are small and the distributional overlap with clean auctions is near-total. AUCs top out at $0.56$. The close-margin RD of \citet{kawai2023using} produces a null. Neither design is internally flawed; both simply lack the statistical punch to separate cartels from noise in this institutional setting.

The second finding is constructive. Worker-flow screens built from matched employer--employee records achieve AUCs of $0.75$, outperforming the best classical screen by roughly 20~percentage points. A co-bidding density measure reaches $0.70$. A specialization placebo attributes about half the worker-flow signal to the natural personnel circulation of thin product markets and the other half to cartel-specific coordination. The signal is symmetric across winner and runner-up roles, survives conditioning on sector and co-bidding intensity, and is stable across bandwidths.

The third finding is the one that ties the story together. The gap between classical and worker-flow screens is not constant: it widens from 10~percentage points in thick labor markets to 33 in thin ones. In thin markets, classical screens actually fire in the wrong direction---cartel auctions show \emph{more} dispersion, not less. This gradient confirms the institutional-thinness mechanism we develop in Section~\ref{sec:model}: where specialized-labor pools are shallow, the cheapest coordination technology runs through personnel rather than prices, and the detection signal migrates from bid patterns to employment records.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Policy implications.} For competition authorities in middle-income economies, the message is direct. Matched employer--employee registries---RAIS in Brazil, DADS in France, IAB in Germany---are increasingly available and digitized. Building a worker-flow screen on top of existing procurement data requires a one-time linkage, not a new data-collection program. The marginal cost is negligible; the detection gain, in thin markets, is large. Authorities that rely exclusively on imported variance screens are not just under-detecting cartels---they are under-detecting them precisely in the thinnest, most vulnerable markets, where deterrence would matter most.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} The principal caveat is selection. CADE convictions are not a random sample of all cartels, and although none of the seven cases in our benchmark was built on worker-flow evidence (Section~\ref{sec:selection}), we cannot fully rule out that firms with dense personnel networks are easier to catch through any channel. A definitive test would require ground truth from a detection process provably orthogonal to labor-market connectivity---pure leniency applications from firms with no prior worker overlap, for instance. The strict pair-matched sample ($N = 10$) is too small to draw conclusions about head-to-head coordination; larger ground-truth sets, assembled from cross-state CADE linkages, would sharpen the test. And the simple model of Section~\ref{sec:model} treats bid-pattern and worker-flow coordination as substitutes; a richer framework allowing complementarity---where cartels use both channels at once---could generate additional predictions and refine the screen-design implications.

\noindent\textbf{Looking ahead.} The broader lesson is that collusion is not one thing. It adapts to the institutional soil it grows in, and the tools built to detect it must adapt too. A screen calibrated for thick, well-policed markets may be exactly the wrong instrument for thin ones---not because it is poorly designed, but because it is listening for the wrong signal. Following the workers, rather than the bids, is one way to recalibrate. There are likely others.
