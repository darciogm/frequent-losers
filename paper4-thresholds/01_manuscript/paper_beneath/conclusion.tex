\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{concl}

This paper set out to test whether worker-flow screens could detect procurement cartels where classical screens fail. The answer is no---but the reason why is more interesting than a simple null.

We assembled a conviction-anchored benchmark linking seven CADE-convicted procurement cartels to 2.6~million competitive auctions and the universe of formal employment in S\~{a}o Paulo. Four findings emerge.

The first is a \textbf{positive detection result}. Classical screens fail in the aggregate (AUC $0.46$--$0.56$), but this null is a composition effect. Disaggregating by coordination technology reveals that classical screens detect bid rigging at AUCs of $0.65$---operationally useful for triage. They are blind to the hub-and-spoke cartel that dominates the sample. The coordination technology determines which screen works: authorities should match the screen to the suspected mechanism.

The second is a \textbf{warning about network-based screens}. Worker-flow screens appear to fill the gap left by classical screens, with an AUC of $0.75$---but this result does not survive temporal conditioning. When worker flows are restricted to information available before each auction, the AUC falls to $0.50$. Co-bidding screens show the same pattern ($0.70 \to 0.55$). Neither network screen achieves prospective detection power on this benchmark.

The third finding is a \textbf{methodological caveat} with broad applicability. The gap between the full-panel AUC ($0.75$) and the temporally conditioned AUC ($0.50$) documents a form of look-ahead bias that affects any detection benchmark built from administrative panel data. When a network screen is evaluated against conviction-anchored ground truth, post-enforcement changes in the network---labor reallocation, ownership restructuring, board turnover---mechanically inflate the apparent detection power. This bias is not specific to worker-flow screens: it applies equally to ownership networks \citep{asai2026ownership}, board-interlock measures \citep{herrera2025collusion}, and any network layer constructed from longitudinal administrative records. The remedy is straightforward---condition the network measure on temporal precedence---but the bias has not, to our knowledge, been documented or tested in the detection literature.

The fourth finding is \textbf{substantive}. The temporal decomposition reveals that worker flows between cartel firms and their rivals are concentrated \emph{after} cartel conduct periods end, not before or during. The post-conduct component alone produces an AUC of $0.77$; the pre-conduct component produces exactly $0.50$. The flows are directional---workers move predominantly from convicted firms to competitors---and the ratio intensifies from $1.4{:}1$ in 2010 to $4{:}1$ by 2016--2017. An exit placebo shows that the pattern is not cartel-specific: non-cartel firms that stop bidding on BEC generate outflow spikes of similar magnitude ($+1.6$~pp vs.\ $+1.9$~pp for cartel firms). The labor-market aftermath is a generic consequence of firm contraction in thin markets, and cartel enforcement is one cause of such contraction. Characterizing the movers confirms this interpretation: workers who leave cartel firms are predominantly operational staff in service and sales roles, not the managers and professionals who might carry coordination know-how.

\medskip

\noindent\textbf{Policy implications.} For competition authorities building data-driven screening pipelines, the message is direct: network-based screens evaluated on historical benchmarks may overstate their real-time usefulness if the evaluation does not condition on temporal precedence. The bias is generic---it arises whenever firms contract and workers reallocate, regardless of whether the contraction is cartel-related---which makes it a concern for any panel-based detection benchmark, not just ours. Authorities adopting worker-flow or ownership-network screens should validate them prospectively, not retrospectively. The exit-placebo result also mitigates one policy concern: because the workers who leave cartel firms are predominantly operational staff rather than managers or coordination-capable professionals, the risk that enforcement-driven labor reallocation diffuses collusive know-how to competitors appears low.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} The principal caveat remains the small number of convicted cartels: seven cases, one of which (medicamentos) accounts for 85\% of the cartel-active sample. The temporal decomposition results are driven disproportionately by this case, and replication on larger or independent ground-truth sets is a priority. The RAIS panel ends in 2017 while CADE judgments for most cartels arrive after 2019, so the ``post-conduct'' window we measure captures the investigation and pre-judgment period, not the post-judgment period. A definitive test of enforcement-driven reallocation would require RAIS data extending through the judgment dates. Finally, the benchmark captures only cartels that were detected and convicted; the worker-flow patterns of undetected cartels remain unknown.

\noindent\textbf{Looking ahead.} The broader lesson is about what administrative records actually capture. Employment data do not record collusion in progress---the personnel flows that sustain coordination, if they exist, are too subtle or informal to leave traces in annual employment censuses. What employment records \emph{do} capture is the aftermath: the reallocation of human capital that follows enforcement. Following the workers, rather than the bids, reveals not the cartel itself but the institutional disruption it leaves behind. Understanding that disruption---its magnitude, its direction, and its consequences for market competition---is the next step.
