\section{Results}\label{res}

We present the results in two acts. The first (Sections~\ref{sec:res_classical}--\ref{sec:res_network}) establishes the facts: classical screens fail, and worker-flow screens appear to succeed. The second (Sections~\ref{sec:temporal}--\ref{sec:rivalry}) tests whether the apparent success is genuine---and discovers that it is not. The remaining subsections interpret the pattern that emerges.

\subsection{Classical screens and the close-margin RD}\label{sec:res_classical}

Table~\ref{tab:unified} presents the unified benchmark comparison of all screens on the same close-margin pregão sample ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$). Panel~A reports the classical bid-distribution screens on the cartel-active benchmark ($N = 2{,}319$ cartel-tainted auctions vs.\ $N = 527{,}259$ clean controls).

All four classical screens fire in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model: the coefficient of variation ($\Delta = -0.035$, $d = -0.07$), bid spread ($\Delta = -0.173$, $d = -0.10$), and number of active bidders ($\Delta = -0.87$, $d = -0.24$) are compressed at cartel-tainted auctions relative to clean controls. Welch $t$-statistics are large and statistically significant, owing to the massive control sample. Yet the effect sizes are small: Cohen's $d$ ranges from $-0.07$ to $-0.24$, indicating near-total distributional overlap between cartel and clean auctions. The AUCs reflect this overlap, ranging from $0.52$ (coefficient of variation) to $0.56$ (number of bidders)---above chance but far too low for investigative triage. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirms the weak rank-order separation ($p < 10^{-4}$ for each screen, consistent with the AUC direction).

The margin of victory exhibits an anomalous pattern: $\Delta MV = +0.004$ ($d = +0.17$), with an AUC of $0.46$---slightly \emph{below} chance and in the \emph{opposite} direction to the bid-rotation prediction. Cartel-active auctions have marginally \emph{larger} winning margins, not smaller. We interpret this as consistent with a partial phantom-bidding mechanism in which cover bids are inflated relative to the designated winner's price, widening rather than compressing the margin.

A direct replication of the \citet{kawai2023using} close-margin RD detects a baseline incumbency advantage on the full sample ($\hat{\beta} = 0.018$, $\text{SE} = 0.005$, $p < 0.001$), reflecting a competitive pattern in which recent winners are slightly more likely to win again. On the cartel-active subsample, however, no \emph{additional} discontinuity is detected ($p > 0.15$): cartel exposure does not amplify the baseline incumbency pattern, nor does it produce a detectable jump in last-bid behavior. A density test detects no manipulation of the running variable ($T_{\text{CJM}} = -0.02$, $p = 0.98$). Neither the RD design nor the density test flags the cartel-active auctions as anomalous relative to competitive bidding.

Classical screens detect bid rigging. On the 342 cartel-active auctions involving bid-rigging cartels, the $CV$ AUC reaches $0.65$, spread reaches $0.65$, and bidder count reaches $0.60$---all substantively above chance and operationally useful as triage indicators. On the 1{,}977 hub-and-spoke auctions, every classical screen is at or near chance ($0.50$--$0.55$). The aggregate null of $0.52$--$0.56$ is a composition effect: the hub-and-spoke cartel, which accounts for $85\%$ of the sample and leaves no trace in bid patterns, dilutes the bid-rigging signal that would otherwise be visible.

The result makes economic sense. Bid-rigging cartels coordinate by arranging cover bids around a designated winner, which compresses the bid distribution---exactly the signature that variance screens are designed to detect. Hub-and-spoke cartels coordinate through a central intermediary (in this case, a pharmaceutical distributor) that sets prices upstream; individual firms may submit bids that look competitive at the auction level even when prices are pre-arranged at the wholesale level. Classical screens, which operate on the bid distribution, are architecturally incapable of detecting coordination that occurs outside the auction.

This finding is the paper's positive detection result. It does not require temporal conditioning---it uses contemporaneous bid data---and it does not depend on the medicamentos cartel that dominates the benchmark. The message for competition authorities is direct: classical variance screens detect bid rigging at operationally useful levels (AUC $\approx 0.65$), but they are blind to hub-and-spoke coordination. Matching the screen to the suspected coordination technology is essential.

\subsection{Network-based screens: an apparent breakthrough}\label{sec:res_network}

If classical screens detect bid rigging but miss hub-and-spoke, can network screens fill the gap? Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:unified} turns to the network screens, evaluated on the same $N = 2{,}319$ cartel-active auctions.

The answer appears to be worker flows. The shared-worker count yields an AUC of $0.750$ ($\Delta = +3.70$, $t = +14.1$), and the Jaccard similarity yields an AUC of $0.739$ ($\Delta = +0.0014$, $d = +0.19$, $t = +20.7$). Both outperform the best classical screen (number of bidders, AUC $= 0.56$) by roughly 20 percentage points. A third screen constructed from co-bidding density yields an AUC of $0.695$, providing independent corroboration through a distinct information channel.

The signal is not an artifact of sectoral composition. Restricting attention to firm pairs that share the same 2-digit CNAE sectoral classification reduces the Jaccard AUC only marginally, from $0.739$ to approximately $0.730$. The residual of worker-flow after partialling out the log co-bidding density of each pair achieves an AUC of $0.749$---essentially unchanged from the raw baseline.

Decomposing the AUC by cartel role reveals symmetry. Of the $2{,}319$ cartel-active auctions, $996$ have the winner as the cartel-active firm, $1{,}313$ have the runner-up as the cartel firm, and only $10$ have both. The AUC is stable across these subgroups ($0.74$--$0.76$), indicating that the signal does not depend on whether the cartel firm wins or loses.

These results appear compelling. But they rest on a critical assumption: that the worker-flow measure captures information available at the time of the auction. The shared-worker count uses the full 2009--2017 RAIS panel---which means that for an auction held in 2010, the screen incorporates worker movements that occurred in 2015. Two features of the benchmark compound this concern. First, one cartel (medicamentos, conduct 2007--2011) accounts for 85\% of the cartel-active sample, so the headline AUC is overwhelmingly a statement about that single case. Second, in 99.6\% of cartel-active auctions only one firm in the (winner, runner-up) pair is a convicted cartelist---the screen is measuring the connectivity of a cartel firm with an arbitrary non-cartel rival, not the coordination between two co-conspirators. We turn next to the question that these observations make unavoidable: does the signal survive when we restrict the employment data to information that was actually available before each auction?

\subsection{Temporal conditioning: the signal collapses}\label{sec:temporal}

For each auction at year $t$, we count shared workers using only RAIS observations from years strictly before $t$: the cumulative backward window $[2009, t-1]$. This preserves the full employment history available at the time of the auction while eliminating any look-ahead. The resulting AUC is $0.52$---indistinguishable from chance. A three-year rolling window $[t-3, t-1]$ produces an essentially identical result ($0.54$). Table~\ref{tab:temporal} summarizes.

The gap between $0.75$ and $0.52$ is the central fact of this paper. The prospective detection power of the worker-flow screen is zero on this benchmark. The signal that produces the headline result comes entirely from worker movements that occurred \emph{after} the auction being screened.

The bias is not specific to worker-flow screens. The co-bidding density measure, which achieves an AUC of $0.70$ on the full panel (Table~\ref{tab:unified}), falls to $0.55$ under cumulative temporal conditioning---a drop of $0.15$, compared with $0.23$ for shared workers. Both network screens lose their detection power once future information is excluded.

\subsection{Temporal decomposition: the signal is post-conduct}\label{sec:decomposition}

If the worker-flow signal is retrospective, where exactly does it come from? We classify each shared worker by when the inter-firm link was first observable relative to the cartel's conduct period:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pre-conduct}: both firms employed the worker before the cartel's start year.
\item \textbf{During-conduct}: the link became observable during the conduct period $[\text{start}, \text{end}]$.
\item \textbf{Post-conduct}: the link became observable after the conduct period ended.
\end{itemize}

The decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:temporal_decomp} is definitive. Pre-conduct worker flows produce an AUC of exactly $0.50$: there is no detectable difference in pre-existing personnel connections between cartel-active and clean auction pairs. During-conduct flows produce a modest $0.62$. Post-conduct flows produce $0.77$---nearly the full headline result. The signal is an aftermath, not a precursor.

The post-conduct flows are directional. Across all cartels, 30{,}977 distinct workers moved from cartel firms to rivals after the conduct period, versus 13{,}236 in the reverse direction---a $2.3{:}1$ ratio. The asymmetry intensifies over time: in 2010, the cartel-to-rival ratio is $1.4{:}1$; by 2016--2017, it reaches $4{:}1$. The pattern is consistent with enforcement-driven labor displacement: as CADE investigations proceed and conduct periods end, convicted firms shed employees who are absorbed by competitors in the same thin labor markets.

\subsection{Event study: do outflows spike after conduct ends?}\label{sec:event_study}

The temporal decomposition shows \emph{where} the signal lives (post-conduct flows), but not whether outflows \emph{increase} relative to a counterfactual. To test this, we estimate a firm-level event study around the end of each cartel's conduct period:
\[
\text{outflow\_rate}_{it} \;=\; \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \sum_{\tau \neq -1} \beta_\tau \cdot D_{i\tau} + \varepsilon_{it}\,,
\]
where the outcome is the share of firm $i$'s workers in year $t-1$ who appear at a different BEC firm in year $t$, $\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_t$ are firm and year fixed effects, and $D_{i\tau}$ indicates that firm $i$ is a cartel firm at event time $\tau = t - \text{end\_year}_i$. The omitted category is $\tau = -1$. Standard errors are clustered at the firm level. The control group is all non-cartel BEC firms with at least three employees, yielding a panel of $159{,}140$ firm-year observations ($26{,}928$ firms, $257$ treated observations from $41$ cartel firms with conduct ending by 2017).

Figure~\ref{fig:event_study} plots the event-study coefficients. Two patterns stand out. \emph{First}, pre-treatment coefficients are flat and insignificant (max $|t| = 0.65$ for $\tau \in [-4, -2]$): cartel and control firms follow parallel outflow trajectories before the conduct period ends. \emph{Second}, outflows rise after the conduct period ends. The post-treatment coefficients average $+0.019$, a $29\%$ increase relative to the control-group mean outflow rate of $6.7\%$. The effect peaks at $\tau = +1$ ($\hat{\beta} = +0.035$, $t = 1.94$) and $\tau = +4$ ($\hat{\beta} = +0.040$, $t = 1.17$).

\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{../../04_figures/event_study_outflows.pdf}

The estimates are imprecise---only $\tau = +1$ is marginally significant at the 10\% level---reflecting the small number of treated firms ($41$).\footnote{Because cartel conduct ends in different years (2010--2014), the TWFE specification faces potential bias from staggered treatment timing \citep{goodman2021difference}. With only 41 treated firms and clean pre-trends, the bias is likely small. A stacked-cohort estimator \`{a} la \citet{cengiz2019effect} is a planned robustness check.} But the direction is unambiguous, the pre-trends are clean, and the magnitude ($+29\%$) is economically meaningful. The event study provides suggestive evidence that the post-conduct worker-flow pattern documented in Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} reflects a genuine increase in outflows from cartel firms, not a pre-existing difference in mobility rates.

The bulk of the reallocation ($84.5\%$) goes to bystanders, consistent with generic labor-market mobility. A direct test of pair-level absorption reinforces this pattern. Of $993$ close-margin auctions where the cartel firm won during conduct, $463$ ($46.6\%$) show at least one ex-worker of the winning cartel firm subsequently appearing at the specific runner-up of that auction. Nearly half of all close-margin winner--runner-up pairs exhibit a post-conduct worker transfer---not mass displacement along auction lines, but a fine-grained pattern of specialized workers finding their way to the small set of firms in the same thin product market. Whether this pair-level absorption exceeds what any contracting firm would generate is tested in the exit placebo below (Section~\ref{sec:exit_placebo}).

The profile of cartel movers is clear---and it is not the profile of knowledge carriers. The modal cartel mover is a service or sales worker ($55\%$ of movers vs.\ $37\%$ of stayers), female ($60\%$), earning $2.4$ minimum wages, with lower educational attainment than stayers. The finding has a direct implication for the collusion-contagion concern. If cartel firms were shedding coordination-capable workers to competitors, we would expect high-skill, high-wage movers. Instead, the outflow is dominated by operational workers whose departure is consistent with generic firm contraction rather than strategic knowledge diffusion.

The exit-placebo coefficients are highly significant ($t = 5$--$8$, reflecting the much larger treated sample) and economically similar to the cartel coefficients: post-event outflows average $+1.6$~pp for exit firms versus $+1.9$~pp for cartel firms, a ratio of $1.25$. This is a sobering finding. The post-conduct outflow pattern that drives the full-panel worker-flow AUC is not cartel-specific---it is a generic consequence of firm contraction in thin labor markets. Any firm that stops bidding on BEC generates elevated worker outflows to competitors in subsequent years, at roughly the same magnitude as cartel firms after their conduct period ends. Cartel enforcement is \emph{one cause} of firm contraction, but the labor-market response it triggers is not distinctive.

The result sharpens the paper's contribution in two ways. First, it reinforces the look-ahead bias warning: full-panel network screens absorb post-event labor reallocation regardless of whether the event is a cartel conviction, a voluntary exit, or any other firm contraction. The bias is \emph{generic}, not cartel-specific, which makes the methodological caveat broader and more important. Second, it disciplines the interpretation: the matched employer--employee record captures the labor-market aftermath of firm exit in thin markets, not a cartel-specific enforcement effect.

Thin markets, by definition, have few employers capable of absorbing displaced workers---so post-conduct flows between cartel firms and rivals are mechanically denser, producing higher retrospective AUCs.

The original interpretation was that $44\%$ of the worker-flow signal reflects natural thin-market labor circulation and $56\%$ reflects cartel-specific coordination. In light of the temporal evidence, the ``cartel-specific residual'' is more precisely a \emph{post-conduct displacement residual}: cartel firms shed more workers to rivals than same-market non-cartel firms do, because enforcement-driven contraction is concentrated in cartel firms. The specialization component remains what it was---thin markets generate spontaneous worker overlap among all firms---but the cartel residual is not evidence of coordination during the cartel period. It is evidence of accelerated labor reallocation after the cartel period ends.

\subsection{Robustness: permutation falsification}\label{sec:robustness}

We randomly reassign the cartel-active label $1{,}000$~times and recompute the full-panel AUC. The permuted AUCs cluster at $0.500$ ($\text{sd} = 0.003$, $95\%$ interval $[0.494, 0.505]$). The real AUC of $0.75$ lies at the 100th percentile ($p < 0.001$): the full-panel signal is non-random. But as the temporal decomposition shows, it is retrospective rather than prospective.

\subsection{Selection and the interpretation of the benchmark}\label{sec:selection}

The temporal conditioning results largely supersede the selection concern. The full-panel AUC is inflated not by selection but by look-ahead. Once temporal conditioning is applied, the AUC falls to $0.52$ regardless of selection on detectability.

For the record, none of the seven CADE cases lists employer--employee data or worker flows as part of the evidentiary basis (Table~\ref{tab:evidence}). The selection concern remains relevant for interpreting the post-conduct displacement pattern: if CADE preferentially convicts cartels in markets where labor reallocation is naturally high, the post-conduct AUC could partly reflect market selection rather than enforcement effects.
