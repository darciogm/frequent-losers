\section{Results}\label{res}

We present the empirical results in three parts. Section~\ref{sec:res_classical} reports the performance of classical bid-distribution screens and the close-margin RD on the unified benchmark. Section~\ref{sec:res_network} reports the performance of worker-flow and co-bidding network screens. Section~\ref{sec:res_placebo} reports the specialization-placebo decomposition.

\subsection{Classical screens and the close-margin RD}\label{sec:res_classical}

Table~\ref{tab:unified} presents the unified benchmark comparison of all screens on the same close-margin pregão sample ($\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$). Panel~A reports the classical bid-distribution screens on the cartel-active benchmark ($N = 2{,}319$ cartel-tainted auctions vs.\ $N = 527{,}259$ clean controls).

All four classical screens fire in the direction predicted by the bid-rotation model: the coefficient of variation ($\Delta = -0.035$, $d = -0.07$), bid spread ($\Delta = -0.173$, $d = -0.10$), and number of active bidders ($\Delta = -0.87$, $d = -0.24$) are compressed at cartel-tainted auctions relative to clean controls. Welch $t$-statistics are large and statistically significant, owing to the massive control sample. Yet the effect sizes are small: Cohen's $d$ ranges from $-0.07$ to $-0.24$, indicating near-total distributional overlap between cartel and clean auctions. The AUCs reflect this overlap, ranging from $0.52$ (coefficient of variation) to $0.56$ (number of bidders)---above chance but far too low for investigative triage. A Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirms the weak rank-order separation ($p < 10^{-4}$ for each screen, consistent with the AUC direction).

The margin of victory exhibits an anomalous pattern: $\Delta MV = +0.004$ ($d = +0.17$), with an AUC of $0.46$---slightly \emph{below} chance and in the \emph{opposite} direction to the bid-rotation prediction. Cartel-active auctions have marginally \emph{larger} winning margins, not smaller. We interpret this as consistent with a partial phantom-bidding mechanism in which cover bids are inflated relative to the designated winner's price, widening rather than compressing the margin. This finding warrants further investigation but does not affect the headline conclusion: classical screens as a portfolio do not achieve operational discriminative power on this benchmark.

A direct replication of the \citet{kawai2023using} close-margin RD on the cartel-active subsample yields no statistically detectable discontinuity in incumbency ($\hat{\beta} = 0.018$, $\text{SE} = 0.005$ on the full sample; $p > 0.15$ on the cartel-active subsample) or last-bid behavior at the cutoff. A density test in the spirit of \citet{imhof2018screening} detects no manipulation of the running variable ($T_{\text{CJM}} \approx 0$, $p = 1.0$ on the cartel-active benchmark). Neither the RD design nor the density test flags the cartel-active auctions as anomalous relative to competitive bidding.

\subsection{Network-based screens}\label{sec:res_network}

Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:unified} reports the network screens on the same unified benchmark ($N = 2{,}319$ cartel-active pregão auctions, $\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$).

The worker-flow screens achieve markedly higher discriminative power. The shared-worker count yields an AUC of $0.750$ ($\Delta = +3.70$, $t = +14.1$), and the Jaccard similarity yields an AUC of $0.739$ ($\Delta = +0.0014$, $d = +0.19$, $t = +20.7$). Both outperform the best classical screen (number of bidders, AUC $= 0.56$) by roughly 20 percentage points. A third screen constructed from co-bidding density yields an AUC of $0.695$, providing independent corroboration through a distinct information channel.

Critically, the worker-flow signal is not an artifact of sectoral composition. Restricting attention to firm pairs that share the same 2-digit CNAE sectoral classification reduces the Jaccard AUC only marginally, from $0.739$ to approximately $0.730$. The residual of worker-flow after partialling out the log co-bidding density of each pair achieves an AUC of $0.749$---essentially unchanged from the raw baseline, indicating that worker mobility carries detection information above and beyond what co-bidding patterns alone can reveal.

On the convite subsample ($N = 263$ cartel-active, $\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$), the worker-flow screens maintain strong performance: shared-worker AUC $= 0.704$, Jaccard AUC $= 0.701$. The consistency of network-screen performance across auction formats---in contrast to the format-sensitive behavior of classical screens---supports the view that worker-flow connectivity captures a channel of coordination that is invariant to the procedural details of the bidding mechanism.

A natural question is what the cartel-active AUC actually measures, given that in most auctions only \emph{one} of the two firms in the (winner, runner-up) pair is a convicted cartelist. Of the 2{,}319 cartel-active auctions, 996 have the \emph{winner} as the cartel-active firm and the runner-up as a non-cartel firm; 1{,}313 have the \emph{runner-up} as the cartel-active firm; and only 10 have both firms cartel-active. Decomposing the AUC by cartel role clarifies the interpretation:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Subgroup & $N$ & AUC (shared workers) & AUC (Jaccard) \\
\midrule
Winner cartel, runner-up clean & 996 & 0.741 & 0.730 \\
Runner-up cartel, winner clean & 1{,}313 & 0.759 & 0.747 \\
Both cartel-active & 10 & 0.558 & 0.550 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Three observations emerge. \emph{First}, the AUC is remarkably stable across the two one-sided subgroups ($0.74$--$0.76$), indicating that the detection signal is symmetric: it does not matter whether the cartel firm wins or loses the auction. This rules out the concern that the screen merely captures a ``winner effect'' driven by a cartel firm's incumbency or market dominance. \emph{Second}, the signal is present even when the \emph{runner-up} is the cartel firm (AUC $= 0.759$), confirming that elevated worker-flow connectivity is a property of cartel-firm pairs in thin labor markets, not an artifact of winning per se. \emph{Third}, intra-cartel pairs (both firms cartel-active, $N = 10$) exhibit the highest mean shared-worker count ($\bar{W} = 28.4$, compared with $6.5$ for winner-only and $2.1$ for clean controls) but an AUC near chance ($0.56$), reflecting severe power constraints at small $N$ rather than an absence of signal.

The decomposition clarifies the mechanism: the worker-flow screen detects that cartel firms are embedded in thin specialized-labor markets where personnel flows with \emph{all} rivals---not just co-conspirators---are dense. The specialization placebo (Section~\ref{sec:res_placebo}) further separates this market-embeddedness component from the cartel-specific coordination residual. The headline AUC of $0.75$ thus captures both channels; the cartel-specific residual, net of market specialization, is approximately $0.64$ (Section~\ref{sec:res_placebo}).

\subsection{Specialization-placebo decomposition}\label{sec:res_placebo}

Table~\ref{tab:placebo} reports the three-way comparison of worker-flow distributions across the cartel, same-market placebo, and clean-global samples (pregão, $\lvert MV \rvert \le 0.10$).

Cartel pairs share at least one worker in 57.9\% of cases, compared with 31.1\% for same-market placebo pairs and 9.9\% for pairs in other markets. The Jaccard AUC against the global clean baseline is $0.740$ for cartel pairs and $0.605$ for same-market placebo pairs. In a linear decomposition of the total AUC gain (cartel vs.\ clean minus $0.5$):

\begin{itemize}
\item The \textbf{specialization component} (placebo vs.\ clean) absorbs approximately $0.105$ AUC points, or roughly 44\% of the total signal.
\item The \textbf{cartel-specific residual} (cartel vs.\ placebo) accounts for approximately $0.138$ AUC points, or roughly 56\% of the total signal.
\end{itemize}

Both components are consistent with the institutional-thinness mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:model}: thin specialized-labor markets generate spontaneous labor circulation among same-market firms (the specialization component), and cartels additionally reinforce those flows to sustain coordination (the cartel-specific residual). The two-component structure also has a practical implication: a worker-flow screen deployed without conditioning on market identity would capture both components, potentially flagging non-colluding same-market firm pairs as suspicious. Conditioning on market or product code absorbs the specialization component, yielding a purer cartel signal at the cost of a modest reduction in overall AUC (from $0.740$ to $0.638$).

\subsection{Selection and the interpretation of the benchmark}\label{sec:selection}

A natural concern with any conviction-anchored benchmark is that the cartels CADE detected and convicted are not a random sample of all cartels that existed during the study period. If the detection channel itself correlates with worker-flow intensity---for instance, if CADE is more likely to investigate firms when their personnel connections are visible to investigators---then the worker-flow AUC may overstate the screen's performance on the universe of undetected cartels. We address this concern in three ways.

\emph{First}, we document the evidentiary basis of each of the seven convicted cartels from the public record of CADE's administrative proceedings. Table~\ref{tab:evidence} summarizes the primary detection channel and the nature of the evidence that sustained each conviction. The evidence channels are heterogeneous: one case (aquecedores solares) was convicted ``based exclusively on indirect evidence''---that is, bid-pattern analysis alone; one case (transporte escolar) relied on IP address coincidence during electronic bidding; one case (sacos de lixo) originated from a state-level police operation (\emph{Opera\c{c}\~{a}o Colludium}); and the remaining four were built from leniency confessions, procurement-document analysis, or investigation-triggered dawn raids. Crucially, \emph{none} of the seven cases mentions matched employer--employee data, RAIS records, or inter-firm personnel connections as part of the evidentiary basis for the investigation or conviction. CADE does not, as a matter of standard practice, use worker-flow analysis as an investigative tool---the data linkage we construct in this paper is, to our knowledge, the first of its kind. The detection channel (CADE investigations driven by complaints, leniency, police operations, and bid-pattern screens) is therefore independent of the screening channel (worker-flow connectivity measured from RAIS).

\emph{Second}, we note that the worker-flow screen is constructed from an entirely external administrative data source (RAIS, held by the Ministry of Labor) that is not part of CADE's standard enforcement toolkit. Unlike co-bidding patterns---which are constructed from the same procurement data that CADE analysts routinely examine---worker-flow connectivity requires a separate data-access agreement and a non-trivial linkage procedure. The informational independence of the two channels reduces the scope for mechanical circularity.

\emph{Third}, we observe that the worker-flow AUC is relatively stable across the seven cartels despite the heterogeneity in detection channels. If selection-on-detection were driving the result, we would expect the worker-flow signal to be concentrated in cases where personnel connections played a role in the investigation. Instead, the signal is present even in the aquecedores solares case---convicted on bid-pattern evidence alone---and in the transporte escolar case---detected through IP forensics. While this cross-cartel stability is suggestive rather than definitive (small $N$ of cartels limits formal testing), it is inconsistent with a pure selection story.

These observations do not fully eliminate the selection concern. It remains possible that firms with dense worker-flow networks are easier to detect through \emph{any} channel---for instance, because the same organizational proximity that generates personnel flows also generates careless document trails or complaints from disgruntled employees. A definitive test would require evaluating the worker-flow screen against cartels detected through channels that are \emph{provably} orthogonal to personnel connectivity, such as pure leniency applications from firms with no prior labor-market overlap. We leave this test to future work and flag the selection caveat as the principal interpretive limitation of our benchmark design.
