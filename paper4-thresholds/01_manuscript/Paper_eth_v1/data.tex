\section{Data}\label{data}

Our analysis is based on a novel dataset of public procurement auctions drawn from official government records. The primary source is the national electronic procurement system, which records detailed information on each tender—including bidder identities, bid amounts, and auction outcomes. The dataset covers a broad span of recent years, capturing thousands of individual contract awards across multiple regions and sectors. This wide coverage ensures that our analysis reflects general procurement patterns rather than isolated cases. All monetary values are expressed in constant terms, adjusted for inflation, to enable meaningful comparisons over time.

We focus on competitive bidding processes, including only those auctions where at least two bidders participated. This restriction is crucial because it allows us to meaningfully compare the winning bid to the runner-up, which is central to our empirical strategy. After excluding tenders with missing information or only a single bidder, our final sample consists of a comprehensive panel of auctions that span from Year X to Year Y, involving N auctions and M unique firms. This rich sample provides the necessary variation to investigate how firm status and past behavior affect auction outcomes.

Key variables in our dataset include:

\textbf{Contract Award and Value:} For each tender, we record the winning bidder and the contract value at which the award was made. To account for differences in project scale, bid amounts are normalized by the auction's reserve price or official cost estimate, enabling comparability across projects.

\textbf{Incumbent Winner:} We identify whether the winning firm is an incumbent, meaning it had won the previous similar auction for the same type of contract. This binary indicator equals 1 if the firm was the provider in the preceding procurement cycle and 0 otherwise. Determining incumbency required linking each auction to its predecessor based on project identifiers or descriptive keywords. In our sample, incumbents win a substantial share of auctions, highlighting their persistent presence in public procurement.

\textbf{Bidder Backlog:} The backlog represents the firm's current workload, calculated as the total value of ongoing contracts that the firm is executing. For each auction, we sum the contract values for projects that were active in the 120 days preceding the auction date. This measure is then standardized—by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation within each procurement category—to allow comparisons across firms of different sizes. The backlog is a key indicator because firms with lower workloads may bid more aggressively, while those with higher workloads might be less competitive.

\textbf{Recent Win Rate:} We compute each firm’s recent win rate as the proportion of auctions it won within the 90-day period preceding a given auction. This rolling measure captures short-term bidding success and helps to gauge whether a firm has been experiencing a streak of wins or losses. A high recent win rate may suggest that a firm is either exceptionally competitive or, alternatively, benefiting from favorable treatment.

\textbf{Bid Margin and Competitiveness:} Using detailed bid data, we construct the bid margin, defined as the difference between the winning bid and the second-lowest bid. This margin is often expressed as a percentage of the winning bid or normalized by the reserve price, and it serves as an indicator of how close an auction was. A small bid margin indicates a highly competitive auction, which is central to our analysis, as such contests are used in our Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) to identify the causal effects of narrowly winning versus narrowly losing.

\medskip

We conducted extensive data cleaning and preprocessing to ensure accuracy and consistency. First, we standardized bidder names using unique identifiers (when available) to track firms accurately across multiple auctions. This consolidation was essential for constructing the incumbency indicator, backlog, and recent win rate. Next, we merged the auction records with historical contract data to link each auction with its preceding contract, allowing us to flag incumbent wins. We also integrated contract timeline information to compute backlog by identifying contracts that overlapped with the auction date.

We addressed missing data and outliers by excluding auctions with incomplete key information and winsorizing extreme values in variables such as contract value and backlog. All monetary values were converted to real terms using the national Consumer Price Index (CPI), ensuring that our comparisons over time remain meaningful.

Finally, we restrict our analysis to auctions that meet the criteria for our identification strategy. Only auctions with at least two bidders and where an incumbent can be defined are included. This filtering yields a clean sample of N' auctions. Within this sample, incumbents win approximately 70\% of the time, and the average bid margin is on the order of X\% of the contract value, with many auctions decided by very narrow margins. Such characteristics indicate strong incumbent advantages and competitive pressure in bidding, and they form the basis for our subsequent empirical analysis.

Overall, our dataset offers a detailed and comprehensive view of public procurement auctions. It allows us to track firm performance, such as incumbency, workload, and bidding competitiveness, thereby providing a robust foundation for evaluating the presence and impact of collusive behavior in these markets.
