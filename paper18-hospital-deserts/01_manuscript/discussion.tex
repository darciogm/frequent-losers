\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Cross-Sectional Mortality Null is Misleading}

The natural reading of Table \ref{tab:cv-mortality} would be that
network-revealed isolation ``does not matter'' for mortality, since
adding it to a regression already containing geographic isolation
yields no gain. We argue this reading is incorrect, and that the
correct interpretation is that the cross-sectional, residential-
municipality-level amenable mortality rate is the wrong outcome to
detect what the divergence captures.

Two confounders operate in the same direction.

\paragraph{SIM under-reporting.}
Vital registration coverage in Brazil is uneven. The Northern region
has historically estimated coverage of $85\text{--}95\%$, vs
$95\text{--}99\%$ in the South \citep{ms2014, franca2017}. Within the
North, ill-defined-cause deaths (R-codes) range from $7\%$ to $25\%$
across municipalities, and ill-defined cardiovascular deaths in
particular are concentrated in remote ribeirinho municipalities where
death certification is performed by lay personnel rather than
attending physicians. Many deaths that would be classified as
``amenable'' under the Nolte--McKee list are instead recorded under
\texttt{R99} (ill-defined and unknown causes of mortality). The result
is mechanical: the more isolated a municipality is from physician-
attended care, the lower its observed amenable mortality
\emph{rate}---not because it is in fact lower, but because deaths are
either not registered or not classified as amenable. This bias
attenuates and may even invert the sign of the access-mortality
relationship.

\paragraph{Cross-border death recording.}
A second mechanism operates in the opposite spatial regime, the
Northeastern semi-arid hinterland. There, patients with conditions
ranging from acute myocardial infarction to obstetric complications are
routinely transferred from local hospitals (where the divergence-
positive cases reside) to teaching hospitals in state capitals such as
Recife, Salvador, Fortaleza, and Teresina. Per the SIM coding
convention, when a patient dies in a hospital, the death is registered
in that hospital's municipality (\texttt{CODMUNOCO}), not in the
patient's residential municipality (\texttt{CODMUNRES}). Although the
SIM database records both fields, our outcome---and the standard
practice in the access literature---uses \texttt{CODMUNRES}. The
consequence is that amenable deaths originating in the highly-divergent
sertão are partially attributed to the receiving capital, which
typically has \emph{negative} divergence (well-equipped and well-
calibrated). The cross-section thus correlates positive divergence
with anomalously \emph{low} residential mortality, again attenuating
the access-mortality relationship.

\paragraph{Empirical test of the under-reporting hypothesis.}
We can test the SIM-coverage hypothesis directly by computing each
municipality's fraction of deaths recorded with an ill-defined
underlying cause (ICD-10 codes R96 through R99) over $2010\text{--}2023$
and using it as a control in the mortality regression. If the
hypothesis is correct, this fraction should (i) correlate positively
with isolation, (ii) load with a strongly negative coefficient on
amenable mortality (more ill-defined deaths $\Rightarrow$ fewer
classified as amenable), and (iii) absorb a substantial share of the
residual variance once added to the model.

All three predictions are confirmed (Figure \ref{fig:r99-hypothesis}).
The cross-sectional Pearson correlation of \texttt{frac\_R96\_R99}
with $\isokm$ is $+0.089$ and with $\isoemb$ is $+0.063$ (panels (a)
and (b)). Geographic and embedding isolation both predict more
ill-defined coding, with km the slightly stronger predictor. The
regional breakdown (panel c) shows the South with the lowest median
ill-defined fraction (median $2.1\%$), as expected for the
best-resourced regional vital-registration system, and the North,
Northeast and Southeast all clustered between $4\%$ and $5.4\%$ at
the median---surprisingly little raw heterogeneity, consistent with
secular improvements in SIM coverage but with substantial within-
region variance.

The augmented regression (panel d) adds the $z$-scored ill-defined
fraction to the model with $\isokm$. The coefficient is
$-11.17$ (out-of-sample standard deviation $1.0$): a one-standard-
deviation increase in ill-defined coding (roughly $5$ percentage
points) is associated with a $11$ amenable-deaths-per-$100{,}000$
\emph{decrease} in the observed rate. The out-of-sample $R^2$ rises
from $0.409$ to $0.451$ (a $+0.042$ gain), comparable to the
$+0.012$ gain from $\isokm$ over the controls-only model. R99 is
quantitatively a more important confounder than $\isokm$ for this
outcome.

After controlling for R99, the embedding still does not improve
predictive performance over geographic distance ($\Delta R^2 =
-0.001$ from $0.451$ to $0.450$). The implication is more nuanced
than ``the embedding works, the SIM is broken.'' The embedding does
\emph{not} provide additional cross-sectional predictive content
beyond geographic distance, even after the most direct under-reporting
proxy is partialled out, because $\isokm$ and $\isoemb$ co-vary along
the same underlying SIM-coverage gradient. Both metrics inherit the
gradient. What our analysis shows is that the cross-sectional
amenable-mortality regression at the residential-municipality level
is a poor specification for testing access measures of any kind in
Brazil: the appropriate test is causal, exploiting within-municipality
variation, which we discuss next.

\subsection{The Closure Event-Study Path Forward}

The natural way to break the under-reporting confounder is to switch to
a within-municipality identification strategy. Hospital closures
provide such variation: a closure at time $t$ in municipality $m$
generates a discrete shock to $\isoemb_m$ (rerouting patient flow to
more distant hubs) and to $\isokm_m$ (changing the kilometer-nearest
hospital). The CNES habilitation panel allows us to identify
$\approx 350$ exogenously-driven hospital closures in $2010\text{--}2024$
(filtering to administrative or fiscal motives, excluding closures
preceded by a $50\%$+ admission decline that signals demand-driven
exit). Applying the staggered-treatment estimator of \citet{cs2021} to
the resulting event-study allows three quantities of interest:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item the average treatment effect of closure on amenable
        mortality, controlling for residential SIM under-reporting via
        municipality-and-year fixed effects;
    \item the heterogeneity of that effect by pre-closure $\divz$, which
        identifies whether the effect is concentrated in poorly-
        calibrated systems (as theory would predict);
    \item the marginal mortality cost of one unit of network-distance
        increase, mapping directly to the policy question of whether
        SUS regionalization rules should weight network proximity over
        kilometer proximity in their referral allocation.
\end{enumerate}
We treat this as the natural extension of the present descriptive paper
and the focus of a companion manuscript. The data for the analysis is
already constructed (\texttt{hospital\_closures.parquet} in our
intermediate layer); we defer the empirical implementation to ensure
that the descriptive contribution is positioned and reviewed first.

\subsection{Policy Implications}

Three implications follow from the descriptive findings, conditional
on the caveat that the present analysis does not establish causality.

First, the nominal hub-and-spoke regionalization codified in the
$438$-Região-de-Saúde framework is heterogeneously aligned with realized
patient flows. The Northeast and the North both deviate from
the formal hierarchy, but in opposite directions: the Northeast
overstates the local network's effective capacity (red on the
divergence map), while the North understates it (blue). The South
emerges as the calibrated benchmark.

Second, distance-based access measures used in the
academic and policy literature systematically misclassify access in
exactly the regions where access deficits are most epidemiologically
consequential. Our embedding offers a low-cost diagnostic: any
municipality with $|\divz| > 1.0$ should be flagged for review by
both the regionalization authority and the resource allocation
formula.

Third, the embedding---built from SUS-billed admissions---necessarily
omits private flows. In states with high private-sector penetration
(São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, Distrito Federal), the divergence reflects
SUS patient routing only. In states with negligible private penetration
(most of the North and rural Northeast), the divergence is closer to
universal. This asymmetry should be kept in mind when reading the map.
