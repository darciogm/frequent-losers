\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Critics of psychiatric deinstitutionalization warned that closing
hospitals would raise patient mortality. Testing this requires knowing
who actually depended on a closing hospital, and where patients cross
municipal borders for specialized care the distance-based exposure rule
standard in the closure literature misidentifies them---misclassifying
$\valNmisclass$ of the $\valNunionPairs$ municipality--closure pairs in
our sample. We replace it with a patient-flow exposure rule built from
$\valNadmissions$ million Brazilian SUS admissions and estimate effects
on $\valNclosuresPnash$ PNASH-driven psychiatric closures with a staggered
event study, testing pre-trends on data back to $2010$. A first stage
validates the rule: travel burden falls $\valTravAbs$~km after closure,
twice what distance-based exposure detects. For the feared outcome we
find no harm---suicide mortality changes by $\valSuicWAtt$ per
$100{,}000$ (95\% CI $[\valSuicWLo, \valSuicWHi]$), ruling out the large
increases critics predicted, and self-harm is likewise null. We are
candid about a limit: preventable hospitalizations were already rising
before closure, so we do not read them causally. The catastrophe
predicted for Brazil's psychiatric reform does not appear among the
populations that bore the closures---the ones a distance-based analysis
would have missed.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hospital closures, deinstitutionalization,
patient-flow exposure, mortality, suicide, staggered
difference-in-differences, SUS, Brazil.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} I18, I14, I11, H75.
\end{abstract}
