\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Geographic distance is the standard metric for measuring healthcare access,
yet it ignores how patients actually traverse the hospital network: through
referrals, informal flows, and transport modes that roads do not encode.
We build a bipartite graph of $5{,}588$ Brazilian municipalities and
$6{,}710$ public hospitals from $179.5$ million federal SUS
hospital admissions ($2010\text{--}2024$), and learn $128$-dimensional node embeddings
via a sparse-on-the-fly node2vec implementation. Defining each
municipality's network-revealed isolation as the embedding distance to its
nearest high-complexity hospital---and comparing it to the haversine
distance to the same set---we find systematic and geographically coherent
divergence in roughly half of all municipalities. The Northern region of
Brazil is more connected than the map suggests, as river and air transport
funnel patients to the Manaus and Boa Vista hubs along corridors invisible
to road-based metrics. Conversely, the Northeastern semi-arid hinterland is
more isolated than the map suggests, as patients bypass underequipped
local hospitals and travel to distant regional capitals. The headline
divergence map and a structured taxonomy of canonical cases provide a
diagnostic of where the Brazilian SUS network deviates from its planning
geography. We discuss why the cross-sectional relationship between this
divergence and amenable mortality is confounded by SIM under-reporting and
cross-border death recording, and outline a closure event-study design as
the natural next step toward causal identification of the marginal
mortality cost of network distance.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hospital access, healthcare deserts, graph
embeddings, node2vec, amenable mortality, SUS, Brazil.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} I11, I14, I18, R12, C45.
\end{abstract}
