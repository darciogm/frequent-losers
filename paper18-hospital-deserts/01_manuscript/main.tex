\begin{abstract}
\noindent
When a hospital closes, the towns that lose access are usually defined by
distance: the residents nearest the facility. In public referral networks,
where patients cross borders for care, that rule names the wrong towns.
Using Brazilian SUS inpatient records, I define each closure's exposed
population from revealed pre-closure patient flows. Across
$\valNexoClosures$ closures, the flow and distance rules disagree on
$\valNmisclass$ of $\valNunionPairs$ municipality--closure pairs: distance
misses the towns that traveled to the hospital and flags nearer ones that
never used it. Applying the flow design to $\valNclosuresPnash$ psychiatric
closures in a staggered event study, the referral link breaks: inpatient
psychiatric admissions fall by $\valPsychAdmPct\%$: other hospitals absorb only
a small fraction of the displaced volume (about $\valAbsorbFrac\%$), so the
closure is a net contraction of inpatient psychiatric care rather than a
reallocation across providers; I find no evidence of large
acute increases in suicide or self-harm mortality, with the suicide bound
excluding increases above roughly one-quarter of baseline---a bounded null
that holds across specifications and under a synthetic
difference-in-differences design. The design ports to
provider-loss settings with administrative data.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hospital closures, deinstitutionalization,
patient-flow exposure, mortality, suicide, staggered
difference-in-differences, Brazil.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} I18, I14, I11, H75.
\end{abstract}
