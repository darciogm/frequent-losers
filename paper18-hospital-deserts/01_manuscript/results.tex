\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The results come in four steps. The full F5 sample shows that flow and
distance pick out different exposed catchments. Travel-burden first stages
then show that the flow-exposed municipalities had real utilization links
to the closing providers, separately for the F5 sample and the PNASH
psychiatric subset. The mortality estimates follow, on the PNASH closures
alone. Nonpsychiatric F5 closures and failed-F5 predecline closures close
the section as contrast and diagnostic cases. The full robustness battery
is in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

% =====================================================================
% §5.1 — Measurement result
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Flow versus distance in the full F5 closure sample}
\label{sec:results-f5-measurement}

Start with measurement, on the full F5 sample of $\valNexoClosures$
economically meaningful closures. Table~\ref{tab:measurement-compact}
shows the two rules pick out sharply different catchments. Of the
$\valNunionPairs$ municipality--closure pairs either rule flags,
$\valNmissed$ are flow-only and $\valNfalse$ are distance-only; just
$\valNboth$ are flagged by both. The disagreement, $\valNmisclass$ of
$\valNunionPairs$ pairs, is the paper's central evidence that geographic
proximity is an incomplete proxy for exposure to provider loss in a
referral network. The same table carries the specialty-aware distance
benchmarks and flags the comparisons whose support is too thin to trust.

The misclassification has teeth. Re-running the same Poisson first stage
under the distance rule treats $\valFDnDist$ municipalities to the flow
rule's $\valFDnFlow$, agreeing on only $\valFDoverlap$; the distance-treated
set used inpatient psychiatric care at less than half the rate before closure
($\valFDbaseDist$ versus $\valFDbaseFlow$ per $1{,}000$), so most were never
the hospital's catchment. Averaging them in dilutes the estimated decline to
$\valFDdistPct\%$, from the flow rule's $\valFDflowPct\%$ (online appendix).

% =====================================================================
% §5.2 — First stage
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Pre-closure flows identify the exposed catchments}
\label{sec:results-firststage}

Before asking what happened to the exposed populations, I check that the
flow rule is selecting municipalities with real admission links to the
closing hospital, not ones that merely sit near it. Travel burden is the
diagnostic. A post-closure change in travel distance can come from
redirection to nearer providers, from the disappearance of long-distance
admissions, or from a shift in admission composition; its sign and size
speak to realized utilization, not to welfare.

In the full F5 sample, flow exposure gives a $\valTravFFiveFlow$~km
post-closure travel-burden ATT, against $\valTravFFiveDist$~km under the
generic distance rule. In
the PNASH psychiatric causal sample the flow first stage is larger,
$\valTravAtt$~km, while the distance-exposed series carries a strong pre-trend.
Both patterns support the measurement claim and the choice to run the causal
mortality design on the PNASH psychiatric sample. First-stage estimates
for every sample are in the online appendix.

The sign of the first stage matters for how to read the mortality null.
Travel \emph{falls} after closure, because the long-distance admissions
to the closing psychiatric hospital disappear or shift to nearer
providers. The mechanical channel through which closures are usually
feared to raise mortality---patients facing longer trips to care---does
not operate here. Travel falls not because access improved but because the specialized destination vanished; the two margins move in opposite directions, and the null concerns the capacity margin, not the distance one. Any acute mortality effect would have to run through the
loss of the specialized inpatient option itself, not through added
distance, which makes the absence of a mortality response easier to
credit rather than harder.

% §5.3 — The dose: inpatient psychiatric utilization
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Where inpatient psychiatric use goes after closure}
\label{sec:results-utilization}

Before turning to mortality, it helps to know how large the shock to inpatient
psychiatric care actually is, and where the admissions go. Inpatient
psychiatric admissions---admissions with a mental or behavioural primary
diagnosis (ICD-10 F$00$--F$99$), counted at the municipality of residence
across \emph{all} hospitals---fall by about $\valPsychAdmPct\%$ after closure
(95\% CI $[\valPsychAdmLo, \valPsychAdmHi]\%$, a Poisson event study on admission
counts), against a pre-closure treated-group baseline of $\valPsychAdmBase$
admissions per $1{,}000$ residents. The decline is $\valPsychAdmImpact\%$ on
impact and deepens to about $\valPsychAdmStable\%$. I estimate counts directly
rather than the per-capita rate because the municipal population denominator is
interpolated before $2015$ and injects a spurious pre-trend into the rate that is
absent from the counts; the count event study has an economically flat pre-period
(largest pre-closure deviation $\valPsychAdmMaxPre\%$, an order of magnitude below
the effect) and the estimate survives a relative-magnitudes restriction up to
$M = \valPsychAdmHonestM$ (HonestDiD; event-study figure in the online appendix).

The decomposition explains where the admissions go.
Across the \valPsychDispNClosures{} closures with adequate flow support, exposed
catchments averaged \valPsychClosingPre{} psychiatric admissions per year to the
closing hospital before closure---about $\valClosingShare\%$ of their total.
After closure that volume goes to zero; admissions to other hospitals rise only
modestly, so total inpatient psychiatric admissions fall
(Figure~\ref{fig:where-inpatient-use-goes} and
Table~\ref{tab:displacement-compact}). The partial absorption is causal, not a
trend artifact. A Poisson event study on admissions at hospitals \emph{other}
than the closing one puts their rise at $\valOtherHospSR\%$ in the first years
(aggregate ATT $\valOtherHospPct\%$, 95\% CI
$[\valOtherHospLo, \valOtherHospHi]\%$, flat pre-trends, $p = \valOtherHospPpre$),
while the same specification on total admissions reproduces the
$\valPsychAdmPct\%$ decline (online appendix). The rise recovers only about
$\valAbsorbFrac\%$ of the lost volume: other hospitals absorb a small share of
what the closing hospital removed, and the loss is a net contraction of
inpatient psychiatric capacity, not a hospital-to-hospital reallocation.

The decline is a net loss of inpatient psychiatric \emph{admissions}, not
necessarily of all mental-health care. What disappeared was real capacity, not
low-intensity custodial volume: admissions at the closing hospitals were no
shorter in length of stay than at the hospitals that survived, and bed-days fell
as steeply as admissions (\valPsychBeddaysDropPct\% against \valPsychDispNetDropPct\%
in raw catchment means, in the online appendix). Where that volume went is only partly
identifiable from inpatient records. The plausible destination is the community
and outpatient care the reform was building---the CAPS rollout that
\citet{diasFontes2024} study, where hospitalizations also fall but outpatient
care substitutes by design---yet the SIH does not observe CAPS production, except
through the coarse baseline capacity controls in the appendix, and a residual of
unmet need cannot be ruled out. What the data do establish is the negative the title can answer: the inpatient use leaves the closing hospital and does not move to other hospitals. Its role here is to size the shock that
follows: a contraction this large is what makes the mortality null informative
rather than a quiet non-event.

% §5.4 — Mortality effects
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Mortality after a large inpatient shock}
\label{sec:results-mortality}

Here I leave the measurement sample and restrict the causal analysis to
the $\valNclosuresPnash$ PNASH-anchored psychiatric closures. The
utilization result gives the mortality test its bite: the null that follows
is not a quiet non-event after a weak first stage, but a bounded mortality
response after a large loss of inpatient psychiatric care---the exact
setting in which acute mortality should move, if it moves at all. The
question is whether these psychiatric provider losses raised suicide or
self-harm mortality in the municipalities that had relied on the closing
hospitals. I measure two outcomes at the municipality of residence,
suicide (ICD-$10$ X$60$--X$84$) and a broader self-harm measure that adds
deaths of undetermined intent (Y$10$--Y$34$), each per $100{,}000$
inhabitants. Population-weighted estimates are primary, because the
policy-relevant quantity is the effect on the average exposed
\emph{person}, not the average exposed municipality; the
municipality-weighted estimate is its unweighted complement.

Figure~\ref{fig:es-mortality} plots the event-study trajectories and
Table~\ref{tab:main-mortality-scaled-bounds} reports the summary
estimates. Population-weighted suicide mortality changes by
$\valSuicWAtt$ per $100{,}000$ after closure (95\% CI $[\valSuicWLo,
\valSuicWHi]$), against a treated-group baseline of $\valSuicBaseline$,
with flat pre-period coefficients and a parallel-trends test that does
not reject ($p = \valSuicWPpre$). Self-harm is likewise indistinguishable
from zero ($\valSelfWAtt$, 95\% CI $[\valSelfWLo, \valSelfWHi]$, $p =
\valSelfWPpre$). The municipality-weighted estimates are, if anything,
negative, and stay statistically null. Across every specification the
post-period trajectory sits near zero, with no upward drift large enough
to show up in these data.

A non-rejecting pre-test can simply be underpowered, so I do not rest the
null on it. Two things carry the weight instead. First, a synthetic
difference-in-differences design that reweights controls to match each
cohort's pre-closure path---and so does not require never-treated parallel
trends---returns a suicide ATT of $\valSdidAtt$ per $100{,}000$ (95\% CI
$[\valSdidLo, \valSdidHi]$), inside this interval
(Section~\ref{sec:method-identification}, online appendix). Second, the null
survives every network-contamination exclusion of potentially spilled-over
controls (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). I also report a HonestDiD
relative-magnitude sensitivity \citep{rambachanRoth2023}, but state plainly
what it can and cannot do here: at the per-$100{,}000$ scale of small
municipalities the mortality series is too noisy for relative-magnitude
restrictions to be informative---the robust set widens quickly once any
pre-trend extrapolation is allowed---so the bounded-null reading rests on
the classical interval and the synthetic design, not on trend
extrapolation. The same sensitivity is informative against ICSAP, where it
confirms no bounded extrapolation can rescue the effect.

The null is bounded, not a precise zero. The upper end of the suicide
interval, $\valSuicWHi$ per $100{,}000$, is about a quarter of the
treated-group baseline. The estimate rules out large acute
increases---a one-quarter rise in exposed suicide would fall outside the
interval---but it does not pin down a precise zero: effects smaller than
that bound are beyond what $\valNclosuresPnash$ closures can resolve. The
policy reading follows the same line. The data do not show the large
acute mortality spike that would be visible at this precision; they
cannot speak to subtle harms or to non-mortality welfare margins.
Suicide and self-harm form a small, pre-specified outcome family, so
multiple testing works in the null's favor rather than against it: any
family-wise or false-discovery adjustment only widens these intervals,
and wider intervals around estimates this close to zero cannot
manufacture a significant effect where the unadjusted tests find none.
The null is not specification-dependent: across $\valNspecCurve$
specifications spanning samples, estimators, threats, and inference, the
suicide estimate stays a tight null
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}, Figure~\ref{fig:spec-curve}).

\paragraph{Population versus municipality weighting.} The point estimates
differ in sign across the two weightings: population-weighted slightly
positive, municipality-weighted slightly negative. That reflects an
effect that, if present, differs between small and large exposed
municipalities, not a fragile result. Both weightings give
imprecise estimates around zero with intervals that exclude large
increases; they differ in magnitude, not in the qualitative conclusion. I
lead with the population-weighted estimate because it answers the policy
question---harm to people, not to places---and report the
municipality-weighted one so the reader can see the null is not an
artifact of down-weighting small areas.

% §5.4 — Contrast and diagnostic samples
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Contrast and endogeneity diagnostics}
\label{sec:results-contrast-diagnostics}

General-hospital closures play no part in estimating the psychiatric
mortality effect. They do a different job: testing whether the
exposure-measurement problem is particular to psychiatric closures or
shows up in hospital closures more broadly. The nonpsychiatric F5 sample
holds $\valNgenClosures$ general-hospital closures and one hospital-day
closure. Flow-defined and distance-defined catchments part ways here too
(online appendix), which says that realized reliance and geographic
proximity are distinct objects outside psychiatric reform as well. The
travel-burden first stage is not read causally in this small,
heterogeneous contrast sample.

General-hospital closures can also move measured travel in the opposite
direction from psychiatric ones. A general closure may push patients
toward farther substitutes, while a psychiatric closure can cut measured
travel when long-distance specialized admissions vanish or shift to
nearer providers. For that reason the nonpsychiatric rows are contrast
evidence about exposure measurement, not a pooled mortality estimate.

The failed-F5 closures show why the F5 filter matters. These events clear
the bed and volume screens but had already shed much of their patient
volume before the formal closure date. Pooling them would blend abrupt
provider loss with gradual institutional decline. The online appendix
documents the pre-decline pattern and shows their travel-burden event
studies failing pre-trend checks too, so I hold them out of the main
design and use them only as a diagnostic.

% §5.5 — The declined non-result (ICSAP)
% =====================================================================
\subsection{A result I decline to read causally}
\label{sec:results-icsap}

A credible null has to be as honest about the outcomes the design cannot
identify as about the one it can. Preventable hospitalizations (ICSAP),
the canonical Brazilian access-to-care marker \citep{macinkoHarris2015,
rochaSoares2010}, do rise after closure in the exposed municipalities, by
$\valIcsapAtt$ per $1{,}000$ in the post-period. Read naively, that looks
like the access deterioration one would expect if closures pushed unmet
need into preventable admissions. It is not identified.

The online appendix plots the trajectory. With pre-periods observed back
to $2010$, the pre-treatment coefficients are large and significantly
positive---$\valIcsapPreThree$, $\valIcsapPreFour$, and
$\valIcsapPreFive$ per $1{,}000$ at three, four, and five years before
closure---and the parallel-trends test rejects decisively ($p < 0.001$);
a Rambachan-Roth sensitivity confirms that no bounded extrapolation of
the pre-trend rescues the estimate. ICSAP was already climbing in the
exposed municipalities well before any hospital closed, so the
post-closure level is the continuation of a pre-existing trend, not a
treatment effect. I had earlier mistaken this for a clean result when the
pre-$2015$ data were missing and the pre-period looked empty; with the
denominators backfilled to $2010$, the trend is visible and the apparent
effect dissolves.

I therefore report the ICSAP trajectory in full and treat it as
non-identified. I do not bound it, do not read it causally, and do not
let it carry weight in the paper's conclusions. The standard is
symmetric: I read the outcomes that pass the pre-trend test---suicide and
self-harm, whose pre-periods are flat---and decline the one that fails it,
ICSAP, whose pre-periods reject parallel trends at $p < 0.001$; the
asymmetry is in the data, not in a choice of which result to believe. The
identified result is the mortality null; the ICSAP series is informative
only as a description of where the exposed municipalities were already
heading.
