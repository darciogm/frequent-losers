\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The analysis assembles a municipality-year panel covering the universe
of Brazilian municipalities, their public hospital network, inpatient
admissions ($2010$--$2024$), and mortality ($2010$--$2023$). Four primary
sources contribute: the federal hospital admissions register (SIH-RD,
\ref{sec:data-sih}), which yields both the patient-flow exposure measure
and the travel-burden outcome; the federal vital registration database
(SIM, \ref{sec:data-sim}), which yields the mortality outcomes; the
federal establishment register (CNES, \ref{sec:data-cnes}), which yields
the closure events; and IBGE auxiliary panels for population and
centroids (\ref{sec:data-aux}). Documentary sources used only to form
robustness sub-samples are described in Appendix~\ref{app:nlp}.
Table~\ref{tab:summary-stats} reports summary statistics by macro-region.

\input{tables/tab_summary_stats.tex}

Two patterns matter for the design. First, travel burden is structurally
unequal: Norte and Centro-Oeste municipalities average $94$--$96$~km from
residence to admitting hospital, more than twice the $42$--$45$~km of the
Sudeste and Sul. Second, the outflow share---SUS admissions sent outside
the residential municipality---is high everywhere ($58$--$73\%$), which
is what makes a within-municipality, closure-driven design operate on
real referral dependence rather than noise. The same cross-region
heterogeneity also confounds any cross-sectional comparison of access
and health, which is why we identify effects only from within-municipality
variation under a closure shock.

% §3.1 — SIH-RD

\subsection{SIH-RD: admissions, the flow graph, and travel burden}
\label{sec:data-sih}

The Sistema de Informações Hospitalares--Reduzido (SIH-RD) is the
billing record for every SUS-financed inpatient admission. Each record
is one Autorização de Internação Hospitalar (AIH). Our universe spans
$2010$--$2024$ at monthly granularity: $\valNadmissions$ million admissions across
municipalities of patient residence (\texttt{MUNIC\_RES}) and roughly
$7{,}000$ active SUS hospitals (\texttt{CNES}). The register covers the
SUS-using population only ($\sim 96\%$ of residents; private inpatient
flows are unobserved), so our estimates are identified within that
population (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-limits}).

From this microdata we build the weighted bipartite graph whose edges
$(m, h, t)$ count admissions of residents of $m$ at hospital $h$ in year
$t$; this graph defines the flow exposure shares of
equation~\eqref{eq:share}. Two outcomes are derived from it.
\emph{Travel burden} is the admission-weighted haversine distance from
$m$'s centroid to the centroid of the municipality of admission, at the
municipality--year level; it is the first-stage outcome that validates
the exposure rule (Section~\ref{sec:results-firststage}). \emph{ICSAP}
is the count of admissions whose primary diagnosis
(\texttt{DIAG\_PRINC}) falls in the Brazilian primary-care-sensitive
list (Portaria~MS~$221$/$2008$) per $1{,}000$ population, the canonical
Brazilian access marker \citep{macinkoHarris2015, rochaSoares2010}; we
report it transparently as a non-identified outcome
(Section~\ref{sec:results-icsap}).

\subsection{SIM: psychiatric mortality outcomes}
\label{sec:data-sim}

The Sistema de Informação sobre Mortalidade (SIM) records every death in
Brazil with cause coded in ICD-$10$; our window comprises $\valNdeaths$ million
deaths during $2010$--$2023$, attributed to the municipality of
\emph{residence} (\texttt{CODMUNRES}). The reform's critics warned
specifically about deaths of discharged or displaced psychiatric
patients, so we construct two residence-based mortality outcomes that
target that fear directly. \emph{Suicide} mortality counts deaths from
intentional self-harm (ICD-$10$ X$60$--X$84$) per $100{,}000$. A broader
\emph{self-harm} measure adds deaths of undetermined intent
(Y$10$--Y$34$), which absorb the misclassification of suicides as
accidents that is common in vital registration. We also track deaths
with a mental or behavioural disorder as the underlying cause
(F$00$--F$99$) as an auxiliary series; because such deaths partly reflect
in-facility mortality that mechanically falls when an asylum closes, we
do not treat it as a welfare outcome.

\paragraph{Why mortality is recorded at residence, and why that matters.}
Recording deaths by residence rather than occurrence is essential here:
a displaced patient who dies after traveling---or after failing to
travel---to care is counted against her home municipality, which is the
unit our exposure rule treats. (The occurrence-based attribution that
SIM also carries would displace such deaths to the hospital's
municipality, breaking the link to the exposed population; we use the
residence field throughout.) Two limits of SIM remain. Ill-defined-cause
recording (R$96$--R$99$) is higher in the rural North and Northeast
($14$--$22\%$ versus $4$--$6\%$ in the South), adding noise to mortality
rates there; and suicide is known to be under-recorded relative to
undetermined-intent deaths, which is why we report the broader self-harm
measure alongside. Both are absorbed by municipality fixed effects in the
event study to the extent they are time-invariant.

\subsection{CNES: establishments, beds, and closure detection}
\label{sec:data-cnes}

The Cadastro Nacional de Estabelecimentos de Saúde (CNES) is the federal
register of every health establishment, updated monthly. The
establishment register (\texttt{cnes\_st}) provides establishment type
(\texttt{tp\_unid}) and municipality; the bed register
(\texttt{cnes\_lt}) provides SUS-contracted beds (\texttt{QT\_SUS}), used
in the closure filter. Closures are identified by the cessation of
\texttt{cnes\_st} reporting for a \texttt{CNES} after a candidate year.
The candidate set ($3{,}518$ establishments ceasing to report between
$2010$ and $2023$) is dominated by registry artefacts; the cumulative
F1--F5 cascade (Appendix~\ref{app:filter}) reduces it to $\valNexoClosures$ exogenous
closures, $\valNspecClosures$ of them specialized psychiatric hospitals
(Section~\ref{sec:setting-closures}). The cascade does not eliminate
every false positive, but the closure-motive classification of
Appendix~\ref{app:nlp} provides a cross-check used to form robustness
sub-samples.

\subsection{IBGE auxiliary: population and centroids}
\label{sec:data-aux}

Municipality population denominators come from the IBGE annual estimates
(SIDRA table $6579$) and the $2022$ Census. A point of construction
matters for the pre-trend test: the rate outcomes require a population
denominator in every panel year, and the early cohorts' pre-treatment
years fall before $2015$. We therefore extend the denominators back to
$2010$---using the IBGE annual estimates for $2011$--$2014$ and a linear
back-extrapolation for $2010$---so that every cohort's pre-treatment
trajectory is observed rather than missing. Without this backfill the
pre-period of the earlier cohorts is empty and the parallel-trends test
is uninformative; with it, the test is real, and it is what reveals the
pre-existing ICSAP trend (Section~\ref{sec:results-icsap}). Municipality
centroids (IBGE shapefile, $2022$ vintage) enter the haversine distance
behind the distance exposure rule E2 and the travel-burden outcome.

\paragraph{The centroid-to-centroid distance approximation.} CNES does
not record hospital coordinates, so distances are computed
centroid-to-centroid between the patient's municipality and the admitting
hospital's municipality---standard in the Brazilian access literature
\citep{macinkoHarris2015, rochaSoares2010}. The approximation
understates within-municipality distance in large urban municipalities
and near-border cases, attenuating the distance measure; excluding the
$27$ state capitals (the principal sites of hospital dispersion) changes
the first-stage travel ATT by less than $1\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}); a coordinate-level sensitivity for a
sub-sample of closures appears in Appendix~\ref{app:distance}.
