\section{Institutional setting}
\label{sec:setting}

The policy question---did closing psychiatric hospitals harm the
patients who depended on them?---and the measurement problem at its
heart both arise from three features of the Brazilian healthcare system.
First, SUS regionalization sends patients across municipal and regional
boundaries at scale, so geographic proximity is a poor proxy for who
depends on a given hospital (\ref{sec:setting-sus}). Second, the Reforma
Psiquiátrica (Lei~$10.216$/$2001$), combined with a chronic real-value
erosion of SUS reimbursements, closed specialized psychiatric hospitals
on a federally-driven timeline that supplies our identifying variation
(\ref{sec:setting-psiquiatrica}). Third, the closure events themselves,
recovered from the federal establishment register, form the sample we
analyze (\ref{sec:setting-closures}).

% §2.1 — SUS regionalization and cross-border patient flow
% =====================================================================
\subsection{SUS regionalization and cross-border patient flow}
\label{sec:setting-sus}

Brazil's Sistema Único de Saúde, established by the $1988$ Constitution
and operationalized by Laws~$8.080$ and $8.142$~($1990$), is a publicly
financed, universal system covering the entire resident population in
principle. Approximately $71\%$ of Brazilians rely on it exclusively and
a further $25\%$ use it as a complement to private insurance
\citep{macinkoHarris2015}. Inpatient care is delivered through a mix of
municipal, state, philanthropic, and private contracted providers. The
spatial architecture is nested: each of Brazil's $5{,}570$
municipalities is a management unit; municipalities aggregate into $438$
Regiões de Saúde (CIRs) meant to articulate flows across levels of care;
and the $27$ Federation Units operate or contract the referral hospitals
at the apex.

\paragraph{Cross-border flow at scale.} The architecture prescribes a
regional flow hierarchy; it does not enforce one. Patients cross
municipal and CIR boundaries routinely, particularly for specialized
care. Across the $\valNadmissions$ million SUS hospital admissions in our pooled
bipartite graph ($2010$--$2024$), $\valCrossBorderPct\%$ are recorded against a
hospital outside the patient's municipality of residence
(volume-weighted); the unweighted municipality-mean outflow share is
$70\%$, reflecting that smaller municipalities depend far more on
elsewhere than the national volume average suggests. Cross-border rates
exceed $55\%$ in Sergipe, Pernambuco, and the Distrito Federal. These
flows are not residual---they are how a large share of the SUS
population reaches inpatient care. The consequence for closure analysis
is direct: a patient whose routine referral hospital sits across a CIR
boundary is recorded as ``unexposed'' to its closure under a
nearest-hospital distance rule, even though that hospital was her actual
destination. The distance proxy standard in the closure literature
\citep{avdic2016, carroll2019, joynt2015, lindrooth2018} silently
assumes flow aligns with road geography---defensible in systems with
thick local capacity (Sweden, much of the NHS), questionable even in the
U.S. Medicare context, where \citet{wang2020hrr} document drift between
patient flows and the Hospital Referral Region boundaries the Dartmouth
Atlas has used since the early $1990$s. Brazil's $\valCrossBorderPct\%$ cross-border
share is larger still, and is precisely where a flow-based exposure
correction is most likely to matter.

% §2.2 — Reforma Psiquiátrica as federal closure shock

\subsection{The Reforma Psiquiátrica and the source of closure timing}
\label{sec:setting-psiquiatrica}

Our closure sample is dominated by an ongoing structural reform of
mental-health care, and the reform's architecture is both the source of
our identifying variation and the reason the mortality question is
politically charged. Lei~$10.216$/$2001$ prescribes deinstitutionalization:
the progressive replacement of asylum-style psychiatric hospitals
(\emph{hospitais psiquiátricos}) by community outpatient networks
(Centros de Atenção Psicossocial, CAPS) and supported residences
(Serviços Residenciais Terapêuticos). Critics of the reform have warned
throughout its implementation that closing inpatient beds faster than
communities build substitutes would leave the most vulnerable patients
without care, raising suicide and crisis mortality
\citep{bastiampillai2016, yoonBruckner2009}.
Whether that fear was realized is the question this paper answers.
Implementation proceeded along two reinforcing federal tracks.

\paragraph{Track 1: PNASH inspection cycles.} The Programa Nacional de
Avaliação dos Serviços Hospitalares (PNASH) audited every accredited
psychiatric hospital on cycles in $2002$--$2003$, $2007$--$2008$,
$2011$--$2012$, and $2015$--$2016$, scoring infrastructure, staffing,
and care processes; facilities below roughly $61\%$ on the composite
index were issued an indication for de-accreditation under Portaria
SAS/MS~$501$/$2007$. The recurring inspection schedule is the critical
feature for identification: closure timing is structured by a federal
calendar exogenous to municipality-specific demand or supply shocks.
Our primary sample anchors to it (closures within $\pm 1$ year of a
cycle; Section~\ref{sec:method-identification}).

\paragraph{Track 2: chronic real-value erosion of reimbursement.} The
SUS daily reimbursement for psychiatric inpatient care was nominally
near R\$$30$ per patient-day from the early $2000$s, unadjusted for
inflation; over the panel window real per-diem reimbursement fell by
roughly half. The squeeze produced institution-level insolvency at scale
on a timeline driven by federal reimbursement policy, not by local
fiscal capacity or local demand.

Both pressures apply identical federal thresholds and reimbursement
schedules across all $5{,}570$ municipalities and generate closure
decisions at the institution rather than the municipality level. The
closures we study are therefore not induced by municipality-specific
demand shocks, local fiscal changes, or local primary-care access; they
are induced by federal policy reaching individual institutions. An
event-by-event natural-language classification of declared motives
(Appendix~\ref{app:nlp}) cross-checks this mechanism for each closure;
because its external validation is incomplete, we use it only to define
robustness sub-samples, not as the primary exogeneity warrant.

\subsection{Closure events, $2010$--$2024$}
\label{sec:setting-closures}

Closure events are identified from the Cadastro Nacional de
Estabelecimentos de Saúde (CNES), the federal establishment register. We
flag establishments whose reporting ceases before the end of the window;
the raw count is dominated by registry artefacts (small clinics, day
units, code changes). A multi-stage filter
(F1--F5; Appendix~\ref{app:filter}) reduces the set to $\valNexoClosures$ closures
meeting five cumulative criteria: closure in the non-pandemic windows
$2012$--$2018$ or $2022$--$2023$; hospital-grade establishment type
(\texttt{tp\_unid} $\in \{05, 07, 15, 62\}$); $\geq 30$ SUS-contracted
beds and $\geq 100$ admissions in the pre-closure year; and no $> 50\%$
admission decline in the pre-closure year (ruling out gradual
demand-driven exit). Of the $\valNexoClosures$, $\valNspecClosures$ are specialized hospitals
(\texttt{tp\_unid} $= 07$, predominantly psychiatric), $\valNgenClosures$ general
(\texttt{tp\_unid} $= 05$), and $1$ a day unit. The Southeast holds
$62\%$ and the Northeast $25\%$; the densest year is $2012$ ($11$
events), reflecting the early psychiatric-audit cycles.

\paragraph{The analysis sample.} The mortality analysis restricts to the
psychiatric closures, where the PNASH--reimbursement exogeneity argument
is strongest and the deinstitutionalization question is posed. Our
primary sample is the $48$ psychiatric closures within $\pm 1$ year of a
PNASH cycle; the $\valNspecClosures$ statistically-filtered specialized closures and a
broader $\valNclosuresPsymax$-closure psychiatric set serve as robustness
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). Closure-motive classifications, used to
form additional robustness sub-samples, are cross-tabulated against
facility type in Appendix~\ref{app:nlp}: administrative closures
concentrate in specialized hospitals ($20$ of $30$), consistent with the
PNASH de-accreditation mechanism.
