\section{Institutional Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

The Brazilian Sistema Único de Saúde (SUS), established by the 1988
Constitution and operationalized by the 1990 Lei Orgânica da Saúde
(Laws 8.080 and 8.142), is a publicly financed, universal, and
hierarchically organized healthcare system. It covers the entire
resident population in principle, with $\approx 71\%$ of the population
relying exclusively on it and an additional $\approx 25\%$ using it as
a complement to private health insurance. Federal financing flows
through state and municipal health funds; service delivery is
predominantly through municipally-owned units for primary care, and
through a mix of municipal, state, philanthropic, and private
contracted providers for secondary and tertiary inpatient care.

\paragraph{Regionalization.}
Throughout the paper we refer to the standard IBGE macro-regional
partition---North, Northeast, Center-West, Southeast, and South
(Figure \ref{fig:regional-map})---which underlies the geographic
patterns documented in Section \ref{sec:results}.

SUS organizes inpatient care along a hub-and-spoke logic codified in
the National Pact for Health (2006) and the Decree 7.508/2011, which
introduces the figure of the \emph{Região de Saúde}---a coordinated
group of municipalities sharing a referral network for primary,
secondary, and tertiary care. There are $438$ Regiões de Saúde as of
$2023$, nested into $26$ states and the Federal District. Patients are
expected to receive primary care in their municipality of residence
and to be referred up the hierarchy for procedures requiring more
complex inputs.

In practice, the actual flow of patients deviates substantially from
the formal regionalization. Cross-region referrals are common,
particularly when the formal hub lacks specific specialties.
Cross-state flows are systematic in metropolitan areas straddling state
borders (e.g., Petrolina-PE/Juazeiro-BA), in the southern fringe of
Amazonas where Manaus draws patients from Rondônia and Roraima, and
along the BR-364 corridor through Acre and Rondônia. Informal flows
follow ad-hoc personal networks, employer health-plan arrangements (in
the small fraction of Northeastern municipalities with significant
formal employment), and---in remote Amazonian municipalities---air and
river transport links that often take patients directly to a state
capital, bypassing intermediate hubs.

\paragraph{High-complexity care.}
The SUS habilitation framework, administered by the Ministry of
Health and recorded in the Cadastro Nacional de Estabelecimentos de
Saúde (CNES), grants individual hospitals the legal and financial
authorization to perform specific high-complexity procedures. Categories
include cardiology (\textsc{cardio}), oncology (\textsc{onco}),
neurology and neurosurgery (\textsc{neuro}), nephrology and dialysis
(\textsc{renal}), perinatology (\textsc{perinat}), trauma and
orthopedics (\textsc{trauma\_orto}), and transplantation
(\textsc{transplante}). Habilitation requires meeting structural,
human-resource, and procedural standards verified through periodic
audits, and it carries a higher reimbursement schedule under the SUS
fee-for-service table (Tabela SIGTAP) than non-habilitated equivalents.
We use a hospital's possession of any high-complexity habilitation
during $2010\text{--}2024$ as our operational definition of
``high-complexity hospital''. Of the $15{,}316$ hospitals in our
universe, $10{,}690$ ($69.8\%$) hold at least one such habilitation
during the panel.

\paragraph{Distance and travel time.}
The geographic literature on Brazilian healthcare access typically uses
either centroid-to-centroid haversine distance \citep{macinkoHarris2015}
or road network travel time computed over the OpenStreetMap layer
\citep{rochaSoares2010}. Both approaches share a common limitation: they
encode the road network and ignore the air, river, and rail-bus
intermodal flows that are non-trivial precisely in the regions where
the regionalization framework is weakest. Within Amazônia, no all-weather
roads connect, e.g., Caroebe-RR or Bagre-PA to their state capitals;
patients reach high-complexity care via subsidized municipal flights
or commercial river boats. In the semi-arid Northeast, paved highways
connect virtually all municipalities to small local hospitals, but the
relevant access decision is to the regional teaching hospital one to
three hours' drive away---and that decision is made on quality grounds
that no road metric captures.

This setting motivates an access measure built directly on observed
patient flows, rather than on the infrastructure that those flows
nominally use. We turn to the construction of such a measure in the
next two sections.
