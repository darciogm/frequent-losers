\section{Measuring exposure and identifying closure effects}
\label{sec:method}

The analysis rests on a single measurement choice---who counts as
exposed to a hospital closure---and a single source of identifying
variation---the timing of closure. Section~\ref{sec:method-exposure}
defines the patient-flow exposure rule and contrasts it with the
distance rule it replaces. Section~\ref{sec:method-identification}
states the exogeneity argument and the three identification assumptions.
Section~\ref{sec:method-estimator} gives the estimator. Throughout, the
object is the effect of a closure on the populations that depended on
the closing hospital; the methodological contribution is getting that
population right.

% =====================================================================
% §4.1 — Patient-flow exposure
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Patient-flow exposure}
\label{sec:method-exposure}

\paragraph{The flow rule (E1).} For each closing hospital $h$ with
closure year $t_h^{\star}$, let
\begin{equation}
  s_{m, h} \;=\;
  \frac{w_{t_h^{\star} - 1}(m, h)}{\sum_{h' \in H_{t_h^{\star} - 1}}
  w_{t_h^{\star} - 1}(m, h')}
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
denote the share of municipality $m$'s SUS admissions in the year
preceding closure that were sent to $h$, where $w_t(m,h)$ counts
admissions from residents of $m$ to hospital $h$ in year $t$, drawn from
the bipartite municipality--hospital graph of $\valNadmissions$ million SUS
admissions (Section~\ref{sec:data}). Municipality $m$ is
\emph{flow-exposed} to the closure of $h$ if $s_{m,h} \geq \theta$, with
default $\theta = 0.05$. The rule encodes dependence as revealed by
care-seeking: a municipality whose residents systematically traveled to
$h$ is exposed even when $h$ is distant, and a geographically near
hospital that residents never used is correctly excluded. Robustness to
a continuous sweep $\theta \in [0.01, 0.20]$ is reported in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\paragraph{The distance rule (E2).} The comparison is the exposure rule
standard in the closure literature: $m$ is \emph{distance-exposed} to
$h$ if $h$ is among the three kilometer-nearest hospitals to the
centroid of $m$ in the pre-closure year,
\begin{equation}
  m \in \mathrm{E2}(h) \iff
  h \in \mathrm{argmin}^{(3)}_{h' \in H_{t_h^{\star} - 1}} \;
  \mathrm{km}(m, h'),
  \label{eq:e2}
\end{equation}
with top-$3$ chosen as a generous catchment so the comparison set is not
artificially small.

\paragraph{Where the two rules disagree.} The rules are not nested.
Across the $\valNexoClosures$ exogenous closures, of the $\valNunionPairs$ municipality--closure
pairs that either rule flags, only $\valNboth$ are flagged by both: $\valNmissed$ are
flow-only (the patients depended on $h$ but it was not among their
nearest hospitals) and $\valNfalse$ are distance-only (the nearest-hospital
catchment, but the residents never used $h$). Distance thus misclassifies
$\valNmisclass$ of the $\valNunionPairs$ pairs. The disagreement is geographically systematic
(Figure~\ref{fig:map-divergence}): in the river-corridor North, flow
reaches regional hubs by routes road distance ignores; in the semi-arid
Northeast, the near hospital is bypassed for a more distant referral
center; the South, with mature SUS regionalization, shows the smallest
divergence. The first-stage travel-burden result
(Section~\ref{sec:results-firststage}) confirms which rule is right: the
flow-exposed municipalities are the ones whose travel actually falls
when $h$ closes.

\paragraph{The embedding as a measurement diagnostic.} We also summarize
the referral network with a node2vec embedding of the patient-flow
graph, which recovers Brazilian metropolitan and \emph{ribeirinho}
geography from co-attendance alone and supplies the divergence measure
mapped in Figure~\ref{fig:map-divergence}. The embedding is a
descriptive diagnostic, not the treatment assignment: the causal rule is
the share-based E1 of equation~\eqref{eq:share}, which is invariant to
the embedding and to its random seed. Construction, stability across
seeds, and the cross-sectional finding that the embedding does
\emph{not} outperform simple distance or travel-time baselines for
pooled mortality prediction are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:embedding}; none of it bears on the within-event
estimates below.

\subsection{Identification: closure exogeneity and the three assumptions}
\label{sec:method-identification}

\paragraph{The exogenous shock.} The flow share $s_{m,h}$ is not
randomly assigned---it is the equilibrium of a referral network shaped
by past policy and patient sorting---and we do not treat it as such. The
identifying variation is the \emph{closure event} $h \to \emptyset$ at
year $t_h^{\star}$, and its exogeneity to local healthcare demand rests
on institutional timing. The psychiatric closures in our sample were
driven by the federal PNASH inspection cycles and the chronic
real-value erosion of SUS reimbursement schedules
(Section~\ref{sec:setting})---forces operating on a recurring national
calendar rather than on municipality-specific demand or capacity shocks.
Our primary sample restricts to the $48$ closures falling within
$\pm 1$ year of a PNASH cycle, where the institutional timing component
is sharpest; the full set of exogenous psychiatric closures
(\texttt{tp\_unid}~$= 07$, $n = 41$ under the statistical filter, $n=60$
relaxing it) appears as robustness in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\paragraph{Three assumptions.} \emph{(i)~Parallel pre-trends}: in the
absence of closure, exposed and never-treated municipalities would have
followed the same average trajectory. We test this on pre-period
event-study coefficients observed back to $2010$ (the population
denominators are backfilled to give the early cohorts real pre-periods;
Section~\ref{sec:data}). The assumption holds for the mortality outcomes
and the first stage, and fails for ICSAP---which we therefore decline to
identify (Section~\ref{sec:results-icsap}). \emph{(ii)~No anticipation}:
closures are quasi-public events with weeks-to-months of notice, not
years; we set the anticipation horizon to zero, with pre-period
coefficients as the implicit test. \emph{(iii)~No spillover}: a closure
may redirect patients to a hospital also serving an untreated
municipality, biasing its outcome. We bound this by excluding state
capitals---the principal referral hubs---in Section~\ref{sec:robustness};
the mortality null is unaffected.

\paragraph{Pre-treatment balance.} Table~\ref{tab:balance} reports
balance between flow-exposed and never-treated municipalities.
Population and GDP are statistically indistinguishable (standardized
differences $\le 0.03$, $p > 0.79$); exposed municipalities have somewhat
\emph{lower} pre-treatment travel burden and ICSAP, ruling out selection
on bad baseline health; the share of admissions sent outside the
residential municipality is mechanically higher in the exposed set, by
construction of the flow rule.

\subsection{Estimator}
\label{sec:method-estimator}

We estimate effects with the staggered event study of
\citet{sunAbraham2021}, which is robust to the heterogeneous
treatment effects across cohorts that a closure sample spanning
$2012$--$2023$ invites. Let $i$ index municipalities (flow-exposed and
never-treated controls), $t$ index years in $[2010, 2024]$, and $g_i$
the year of the earliest closure to which $i$ is exposed
($g_i = \infty$ for never-treated). With $\mathrm{rel}_{i,t} = t - g_i$,
\begin{equation}
  Y_{i, t} \;=\; \sum_{e \neq -1} \,
  \sum_{g} \delta_{g, e} \, \mathbf{1}\{g_i = g\} \,
  \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{rel}_{i, t} = e\}
  \;+\; \alpha_i \;+\; \gamma_t \;+\; \varepsilon_{i, t},
  \label{eq:sa-estimating}
\end{equation}
with municipality fixed effects $\alpha_i$, year fixed effects
$\gamma_t$, and reference period $e = -1$. We aggregate the
cohort-specific $\widehat{\delta}_{g,e}$ into the dynamic trajectory
(plotted in the event studies) and the simple post-period average (the
ATT reported in tables), following \citet{sunAbraham2021}. Standard
errors are clustered at the municipality level.

\paragraph{Weighting.} For the rate outcomes we report
population-weighted estimates as primary: the policy-relevant quantity is
the effect on the average exposed \emph{person}, and population weights
also stabilize the per-capita rates of small municipalities. The
unweighted (municipality-weighted) estimate is reported alongside; both
deliver the same null (Section~\ref{sec:results-mortality}).

\paragraph{Alternative estimators and machinery.} The mortality null is
reproduced under \citet{cs2021} and \citet{borusyak2024} in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}. A causal-forest attribution of
heterogeneity \citep{atheyWager2019} and a counterfactual
graph-perturbation diagnostic, both auxiliary to the main design, are
reported in Appendices~\ref{app:cf} and~\ref{app:perturbation}.
A natural-language classification of declared closure motives, used to
cross-check the exogeneity filter, is described in
Appendix~\ref{app:nlp}; its external validation is incomplete, so it
informs robustness only. An auxiliary in-hospital cardiac-mortality
outcome, computed at the receiving hospital to sidestep the SIM
residence-recording issues, is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}.
