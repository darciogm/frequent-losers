\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Bipartite Patient Flow Graph}

We construct a weighted bipartite graph $G = (M \cup H, E, w)$ in which
$M$ is the set of $|M| = 5{,}588$ Brazilian municipalities of residence,
$H$ is the set of $|H| = 6{,}710$ public hospital CNES participating in
the SIH inpatient flow during $2010\text{--}2024$, and $E \subseteq M
\times H$ contains a weighted edge $(m, h)$ whenever municipality $m$
sends at least one SUS-billed admission to hospital $h$ in the panel
window. The edge weight $w_{mh}$ is the cumulative number of admissions
$n_{mh}^{\text{int}}$ over the period.

The pooled graph contains $|E| = 648{,}973$ edges out of a possible
$|M| \times |H| = 37.5\,\text{million}$, for a density of $1.7\%$. The
mean weighted degree of a municipality is $32{,}108$ admissions over
15 years; the median is $1{,}960$. Hospital weighted degrees are far
more right-skewed: the top-$10$ hospitals each receive admissions from
hundreds of distinct municipalities of residence, reflecting the hub
structure of metropolitan tertiary care (Table \ref{tab:tophosp}).
\input{tables/tab_tophosp} We retain the bipartite structure rather than collapsing
to a directed flow because random walks across the bipartition naturally
encode second-order proximity (two municipalities are similar if they
share many hospitals; two hospitals are similar if they share many
referring municipalities).

\subsection{Bipartite Embedding via node2vec}

We compute a $128$-dimensional vector representation $\mathbf{e}_v \in
\mathbb{R}^{128}$ for every node $v \in M \cup H$ using a sparse,
on-the-fly implementation of node2vec \citep{grover2016} from the
\texttt{pecanpy} library \citep{liu2021pecanpy}, which runs the
biased random walks in compiled \texttt{numba} code with no Python-level
materialization of walk sequences. This was a non-trivial implementation
choice: the canonical Python \texttt{node2vec} package
\citep{eliorc2017} pre-computes alias tables for every node-neighbor
pair, materializes the entire walk corpus in RAM, and exhausted our
$16$\,GB memory budget within $10$ minutes of execution on a graph of
this size. The \texttt{pecanpy SparseOTF} variant peaks at $0.55$\,GB
RSS for the full embedding step.

The hyperparameter configuration follows defaults known to perform well
on bipartite graphs of similar density:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Walk length $\ell = 20$, walks per node $r = 10$, yielding
        a corpus of $r |V| = 122{,}980$ walks.
    \item Return parameter $p = 1$, in-out parameter $q = 1$ (unbiased
        walks); both calibrated against neighborhood-recovery sanity
        checks (Section \ref{sec:results-sanity}).
    \item Window size $w = 10$, embedding dimension $d = 128$,
        skip-gram with negative sampling ($k = 10$ negatives), $5$
        training epochs.
    \item Edge weights $w_{mh}$ control transition probabilities
        proportionally; we apply no log-transform of weights, after
        verifying that the alternative does not change top-$k$
        neighborhood structure for the canonical sanity cases.
    \item Random seed fixed at $42$ for reproducibility; we report
        seed-stability robustness in Appendix \ref{app:robustness}.
\end{itemize}
Training takes $17.2$ seconds on a $12$-thread CPU; we forgo GPU
acceleration as it is unnecessary at this scale.

\subsection{Municipality-Only Projection}

The bipartite embedding places municipalities and hospitals in a
common space, but the structure of node2vec random walks ensures that
walks alternate between the two partitions. As a consequence, in
the bipartite embedding a municipality's nearest neighbors tend to be
its directly-connected hospitals, with municipality-to-municipality
cosine similarity attenuated by walk parity. To obtain a clean
municipality-to-municipality similarity space, we additionally
construct a TF--IDF-weighted projection.

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{|M| \times |H|}$ denote the patient-flow matrix,
with $A_{mh} = w_{mh}$. We row-normalize ($\widetilde{A}_{mh} = A_{mh} /
\sum_{h'} A_{mh'}$, so each row is a probability simplex over hospitals)
and apply IDF weights to columns ($\mathrm{idf}_h = \log(|M| / df_h)$,
where $df_h$ is the number of municipalities with $A_{mh} > 0$). The
resulting weighted matrix is $\widetilde{A}_w = \widetilde{A} \cdot
\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{idf})$. Co-patiency similarity between
municipalities is given by the symmetric matrix
\(
\widetilde{A}_w \widetilde{A}_w^\top.
\)
We threshold each row to its top-$30$ largest values, symmetrize
($\max(s_{ij}, s_{ji})$), and obtain a sparse municipality-only graph
$G_M$ with $|V| = 5{,}588$ nodes, $\approx 207{,}000$ edges,
average degree $37.3$, on which we run a second node2vec instance with
identical hyperparameters. The TF--IDF step is essential to suppress
the spurious similarity that hub hospitals (HC-USP, INCOR, etc.) would
otherwise induce between distant municipalities that send a small
share of patients to the same national center.

\subsection{Isolation Metrics}

For each municipality $m$ and the set $H^* \subseteq H$ of $3{,}488$
high-complexity hospitals embedded in our bipartite space (Section
\ref{sec:setting}), we define two scalar isolation measures:
\begin{align}
\isokm_m  &= \min_{h \in H^*} \mathrm{km}(m, h),
    \label{eq:iso-km}\\
\isoemb_m &= \min_{h \in H^*}\,\bigl[\,1 - \cos(\mathbf{e}_m, \mathbf{e}_h)\,\bigr],
    \label{eq:iso-emb}
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{km}(m, h)$ is the centroid-to-centroid haversine
distance and $\cos(\cdot, \cdot)$ is cosine similarity. We log-transform
$\isokm$ to handle right-skewness (median $16$\,km, $p_{99} \approx 200$\,km)
and standardize both to $z$-scores. The \emph{divergence} is then
\begin{equation}
\divz_m = z\bigl(\isoemb_m\bigr) - z\bigl(\log(1 + \isokm_m)\bigr).
\label{eq:divz}
\end{equation}

Equation \eqref{eq:divz} is the central object of Section
\ref{sec:results}. Its sign indicates the direction of disagreement
between the network-revealed and geographic views; its magnitude
indicates how strongly they disagree.

\subsection{Outcome and Predictive Specifications}

To probe whether the divergence is informative about realized health
outcomes, we estimate ridge regressions of the period-pooled amenable
mortality rate on combinations of $\isokm$, $\isoemb$, $\divz$, and
controls. The dependent variable is the average annual amenable
mortality rate per $100{,}000$ residents over $2010\text{--}2023$,
winsorized at the $1\%$ and $99\%$ tails. Controls are log mean
population, log mean GDP, and a vector of $26$ state fixed effects.
Out-of-sample $R^2$ and mean absolute error are computed with $5$-fold
cross-validation, $80/20$ split per fold, ridge penalty $\alpha = 1$,
random seed $42$. We restrict the predictive sample to municipalities
with mean population $\geq 10{,}000$ to dampen the Poisson noise that
dominates small-$N$ amenable death counts. The resulting estimation
sample is $n = 3{,}112$ municipalities.

This specification is intentionally simple: our primary result is
descriptive (the divergence map, Section \ref{sec:results-divergence}),
and the predictive regression serves to falsify a causal interpretation
of the divergence in cross-section, rather than to estimate one.
