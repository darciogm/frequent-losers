\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We construct a network-revealed measure of healthcare access for
$5{,}588$ Brazilian municipalities from $179.5$ million SUS hospital
admissions over $2010\text{--}2024$, and document large, systematic,
and geographically organized disagreement between this measure and the
standard kilometer-based access metric. Roughly $57\%$ of municipalities
have a different nearest high-complexity hospital under the embedding
metric than under the geographic metric, with the disagreement
concentrated in two recognizable regimes: an Amazonian regime where
river and air transport make distant hubs effectively reachable, and
a semi-arid Northeastern regime where local hospitals are bypassed in
favor of regional capitals.

The cross-sectional relationship between this divergence and amenable
mortality at the residential-municipality level is null after standard
controls. We argue that this null is itself an empirical contribution:
it identifies, with the help of a high-resolution access metric, two
biases of vital registration in Brazil (under-reporting in the North,
cross-border death recording in the Northeast) that operate in
\emph{opposite} directions and would be invisible to access measures
that vary only along the geographic gradient.

The divergence map should be read primarily as a diagnostic of where
the formal SUS regionalization deviates from the realized hospital
network. As such, it serves three audiences: (i) the regionalization
authority, which can flag municipalities where the formal hub does not
reflect actual patient flow; (ii) the resource-allocation formula
designers, who can identify where distance-weighted financing
underweights or overweights effective access; and (iii) the empirical
health-economics community, which can use the embedding as an
intermediate input for treatment-intensity definition in event-study
designs.

Subsequent work will exploit closure events to estimate the marginal
mortality effect of a unit shock to network distance, exploiting
within-municipality variation to bypass the cross-sectional confounders
identified here.
