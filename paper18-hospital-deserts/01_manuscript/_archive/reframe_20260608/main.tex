\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Critics of psychiatric deinstitutionalization warned that closing
hospitals would raise patient mortality. Testing this requires knowing
who depended on a closing hospital, and distance-based catchments can be
misleading where patients cross municipal borders for specialized care.
Using $\valNadmissions$ million Brazilian SUS admissions, I define
exposure from pre-closure patient flows and study $\valNclosuresPnash$
PNASH-anchored psychiatric closures with a staggered event study. The
flow rule identifies municipality--closure pairs that generic distance
rules often miss: the two rules disagree on $\valNmisclass$ of
$\valNunionPairs$ flagged pairs. A first stage shows that exposed
municipalities had realized utilization ties to the closing hospitals:
average travel burden falls by $\valTravAbs$~km after closure. For
mortality, the estimates rule out large acute increases but not smaller
effects. Suicide mortality changes by $\valSuicWAtt$ per $100{,}000$
(95\% CI $[\valSuicWLo, \valSuicWHi]$), and self-harm mortality is
similarly imprecise around zero. Preventable hospitalizations were
already rising before closure, so I report them as non-identified rather
than causal. The evidence supports a bounded conclusion: in this setting
and population, the data do not show a large acute mortality increase
after psychiatric hospital closures.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} hospital closures, deinstitutionalization,
patient-flow exposure, mortality, suicide, staggered
difference-in-differences, SUS, Brazil.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{JEL:} I18, I14, I11, H75.
\end{abstract}
