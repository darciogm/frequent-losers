\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deinstitutionalization---the closing of psychiatric hospitals in favor
of community-based care---is among the most contested health reforms of
the past half-century. Its critics have long warned that removing
inpatient capacity faster than communities can absorb the displaced
patients raises mortality, and suicide in particular, among those who
relied on the closing facilities
\citep{yoonBruckner2009, bastiampillai2016, pirkola2009}.
Brazil ran this experiment at scale: the Reforma Psiquiátrica
(Lei~$10.216$/$2001$), enforced through federal PNASH inspection
cycles and a chronic real-value erosion of SUS reimbursement, closed
specialized psychiatric hospitals across the country during the
$2010$s. Whether those closures harmed the patients who depended on
them is a first-order policy question---and answering it credibly turns
on a measurement problem that the closure literature has not confronted.

The problem is who counts as exposed. The canonical closure literature
defines exposure by geographic proximity: a population is treated by a
closure if the closing hospital was among its nearest providers
\citep{avdic2016, carroll2019, joynt2015, gujralBasu2019, lindrooth2018}.
But specialized care has thin local substitutes, and in a single-payer
system patients routinely cross municipal borders to reach it---so
proximity mismeasures dependence. Consider one closure in our sample: a
specialized psychiatric facility in São Paulo state closed in $2016$
had redirected $\valEgAdm$ pre-closure SUS admissions drawn from
$\valEgMunis$ municipalities, $\valEgFar$ of which lay between $36$ and $\valEgKmHi$~km from
the facility and would not have been treated under any standard
top-$3$-nearest distance rule.\footnote{CNES $2082683$, IBGE
municipality of closure $350280$; total pre-closure SUS admissions
from exposed municipalities $= \valEgAdm$; median distance from the closing hospital to an
exposed municipality $= \valEgKmMed$~km. Reproducible from
\texttt{02\_data/intermediate/exposure\_panel.parquet} in the
replication package.} The choice of exposure measure is not a
technicality: it determines whether the population the policy actually
touched enters the estimate at all.

We replace proximity with a \emph{patient-flow} exposure rule.
Municipality $m$ is exposed to the closure of hospital $h$ if, in the
year before closure, admissions from $m$ to $h$ exceeded a $5\%$ share
of $m$'s total SUS admissions---dependence revealed by where patients
actually went, not by how near $h$ happened to be. The rule is built
from the bipartite municipality--hospital graph of $\valNadmissions$ million SUS
hospital admissions. Across the $\valNexoClosures$ analyzed closures, the flow rule
and the distance rule disagree on $\valNmisclass$ of the $\valNunionPairs$ municipality--closure
pairs that either rule flags: distance \emph{misses} $\valNmissed$ pairs whose
patients depended on the closing hospital through specialized referral,
and \emph{falsely flags} $\valNfalse$ whose patients never used it. The
disagreement is not random---it concentrates in the river-corridor
North, where patient flow reaches regional hubs by routes road distance
ignores, and the semi-arid Northeast, where the geographically near
hospital is bypassed for a more distant referral center
(Figure~\ref{fig:map-divergence}).

A first stage shows that the flow rule captures realized utilization
dependence rather than geographic proximity alone. Travel burden---the
distance the typical patient travels for inpatient care---falls by
$\valTravAbs$~km after closure in the exposed municipalities, where a
distance-based exposure rule applied to the same closures recovers only
about half as much. The fall means the exposed municipalities had been
sending patients on long trips to the closing hospital; after closure,
those trips disappear or are replaced by different utilization patterns.
This is a dependence check, not evidence by itself that access improved.
(We summarize the broader referral network with a node2vec embedding of
the flow graph; it serves as a measurement diagnostic in
Appendix~\ref{app:embedding} and plays no role in the causal design.)

We estimate effects on the psychiatric closures with a staggered
Sun-Abraham event study \citep{sunAbraham2021}, with
\citet{cs2021} and \citet{borusyak2024} as alternative estimators. The
identifying variation is the \emph{timing} of closure, which we anchor
to the federal PNASH inspection cycles and the national SUS
reimbursement freeze. These institution-level forces operate on a
recurring federal calendar, making closure timing plausibly orthogonal
to local municipality shocks conditional on the event-study checks and
sample restrictions described below (Section~\ref{sec:setting}). Our primary sample is the
$\valNclosuresPnash$ PNASH-anchored psychiatric closures, exposing $\valNtrPnash$ municipalities.
Mortality is recorded at the municipality of residence. The
parallel-trends assumption is tested on full pre-periods: extending the
population denominators back to $2010$ lets us observe the
pre-treatment trajectory of every cohort, including the early closures.

We find no evidence of a large acute mortality increase. Among the
exposed population, the population-weighted suicide estimate is
$\valSuicWAtt$ per $100{,}000$ after closure. The 95\% confidence
interval is $[\valSuicWLo, \valSuicWHi]$, with parallel pre-trends that
do not reject ($p = \valSuicWPpre$); self-harm mortality is likewise
indistinguishable from zero. The interval rules out increases above
one-quarter of baseline. This null excludes large acute increases, not
small effects or non-mortality harms. It is stable across exposure
samples, weighting, modern staggered estimators, and the exposure-share
threshold.

One outcome we decline to read causally. Preventable hospitalizations
(ICSAP), the canonical Brazilian access-to-care marker
\citep{macinkoHarris2015, rochaSoares2010}, do rise in exposed
municipalities after closure---but they were \emph{already} rising
before it. On the full pre-periods, the parallel-trends test rejects
sharply: the post-closure increase is the continuation of a
pre-existing trend, not a treatment effect. We report the trajectory in
full and treat ICSAP as a non-identified outcome rather than a causal
effect of closure. This discipline is part of the
contribution: a credible null requires being equally honest about the
outcomes that are identified and the ones that are not.

The paper makes two contributions. \emph{Methodological}: a portable
patient-flow exposure measure for hospital-closure studies in
single-payer systems with origin--destination claims data, checked
against a mechanical first stage and contrasted with the distance rule
it complements. \emph{Substantive}: a bounded mortality result on
a globally contested reform, estimated on the population that a
distance-based analysis would not have found.
The closest Brazilian evidence is \citet{diasFontes2024}, who study the
municipal rollout of CAPS, the community-based arm of the Reforma
Psiquiátrica. They show that CAPS increased outpatient mental-health
production, reduced psychiatric hospitalizations, did not reduce
mental-health mortality, and increased violent crime. Our design asks a
different question inside the same reform: what happened when specific
psychiatric hospitals closed, and which municipalities were exposed to
those closures through realized patient flows rather than proximity.
Section~\ref{sec:setting} situates the analysis in the SUS and the
Reforma Psiquiátrica. Section~\ref{sec:data} documents the data.
Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the flow exposure measure and the
identification strategy. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the first
stage, the mortality null, and the ICSAP non-result.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} reports the robustness battery.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the null means for
deinstitutionalization policy and the measurement lesson for closure
studies.
