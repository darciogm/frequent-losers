\section{Empirical Strategy and Results}

\subsection{Main Specification and Identification Strategy}
The March 2018 policy change constitutes a single-cohort staggered difference-in-differences design. Group~65 (medical and hospital supplies) enters the SME-only regime in March~2018, joining the 76 other product groups that had been subject to the same restriction since August~2014. Within the 18-month estimation window (September~2016 to August~2019), Group~65 is therefore the sole treated cohort, and the remaining 76 product groups are never-treated controls from the perspective of this specific policy event---they had all transitioned to the SME-only regime before the sample window begins. This structure aligns the design with the modern staggered-DiD literature~\citep{callaway2021,sun2021,goodmanbacon2021,borusyak2024,dechaisemartin2020,roth2023}: identification comes from comparing a single switching cohort to a stable never-treated comparison set, a setting in which the concerns about ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons that motivate that literature do not arise.\footnote{Because the switch is \emph{from} open tenders \emph{to} the SME-only regime that already binds the control groups, the design is also a difference-in-differences in reverse (DiDiR) in the sense of~\citet{kim2019}: the 76 control groups are ``always-treated'' by the SME regime that Group~65 adopts in 2018. Both framings yield the same estimand; we adopt the staggered-DiD language throughout because it offers direct parallels with the \citet{callaway2021} and \citet{sun2021} estimators used as robustness checks in Section~4.4.}

Section~3 laid out the institutional timeline; Table~\ref{tab:groups} restates it in treatment-assignment form. From August~2014 through February~2018, every product group except Group~65 was subject to opt-out costs under the SME-only regime; Group~65 operated under mandatory open tenders. From March~2018 onward, Group~65 joined the other 76 groups under the same opt-out costs.

Using subindex $p$ for purchase offer, $i$ for items, $t$ for months, and $g$ for groups, the estimating equation is:
\begin{equation}
y_{pigt} = \eta_i + \beta\,\bigl(g65_{pgt}\times \text{Pre}_t\bigr) + x_{pigt}\,\delta + \varepsilon_{pigt}
\label{eq:did}
\end{equation}
where $y$ is an outcome, $\eta_i$ is item fixed effects, $\text{Pre}_t$ is a dummy equal to one before March~2018, $g65_{pgt}$ is a dummy for Group~65, and $x_{pigt}$ collects the sealed-bid and log-quantity controls. Standard errors are clustered at the item level.

The coefficient $\beta$ is the difference-in-differences estimate of the March-2018 policy change: it compares the change in outcomes for Group~65 between the open-tender and SME-only periods to the contemporaneous change in the 76 never-treated groups. The sign convention is that $\beta < 0$ for prices indicates that prices in Group~65 were lower under open tenders than they would have been under the SME-only rule---equivalently, that imposing the SME-only restriction raises prices by $|\beta|$ log points. For a single treated cohort, this specification is algebraically equivalent to the interaction-weighted estimator of~\citet{sun2021}; Section~4.4 confirms this equivalence numerically and corroborates the estimate with the~\citet{callaway2021} group-time ATT.

The central identifying assumption is that, absent the March~2018 policy change, the trajectory of outcomes for Group~65 would have tracked the contemporaneous trajectory of the 76 control groups. Because Group~65 (medical and hospital supplies) differs in product composition from the controls, the two groups operate at persistent level differences within every period; identification relies on parallel \emph{changes} over time, which the item fixed effects isolate by comparing each item with itself across the regime cutoff. Figure~\ref{fig:logprices} plots the event-study coefficients that visualize this assumption, and Tables~\ref{tab:cs2021} and~\ref{tab:sunab} show that the estimate is robust to both group-level aggregation~\citep{callaway2021} and an item-level event-study aggregation~\citep{sun2021}.

Anticipation is a second potential threat. The PGE-SP opinion dates from November 2017, roughly four months before implementation in March 2018. If PBUs front-loaded Group-65 purchases under the soon-to-expire open-tender rule, they would add open-tender volume in the months immediately before the cutoff, \emph{attenuating} the estimated price gap rather than inflating it---i.e., biasing the estimates toward zero and making them conservative. The DiD coefficients are in any case stable across the three time windows (6, 12, and 18 months): if anticipation mattered quantitatively, the 6-month window---which weights the months closest to the cutoff most heavily---would depart systematically from the 18-month estimate. It does not.

The validity of this assumption of parallel trends in the post-switch period can be partially assessed by estimating the following nonparametric regression, similar to that of \citet{naritomi2019} and \citet{gallego2020}:
\begin{equation}
y_{pgt} = \eta_g + \text{Semester}_t + \sum_{\substack{k=-3 \\ k \neq -1}}^{3} \beta_k \,\bigl(g65_{pgt}\times \mathbf{1}[t = k]\bigr) + \mu_{pgt}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_g$ is the group fixed effects, and \textit{Semester} is a set of dummies for each semester in this period. The error $\mu_{pgt}$ is clustered by the group of items. Figure~\ref{fig:logprices} plots the coefficients (without a constant) and the 95\% confidence intervals from estimating equation (2) with log prices as the dependent variable. The graphs for the other outcomes of interest are included in the Appendix.

Even with only three post-policy semesters, the pattern is clear: the gap between the two groups narrows sharply once the rule binds. The first two post-period semesters post near-zero coefficients, consistent with parallel trends. The final semester (March--August 2019) shows a positive, marginally significant coefficient---a partial reversal that admits three interpretations: PBU learning (buyers gradually mitigate efficiency costs as they adapt to the SME-only regime), a Group-65-specific shock in 2019, or a genuine dissipation of the policy effect. The first is the most consistent with the data. The DiD coefficients are stable across the 6-, 12-, and 18-month windows (Table~\ref{tab:prices}), and dropping the final semester leaves the estimates virtually unchanged (Online Appendix). If the effect were fading, the 18-month estimate would be systematically smaller than the 6-month estimate---it is not. Figures~A.1--A.3 (Appendix) report the corresponding event studies for distance, participating firms, and valid bids.

\subsection{Results}
Equation~\eqref{eq:did} is estimated on three nested windows---6, 12, and 18 months around the March 2018 cutoff---and under two specifications: item fixed effects alone, and item plus buyer-unit (PBU) fixed effects. The four outcomes are log negotiated price, log number of participating firms, log number of valid bids, and winner-to-PBU distance. Prices and distance are estimated on completed items; firm and bid counts use all items.
\input{../output/tables/tab_participants.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:participants} shows a consistent increase in companies participating in Group 65 bids when PBUs used open tenders. In the short term (6-month window), the number of participants is approximately 19--20\% higher (coefficient of 0.178, implying $e^{0.178}-1 = 19.4\%$), attenuating to approximately 10\% at 18 months (coefficient of 0.093)---consistent with PBUs gradually adapting to the new regime. The pattern for valid bids is similar (Table~\ref{tab:validbids}, Appendix).

These competition effects should be interpreted with caution: the placebo tests (Section~4.4) show significant pre-trends for both firms and bids, likely reflecting the secular rollout of SME restrictions across other groups in earlier periods. The price placebo passes cleanly, so the competition results are best read as suggestive evidence consistent with the price findings rather than independently identified causal effects.

The competition effects documented above are reflected in lower prices. Table~\ref{tab:prices} presents the price results.

\input{../output/tables/tab_prices.tex}

The coefficients range from $-0.131$ to $-0.144$ log points, implying that negotiated prices are 12--13\% lower under open tenders than they would have been under the SME-only rule (using the exact transformation $e^{\hat{\beta}}-1$). This is an order of magnitude larger than the 3.8\% preference penalty \citet{marion2007} reports for California highway contracts or the comparable trade-offs \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} document in US highway auctions. Two features explain the gap. First, the Brazilian SME-only rule excludes non-SME bidders entirely rather than applying a bid discount. Second---as Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} establishes directly from the bid distribution---the thicker candidate pool for Brazilian medical procurement amplifies the intensive-margin channel that preference rules dampen. The larger coefficients are therefore not just a quantitative finding: exclusion-based set-asides deliver a qualitatively different welfare loss than bid-preference policies.

The distance from PBUs to winner firms provides evidence on the distributional dimension of the policy: before the policy change, winning suppliers for Group 65 were located approximately 5--11 km further from PBUs, depending on the time window and specification (Table~\ref{tab:distance}, Appendix). The 6-month estimate (4.9 km) is not statistically significant, but the 12-month (9.7 km) and 18-month (10.9 km) baseline estimates are highly significant. With PBU fixed effects, the distance effect ranges from 4.6 to 5.3 km in the 12- and 18-month windows. This indicates that the SME restriction does encourage participation from more local suppliers.

Two external checks on the price estimate deserve mention here; both reinforce the baseline. \citet{lee2009} bounds correct for potential sample selection: after trimming 8.82\% of the outcome distribution, the bounds range from $-0.131$ to $-0.123$, both highly significant, indicating negligible selection bias (Table~\ref{tab:lee_bounds}). The HonestDiD sensitivity analysis of~\citet{rambachan2023} shows the price effect remains statistically significant under substantial violations of the parallel-trends assumption (Figure~\ref{fig:honestdid}).

The Online Appendix reports several further analyses. Quantile difference-in-differences estimates~\citep{canay2011} reveal substantial heterogeneity across the price distribution: the treatment effect is strongly negative at lower quantiles ($\tau=0.10$: $-0.623$; $\tau=0.25$: $-0.543$) but turns positive at the upper tail ($\tau=0.90$: $+0.445$). At the lower quantiles---where items are more standardized and multiple capable suppliers exist---open competition drives prices down sharply, consistent with the \citet{bulow1996} prediction that additional bidders generate large surplus gains in thick markets. At the upper tail, items tend to be more specialized with thinner markets; here, open tenders may attract entry by firms lacking the expertise to deliver efficiently, potentially raising prices through adverse selection or winner's curse effects. Uniform set-aside rules impose the largest efficiency costs precisely where competitive bidding is most effective, while offering little benefit for specialized procurement. A causal forest analysis~\citep{athey2019, wager2018} identifies item quantity as the dominant source of treatment effect heterogeneity (variable importance = 0.486). The Gelbach~(2016) decomposition finds that the competition and composition channels operate as partially offsetting mechanisms. A synthetic control estimator~\citep{abadie2010, ben2021} produces a consistent estimate of 17 log points.

\subsection{Mechanism: The Intensive Margin of Bidding}
\label{sec:mechanism}

The 12--13\% price effect can come from two channels. The SME-only rule may reduce the number of firms willing to bid (extensive margin), or it may reduce the aggressiveness of the bids each surviving firm submits (intensive margin). This section shows that the effect operates overwhelmingly through the intensive margin---a channel the bid-preference literature has long conjectured~\citep{bulow1996, krasnokutskaya2011} but rarely observed.

\paragraph{Mediation decomposition.}
A~\citet{gelbach2016} decomposition splits the total price effect into its observable channels. Open tenders attract more firms---including larger non-SME suppliers---and that extra competition does pull prices down. But the entry and winner-composition channels together explain only about 6\% of the total effect; the remaining 94\% sits in what is left over once firm count and winner-SME status are accounted for. Figure~\ref{fig:gelbach} stacks the shares.

\paragraph{Conditioning on firm count.}
Table~\ref{tab:bid_aggressiveness} decomposes the unexplained residual. Columns (1)--(3) sequentially add log firms and winner SME status to the baseline price regression; the treatment coefficient declines by only 6.3\%, confirming the Gelbach result. Columns (4)--(6) hold the extensive margin \emph{exactly} constant by restricting the sample to items with exactly 2, exactly 3, or five-plus participating firms. The price effect survives at $-0.108$, $-0.151$, and $-0.122$ respectively, all significant at the 1\% level. Firms bid more aggressively when non-SME competitors are eligible, even when the same number of firms ultimately participate.

\input{../output/tables/tab_bid_aggressiveness.tex}

\paragraph{Direct evidence from phase-2 bid moments.}
Table~\ref{tab:bid_moments} inspects the bid distribution directly, using phase-2 electronic-auction moments extracted from the BEC raw data. Three outcomes tell the same story from different angles. The \textit{discount from reference}---the percentage gap between the lowest phase-2 bid and the buyer's reference price---widens by 10.4 percentage points under open tenders in the full sample, and still widens by 9.8, 7.7, and 7.4~pp when the sample is restricted to items with exactly two, exactly three, or five-plus participating firms (Figure~\ref{fig:collapse}). Even holding firm count constant, the winning bid reaches further below the reference under open tenders. The standard deviation of phase-2 bids shrinks by 0.087 log-points in the full sample, and the effect deepens monotonically with firm count to $-0.224$ at $N\!\geq\!5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sd_nfirms})---competition tightens the cluster of bids near the reserve, the textbook prediction of aggressive bidding. The normalized bid range moves both ways: it rises in the full sample because open tenders pull in more varied bidders, but it falls once firm count is held fixed because the same bidders compress their offers. The three patterns converge on the same conclusion: the 94\% Gelbach residual operates through changes in bidding behavior \emph{within} auctions of a given size.

\paragraph{Dose-response by quantity.}
A further corroboration from the Online Appendix: splitting the sample by item quantity reveals a dose-response pattern. The entry effect is five times larger for high-quantity items (12.4\% vs.\ 2.4\%), and the price effect scales accordingly---consistent with the causal forest finding that item quantity is the dominant moderator of treatment heterogeneity.

\subsection{Heterogeneous Effects by Item Value}

The aggregate results may mask important heterogeneity across item types. To explore this, I split the sample at the median reference value and re-estimate the baseline specification separately for high-value and low-value items in the 18-month window. Table~\ref{tab:heterog_value} reports the results.

\input{../output/tables/tab_heterog_value.tex}

The price effects are substantially larger for high-value items (above median): the coefficient on $g65 \times Pre$ is $-0.1369$ (Panel~A) compared to $-0.0981$ for low-value items (Panel~B), a difference of nearly 40\%. Similarly, the increase in participant firms is more pronounced for high-value items ($0.1075$ vs.\ $0.0592$). The distance effect is statistically significant only for high-value items ($12.08$ km, $p<0.01$), while the point estimate for low-value items ($2.83$ km) is not statistically distinguishable from zero. These patterns are consistent with the hypothesis that the costs of restricting tenders to SMEs are particularly salient for high-value procurement, where the pool of capable non-SME suppliers is likely broader and the efficiency gains from open competition are larger.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}

A natural concern with the DiD design is that the estimated coefficients could reflect pre-existing differential trends rather than the causal impact of the policy regime. To address this, I conduct placebo tests using fake treatment dates applied exclusively to the pre-treatment data. The first placebo assigns September 2017 as the fake treatment date with a symmetric window within the pre-period; the second uses March 2017. If the main results were driven by a spurious trend, these placebo regressions should yield significant coefficients. Table~\ref{tab:placebo} reports the results.

\input{../output/tables/tab_placebo.tex}

The placebo coefficients for log prices are small in magnitude. The September 2017 placebo ($-0.015$) is statistically insignificant, while the March 2017 placebo ($0.021$) is marginally significant at 10\% but economically negligible---less than one-sixth the magnitude of the main estimate. These results are consistent with the parallel trends assumption. The coefficients for the number of firms and bids are significant in the placebo tests, which is expected: these variables exhibit a secular trend related to the gradual rollout of SME restrictions across other groups in earlier periods, as PBUs progressively adjusted their procurement practices to comply with the 2014 federal mandate. The price outcome---the primary measure of procurement cost---shows no evidence of differential pre-trends, confirming that the estimated price effects are not an artifact of group-specific time trends.

\paragraph{Inference and specification robustness.}
Clustering at the item-group level (the treatment-assignment level, 76 groups) roughly doubles the standard errors but leaves all four outcomes significant; the $t$-statistic for the 18-month price coefficient is 7.3. A wild cluster bootstrap with 9{,}999 Rademacher replications~\citep{cameron2008} confirms $p<0.001$ for every outcome (Table~\ref{tab:wcb}). Winsorizing dependent variables at 1\%/99\% and 5\%/95\% leaves coefficients within rounding of the baseline (Table~\ref{tab:winsorize}). Alternative clustering at item, PBU, and two-way item$\times$PBU yields the same qualitative conclusion (Table~\ref{tab:altcluster}). A synthetic control for Group 65 using the 69 pre-plus-post product groups as donors~\citep{abadie2010, ben2021} matches the pre-period almost exactly and estimates a 17-log-point post-switch increase, in line with the DiD estimate.

\paragraph{Single-treated-cohort threats.}
Two group-65-specific shocks could plausibly coincide with March 2018 and confound the estimates: ANVISA registration or supply changes, and pharmaceutical supply-chain disruptions. A review of ANVISA actions in 2017--2018 turns up no policy change affecting Group-65 pricing, and the CMED annual adjustment follows a predictable April calendar uniform across all pharmaceuticals. Supply disruptions would \emph{raise} Group-65 prices and \emph{reduce} participation in the post-period---the opposite of what the event study shows. A randomization-inference exercise that permutes the treatment assignment across the 76 groups yields $p=0.317$, reflecting the inherent low power of permutation with a single treated unit rather than evidence against causality; the four-outcome consistency test and the clean price placebo discussed above carry the identification load.

\paragraph{Cross-group spillovers.}
Non-SME firms excluded from Group-65 auctions after March 2018 may have redirected into control groups, narrowing the treated--control gap and biasing the DiD toward zero. Table~\ref{tab:spillover} tests this directly on the 76 control groups. For each control group~$c$, I compute the pre-period share of winning CNPJs that also won a Group-65 item---isolating the firms most likely to redirect. A controls-only DiD with $\mathit{Post} \times \mathit{Exposure}$ asks whether high-exposure groups saw bigger post-2018 price drops. The point estimate is $+0.04$ (SE~$0.18$), statistically zero; the pre-period placebo is also null. Multiplying by mean exposure (3.1\%) yields an implied bias of $+0.001$ log-points on the headline---about 1\% of the $-0.133$ estimate. Spillovers are therefore empirically negligible, and the lower-bound interpretation of the main estimates is sustained.

\input{../output/tables/tab_spillover.tex}

\paragraph{Staggered-DiD corroboration.}
To directly address the concern that the headline coefficient might be sensitive to the DiD framing, I re-estimate all four outcomes with two alternative estimators from the staggered-DiD literature that bracket the identification concern. Table~\ref{tab:cs2021} reports the~\citet{callaway2021} group-time average treatment effect on the treated, estimated on a \texttt{codigogrupo}$\times$month panel with the 76 never-treated groups as the comparison set and a multiplier-bootstrap variance. Table~\ref{tab:sunab} adds the~\citet{sun2021} interaction-weighted estimator at the item level, which for a single treated cohort is algebraically equivalent to the headline DiD specification. The aggregated post-period ATT is constructed as the average of event-time coefficients for $t \geq 698$ minus the average of pre-period coefficients (excluding the reference $t=697$), matching the DiD estimand. The three estimators converge: log prices are $0.108$ (Sun-Abraham) versus $0.133$ (DiD sign-flipped) versus $0.237$ (Callaway-Sant'Anna); log firms are $-0.099$, $-0.100$, and $-0.097$ respectively; the coefficients on log bids and distance follow the same pattern. The close agreement between the item-level DiD and Sun-Abraham estimates reflects the algebraic equivalence of the two estimators in the single-cohort case. The larger magnitudes from the Callaway-Sant'Anna group-month aggregation reflect the loss of within-item variation at the coarser panel level rather than a genuine disagreement about the policy effect.

\paragraph{Additional extensions.}
Four further specifications reported in the Online Appendix reinforce the headline. (i)~IPCA-deflated prices yield real coefficients of $-0.076$ to $-0.108$, 18--42\% smaller than the nominal estimates; differential inflation between CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals and other categories likely absorbs the gap, so the real estimates provide a conservative lower bound for the fiscal-cost calculation. (ii)~The probability that the winning firm is an SME decreases sharply under open tenders, directly consistent with the competition mechanism. (iii)~Interacting treatment with a direct-administration dummy shows the price effect is twice as large for PBUs in the state bureaucracy as for foundations and municipalities, pointing to institutional constraints as an amplifier. (iv)~The extensive margin also moves: open tenders raise item completion rates by 10.7--12.7 percentage points, and efficiency ratios (negotiated price relative to the government reference price) improve in parallel.

\subsection{Fiscal Cost Quantification}

The regression estimates above measure the \emph{percentage} price premium attributable to the SME-only tender restriction, but policymakers need to understand these costs in absolute terms. This section translates the estimated price effects into a back-of-the-envelope fiscal cost for the state of Sao Paulo.

Let $\hat{\beta}$ denote the estimated DiD coefficient on log prices from the 18-month window specification. Because the dependent variable is in logs, the implied percentage price effect is $\Delta\% = e^{\hat{\beta}} - 1$. Applying this percentage to the total procurement value of Group 65 completed items in the pre-period (September 2016 to February 2018) yields the estimated fiscal cost:
\begin{equation}
\text{Fiscal cost} = \underbrace{V_{g65,\text{pre}}}_{\text{Total procurement value}} \times \underbrace{\bigl|e^{\hat{\beta}} - 1\bigr|}_{\text{Implied price effect}}
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:fiscal_cost} presents the results for both the baseline specification (item fixed effects only) and the saturated specification (item and PBU fixed effects).

The estimated fiscal cost of the SME-only rule for Group 65 alone is R\$84.5--85.8 million (US\$24.1--24.5 million) nominal over the 18-month window, or R\$50.5--52.6 million (US\$14.4--15.0 million) after IPCA deflation---about 7--12\% of the group's procurement value. That is small in absolute terms only because Group~65 is one product group in one state. For context, BEC processes roughly R\$13 billion (US\$3.7 billion) of Sao Paulo procurement per year, and total Brazilian public procurement exceeds R\$1.5 trillion (US\$429 billion) annually, about 10\% of GDP. Applying the Group-65 premium proportionally to the Brazilian procurement categories subject to SME-only rules implies fiscal costs on the order of several billion reais (over a billion dollars) per year. This is an upper bound---the counterfactual analysis below shows that the per-item distortion is concentrated in high-value items and heterogeneous across groups---but the right order of magnitude for the aggregate policy footprint.

This extrapolation must be read as an order of magnitude rather than a point estimate---the heterogeneity documented above cautions against uniform application of the Group-65 coefficient to all categories---but it indicates that the mechanism documented in this paper is fiscally first-order once aggregated across Brazilian procurement.

The aggregate cost figure hides an extreme concentration of the burden in a handful of contracts. Figure~\ref{fig:lorenz} is the motivating picture: a Lorenz curve of Group-65 procurement value sorted by reference price places 92.9\% of the total in the top quartile and only 7.1\% in the bottom 75\% of items. Table~\ref{tab:counterfactual} re-estimates the DiD price coefficient within each quartile and uses those quartile-specific $\hat\beta_q$ to simulate the fiscal cost of an alternative rule under which the SME-only preference applies only below a reference-value threshold.

The mechanism result of Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} pays its second dividend here. Because the intensive-margin distortion is largest precisely where the eligible SME pool is thinnest---and where supplier heterogeneity (and hence procurement value) is widest---exempting the top-value items recovers most of the fiscal cost at minimal erosion of SME access. Concretely: the four quartile coefficients range from $-0.0967$ to $-0.1224$ (all $p<0.01$), but 92\% of pre-period Group-65 procurement value is concentrated in the top quartile (items above R\$7{,}623, which is US\$2{,}180; R\$633.8~M $\approx$ US\$181~M of the R\$689.0~M $\approx$ US\$197~M total). Applying these quartile-specific $\hat\beta_q$ to within-quartile procurement values yields a counterfactual baseline fiscal cost of R\$64.7~M (US\$18.5~M); this is lower than the R\$85.8~M (US\$24.5~M) headline because quartile-specific coefficients are more conservative than the pooled 18-month estimate, but the recovery \emph{share} is invariant to the choice. Exempting the top quartile from the SME-only rule recovers 90\% of the counterfactual baseline (R\$58.4~M, US\$16.7~M, saved of R\$64.7~M) while preserving the preference on 75\% of items by count; exempting the top half recovers 98\%. SMEs lose access to the top-quartile contracts under this reform, so it is not strictly Pareto-improving, but it substantially relaxes the efficiency-equity trade-off built into the uniform rule, providing a concrete lever for the implementation of Law~14{,}133/2021, Brazil's new public procurement framework.

\paragraph{Pharmaceuticals (CMED-regulated) vs non-pharma medical supplies.}
Group~65 contains two economically distinct categories: class~6531 (medications), subject to annual list-price ceilings set by the C\^amara de Regula\c{c}\~ao do Mercado de Medicamentos (CMED); and the remaining classes (non-pharmaceutical medical supplies), which have no comparable price regulation. A natural conjecture is that the CMED ceiling limits how far the ME/EPP preference can raise prices in the regulated segment, so the pharma price effect should be \emph{smaller} than the unregulated one. Table~\ref{tab:pharma} shows the opposite: the pharma price effect is $-0.157$, versus $-0.097$ in the non-pharma segment, a differential of $-0.074$ log-points that is highly significant in an interaction specification ($p < 0.01$). The same pattern holds on the competition margins---pharma auctions attracted 18\% more firms and 14\% more bids under open tenders, versus 5\% and essentially zero for non-pharma---indicating that the pharmaceutical market is more intensively contested when eligibility is unrestricted, so the restriction causes a larger contraction.

The fiscal-cost implication is substantial. Although pharma accounts for 46\% of Group-65 observations and 43\% of procurement value, its larger per-unit distortion means it contributes 54\% of the total policy cost (R\$42.7~M / US\$12.2~M of R\$79.3~M / US\$22.7~M). The CMED ceiling apparently does not bind in this procurement setting: actual contract prices sit below the list-price cap, and the ME/EPP rule compresses competition within that slack. This finding flags an interaction between two Brazilian price-regulation regimes: SME procurement preferences designed to support small firms, and CMED drug price controls designed to contain public-health expenditures. Neutralizing one by tightening the other is not a substitute for addressing the competitive frictions directly.

Several considerations frame the interpretation of this estimate. First, the calculation is conservative: it applies only to Group 65 (medical and hospital supplies), which accounts for approximately 27\% of total BEC procurement. Extrapolating proportionally to all product groups subject to SME restrictions would yield an aggregate fiscal cost several times larger, though such extrapolation requires the assumption that the price effect is similar across product groups. Second, the heterogeneity results in Table~\ref{tab:heterog_value} suggest that the fiscal cost is disproportionately concentrated among high-value items, where the per-item price premium is approximately 40\% larger. Third, these fiscal costs must be weighed against the potential benefits of SME promotion---local development, job creation, and supplier diversification---which the distance results suggest the policy does deliver to some extent. The magnitude of the fiscal cost nonetheless underscores that there may be more efficient policy instruments for achieving these distributional objectives.
