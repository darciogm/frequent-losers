\section{Conclusion}

This paper opened with a forty-year-old question: when a government forces its public buyers to procure from small firms only, do prices rise because fewer firms bid, or because each firm bids less aggressively? The answer, from 3.7~million procurement observations in Sao Paulo's BEC platform around a discrete March-2018 policy cutoff, points unambiguously to the second story. Only six percent of the price premium is explained by entry and winner composition; the remaining ninety-four percent is unexplained by those channels, and direct phase-2 bid-distribution evidence identifies within-auction bidding behavior as its source. SME set-asides do not merely empty desks in the bidding classroom; they change how each remaining student answers.

The bid-distribution evidence is the strongest structural contribution. When the March~2018 reinterpretation extended Sao Paulo's SME-only regime to medical supplies, the winning bid immediately moved ten percentage points closer to the buyer's reference price, and the standard deviation of phase-2 bids shrank monotonically in firm count. These effects survive conditioning on exactly two, three, or five-plus firms---a test the literature has not been able to run from cross-sectional winning-price data alone~\citep{marion2007, krasnokutskaya2011, athey2013}. The~\citet{gelbach2016} decomposition corroborates it: measured entry channels explain six percent of the 10--11\% price increase, and ninety-four percent lies outside them---consistent with the auction-theoretic prediction of~\citet{bulow1996} and~\citet{krasnokutskaya2011} that the intensive margin dominates.

Identification stands on unusually firm ground for a policy-evaluation paper in public procurement. The single-cohort staggered-DiD estimates are robust to the~\citet{callaway2021},~\citet{sun2021}, and~\citet{goodmanbacon2021} estimators; Goodman-Bacon confirms that 100\% of the coefficient comes from the clean treated-vs-never-treated $2\times2$ comparison, with no ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons. A formal spillover test on the 76 control groups bounds the implied bias below one percent of the headline, and RAIS firm-level controls leave the coefficient invariant. The caveat is structural: Group~65 is the sole treated cohort, so classical permutation inference has low power (randomization $p=0.342$). The evidence for causality therefore rests on the consistency of effects across four outcomes, the convergence of multiple estimators, and the legalistic nature of the 2018 reinterpretation---not on that permutation.

The mechanism result has two sharp implications for policy design. First, because 92\% of Group-65 procurement value concentrates in the top value quartile, a mechanical reference-value exemption---applying the SME-only rule only to items below the 75th percentile of Group-65 reference value (approximately R\$7{,}623, or US\$2{,}180)---recovers 90\% of the accounting fiscal cost while preserving SME access to 75\% of items by count. The calculation is static: it assumes within-quartile price coefficients are invariant to the reform and ignores the completion, entry, and reference-price responses that an equilibrium counterfactual would price. SMEs lose access to the top-quartile contracts, so the reform is not strictly Pareto-improving, and a full welfare analysis would require structural modeling of the behavioral margins. Even under these static assumptions, the reform substantially relaxes the efficiency-equity trade-off built into the uniform rule, and it illustrates how administrative procurement data can calibrate second-best reforms---a starting point rather than a blueprint for the ongoing implementation of Law~14{,}133/2021, Brazil's new public procurement framework.

Second, CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531) bear more than twice the price distortion of non-pharma medical supplies, consistent with contract prices lying below the CMED list-price cap and the SME restriction compressing competition within the residual slack. Two Brazilian regulatory regimes---SME procurement preferences and drug price controls---interact perversely: tightening either without accounting for the other leaves procurement cost higher than either instrument alone would deliver. This interaction is new to the literature on procurement preferences and is directly relevant to Brazil, India, Mexico, and other jurisdictions that combine SME preferences with pharmaceutical price regulation.

The static fiscal cost for Group~65 alone is R\$50--74 million (US\$14--21 million) over 18 months, or 7--11\% of the group's procurement value. Scaled to the categories subject to SME-only rules---BEC processes R\$13 billion (US\$3.7 billion) of Sao Paulo procurement per year, and total Brazilian public procurement exceeds R\$1.5 trillion (US\$429 billion) annually, about 10\% of GDP---the implied fiscal cost runs into the several-billion-reais range (above US\$1 billion) at the consolidated federal-state-municipal level. On the distributional side, the 5--11~km increase in winner-to-buyer distance under open tenders confirms that the SME preference does deliver geographic proximity, and the value-threshold reform proposed above would largely preserve that benefit.

Three limitations frame these results. First, the design carries the standard constraints of a single-cohort event study, and external validity to other product groups, states, and procurement regimes requires the untestable assumption of comparable treatment effects. Second, the heterogeneity documented across item value, firm age, and industry sector counsels against naive extrapolation of the \emph{magnitudes}---though the mechanism, the contraction of competitive bidding pressure under set-asides, should generalize. Third, the cost-side estimates ignore the supply-side benefits SME preferences are designed to deliver: employment creation, firm survival, and supplier-network expansion. The present paper uses RAIS matched employer-employee data only for identification robustness. Extending the RAIS window to 2018--2022 would allow direct evaluation of those supply-side outcomes and a rigorous benefit-cost comparison.

Three research extensions are natural. First, a structural auction model with endogenous SME participation and regulated reserve prices would let us run counterfactual welfare comparisons across policy designs, including variants of the value-threshold reform and alternative CMED-procurement interactions. Second, cross-country replication---Mexico, Colombia, and Argentina all maintain SME-only procurement regimes---would sharpen external validity. Third, within Brazil the rollout of Law~14{,}133/2021 creates a second natural experiment through which the reform proposed here can itself be evaluated.

The set-aside debate has long been framed as a binary choice: protect small firms or procure efficiently. The mechanism result and the value-threshold counterfactual developed in this paper show the choice is not binary. The intensive-margin distortion is largest precisely where the eligible SME pool is thinnest---and where supplier heterogeneity, and therefore procurement value, is widest. Exempting that narrow subset of contracts recovers most of the fiscal cost at minimal erosion of SME access. Put differently: the twenty-five percent of contracts that generate ninety-two percent of Group-65 procurement value---and the bulk of the fiscal distortion---deserve a different rule than the other seventy-five percent. Policy that treats them alike pays a premium for an efficiency--equity trade-off that can be undone.
