\section{Conclusion}

This paper opened with a forty-year-old question: when a government forces its public buyers to procure from small firms only, do prices rise because fewer firms bid, or because each firm bids less aggressively? The answer, from 3.7~million procurement observations in Sao Paulo's BEC platform around a discrete March-2018 policy cutoff, is unambiguous. Ninety-four percent of the price premium operates through the intensive margin---the aggressiveness of bids within auctions of a given size---and only six percent through entry. SME set-asides do not merely empty desks in the bidding classroom; they change how each remaining student answers.

The direct bid-distribution evidence settles the channel debate. When the March~2018 reinterpretation extended Sao Paulo's SME-only regime to medical supplies, the winning bid immediately moved ten percentage points closer to the buyer's reference price, and the standard deviation of phase-2 submitted bids shrank monotonically in the number of participating firms. These effects persist under conditioning on exactly two, three, or five-plus firms---a direct test that the literature, working from cross-sectional variation in winning prices, could not perform~\citep{marion2007, krasnokutskaya2011, athey2013}. A~\citet{gelbach2016} decomposition closes the loop: measured entry channels account for six percent of the 12--13\% price increase, and bidding behavior within auctions accounts for ninety-four percent, exactly as the auction theory of~\citet{bulow1996} and~\citet{krasnokutskaya2011} predicts.

Identification stands on unusually firm ground for a policy-evaluation paper in public procurement. The single-cohort staggered-DiD estimates are robust to the~\citet{callaway2021},~\citet{sun2021}, and~\citet{goodmanbacon2021} estimators; Goodman-Bacon confirms that 100\% of the coefficient comes from the clean treated-vs-never-treated $2\times2$ comparison, with no ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons. A formal spillover test on the 76 control groups bounds the implied bias below one percent of the headline, and RAIS firm-level controls leave the coefficient invariant. The caveat is structural: Group~65 is the sole treated cohort, so classical permutation inference has low power (randomization $p=0.317$). The evidence for causality therefore rests on the consistency of effects across four outcomes, the convergence of multiple estimators, and the legalistic nature of the 2018 reinterpretation---not on that permutation.

The mechanism result has two sharp implications for policy design. First, because 92\% of Group-65 procurement value concentrates in the top value quartile, a simple reference-value exemption---applying the SME-only rule only to items below the 75th percentile of Group-65 reference value (approximately R\$7{,}623, or US\$2{,}180)---recovers 90\% of the fiscal cost while preserving SME access to 75\% of items by count. This is not strictly Pareto-improving (SMEs lose access to the top-quartile contracts), but it substantially relaxes the efficiency-equity trade-off built into the uniform rule, and it illustrates how administrative procurement data can calibrate second-best reforms that preserve the equity goals of SME policies while curbing their fiscal footprint. The reform is a concrete lever for the implementation of Law~14{,}133/2021, Brazil's new public procurement framework.

Second, CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531) bear 62\% larger price distortion than non-pharma medical supplies, consistent with contract prices lying below the CMED list-price cap and the SME restriction compressing competition within the residual slack. Two Brazilian regulatory regimes---SME procurement preferences and drug price controls---interact perversely: tightening either without accounting for the other leaves procurement cost higher than either instrument alone would deliver. This interaction is new to the literature on procurement preferences and is directly relevant to Brazil, India, Mexico, and other jurisdictions that combine SME preferences with pharmaceutical price regulation.

The aggregate fiscal cost for Group~65 alone is R\$50--85 million (US\$14--24 million) over 18 months, or 7--12\% of the group's procurement value. Proportionally extrapolating across the procurement categories subject to SME-only rules---BEC processes R\$13 billion (US\$3.7 billion) of Sao Paulo procurement per year, and total Brazilian public procurement exceeds R\$1.5 trillion (US\$429 billion) annually, about 10\% of GDP---indicates fiscal costs on the order of several billion reais (over a billion US dollars) per year at the consolidated federal-state-municipal level. The 5--11~km increase in winner-to-buyer distance under open tenders confirms that the SME preference does deliver geographic proximity, and the value-threshold reform proposed above would largely preserve that benefit.

Three limitations frame these results. First, the design carries the standard constraints of a single-cohort event study, and external validity to other product groups, states, and procurement regimes requires the untestable assumption of comparable treatment effects. Second, the heterogeneity documented across item value, firm age, and industry sector counsels against naive extrapolation of the \emph{magnitudes}---though the mechanism, the contraction of competitive bidding pressure under set-asides, should generalize. Third, the cost-side estimates ignore the supply-side benefits SME preferences are designed to deliver: employment creation, firm survival, and supplier-network expansion. The present paper uses RAIS matched employer-employee data only for identification robustness. Extending the RAIS window to 2018--2022 would allow direct evaluation of those supply-side outcomes and a rigorous benefit-cost comparison.

Research extensions are natural. Structural estimation of an auction model with endogenous SME participation and regulated reserve prices would deliver counterfactual welfare comparisons across policy designs, including variants of the value-threshold reform and alternative CMED-procurement interactions. Cross-country replication---Mexico, Colombia, and Argentina all maintain SME-only procurement regimes---would sharpen external-validity claims. And within Brazil, the rollout of Law~14{,}133/2021 creates a second natural experiment through which the reform proposed here can itself be evaluated.

The set-aside debate has long been framed as a binary choice: protect small firms, or procure efficiently. The mechanism result and the value-threshold counterfactual developed in this paper show the choice is not binary. Because the intensive-margin distortion is largest precisely where the eligible SME pool is thinnest---and where supplier heterogeneity, and hence procurement value, is widest---exempting the narrow subset of contracts that generate most of the cost recovers most of the distortion at minimal erosion of SME access. Put differently: the seven percent of contracts that generate ninety-two percent of Group-65 procurement value---and the bulk of the fiscal distortion---deserve a different rule than the other ninety-three percent. Policy that treats them the same pays a premium for an efficiency--equity trade-off that can be undone.
