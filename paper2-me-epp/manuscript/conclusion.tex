\section{Conclusion}

Restricting public tenders to SMEs costs the state of Sao Paulo approximately R\$310 million (${\sim}$US\$62 million) over 18 months---about 12\% of procurement value. This estimate, derived from a natural experiment in which medical supplies were uniquely exempt from mandatory SME-only tenders until a legal reinterpretation in March 2018, is robust to placebo tests, alternative clustering, sample selection correction, and sensitivity analysis. Open tenders yielded prices 12--13\% lower, attracted 19--20\% more firms, and generated 16--17\% more valid bids. Most of the price reduction operates not through the extensive margin (more bidders) but through the intensive margin (fiercer bidding), as demonstrated by a Gelbach decomposition and a direct bid aggressiveness test that holds the number of participating firms constant.

The heterogeneity analysis reveals that these costs are not uniform. The price effects are approximately 40\% larger for high-value items than for low-value items, and the distance effects are statistically significant only for the high-value subsample. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that the costs of restricting competition are most salient in procurement segments where a broad pool of capable non-SME suppliers exists and where the stakes of each transaction are highest.

The one dimension along which the SME policy appears to deliver on its stated objectives is geographic proximity: before the policy change, winning firms for Group 65 were located approximately 5--11 km further from PBUs (depending on the specification), suggesting that the restriction to SME-only tenders has successfully encouraged participation from more local suppliers.

Translating these price effects into monetary terms yields a concrete measure of the policy's fiscal burden. For Group 65 alone, the estimated fiscal cost of the SME-only tender restriction amounts to R\$84.5--85.8 million in nominal terms (approximately US\$17 million) over the 18-month pre-period---about 12\% of this product group's total procurement value. Using IPCA-deflated prices, the fiscal cost is approximately R\$50.5 million, reflecting the fact that part of the nominal gap is attributable to differential inflation between medical supplies and other product categories. Given that Group 65 represents only 27\% of total BEC procurement, these figures understate the aggregate cost of the policy. A rough extrapolation---applying the Group 65 price effect proportionally to all product groups---yields an estimated aggregate fiscal cost on the order of R\$310 million in nominal terms (approximately US\$62 million) over the same 18-month period, or approximately R\$185 million in IPCA-deflated terms. This extrapolation requires the strong assumption that the treatment effect is comparable across product groups, which the heterogeneity analysis suggests may overstate the cost for low-value items and understate it for high-value ones. The figure should therefore be interpreted as an order-of-magnitude benchmark rather than a precise estimate, but it underscores the fiscal significance of the policy. These estimates provide policymakers with a concrete benchmark against which to weigh the distributional benefits of SME promotion.

These findings carry implications that extend beyond the specific institutional context of Sao Paulo. The mechanisms documented---reduced competition, weaker bid aggressiveness, and worse supplier matching---are generic consequences of restricting the supplier pool~\citep{bulow1996, krasnokutskaya2011}, and they are likely to operate wherever set-aside policies bind. At the same time, the results point to a nuanced policy design space with three concrete implications.

First, the heterogeneity analysis and quantile DiD results jointly suggest that \emph{uniform} set-aside rules are suboptimal. The efficiency costs of SME restrictions are concentrated among high-value, standardized items at the lower end of the price distribution, where competitive bidding is most effective and the pool of capable non-SME suppliers is broadest. At the upper tail---specialized items with thin supplier markets---open tenders may actually raise prices through adverse selection. A differentiated policy that exempts high-value standardized items from SME-only rules while maintaining preferences for low-value or specialized procurement would better balance efficiency and distributional objectives.

Second, the institutional design of \emph{opt-out costs} documented in this paper provides a direct policy lever. The current system imposes substantial justification costs on PBUs that wish to conduct open tenders, effectively penalizing the efficient procurement choice. Reducing these opt-out costs---for instance, by allowing PBUs to default to open tenders for items above a value threshold without requiring audit court approval---would lower procurement costs while preserving the SME preference for the smaller items where it is less distortionary.

Third, and more broadly, the results suggest that alternative instruments to promote SME participation---such as direct subsidies, simplified registration procedures, or targeted capacity-building programs---may achieve similar developmental goals at lower fiscal cost to the public sector~\citep{athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, marion2007}. The distance results confirm that SME-only rules do succeed in promoting local sourcing, but the price premium of 12--13\% suggests that this geographic benefit comes at a steep cost per kilometer of proximity gained.

This paper contributes to a growing literature documenting the costs of restricting competition in public procurement~\citep{bosio2022, szucs2024, decarolis2024}. The findings are particularly timely in the Brazilian context: Federal Law 14,133/2021---Brazil's new public procurement framework, which is gradually replacing Law 8,666/1993---maintains the mandatory SME preferences but introduces new provisions for electronic procurement and contract management. The evidence presented here can inform the implementation of this new framework by identifying the product categories and value thresholds where SME restrictions are most costly.

Several avenues for future research remain open. First, longer post-treatment data would permit a sharper evaluation of whether PBUs learn to mitigate the costs of the restriction over time, a hypothesis suggested by the attenuation of the participation effects in the wider time windows. Second, firm-level data would allow researchers to trace the dynamic effects on SME growth, entry, and exit---the supply-side benefits that these policies are designed to foster. Third, structural estimation of the auction model could quantify the welfare consequences of SME preferences under alternative market structures, complementing the reduced-form evidence provided here. What is clear from the present analysis is that protecting small firms through procurement restrictions is not free---and that the price of protection falls disproportionately on the public budgets least able to afford it.
