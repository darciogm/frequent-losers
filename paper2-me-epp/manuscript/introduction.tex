\introheading

When a government forces its public buyers to procure from small firms only, do prices rise because fewer firms bid, or because each firm bids less aggressively? Four decades of research on bid preferences and set-asides---from \citet{marion2007} through \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}, \citet{athey2013}, and \citet{nakabayashi2013}, covering billions of dollars in policy evaluation---have not answered the question directly. The reason is data: existing strategies back out the bidding channel from cross-sectional variation in winning prices alone, a design that conflates entry with aggressiveness by construction. The bid distribution itself has remained a black box.

The answer matters. Set-asides for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are the most widespread procurement-preference instrument worldwide: the European Union channels a large share of public contract value to SMEs under similar arrangements~\citep{pwc2014,loader2013,loader2015}, OECD and SIGMA surveys document comparable regimes across more than one hundred jurisdictions~\citep{oecd2018,sigma2016,hoekman2020}, and Brazilian federal law mandates exclusive SME tenders for items valued below R\$80{,}000 (US\$22{,}900),\footnote{All USD figures in this paper use a reference exchange rate of R\$3.50 per US dollar, the average of the BCB monthly closing rate over the September 2016--August 2019 sample period. The 2016--2019 annual averages were R\$3.49 (2016), R\$3.19 (2017), R\$3.65 (2018) and R\$3.88 (2019).} covering approximately 97\% of firms representing half of formal employment~\citep{bastos2018, thai2017, bosio2022}. A substantial literature shows that these policies boost firm growth, employment, and supplier-network expansion~\citep{mel2008,freedman2013,szerman2023,cardoza2016,fafchamps2014,mckenzie2015,ferraz2016,cabral2017}. But the design of the fix depends on the channel. If the price premium runs through the extensive margin, expanding the SME roster---through subsidies, registration relief, or capacity-building---can lower the fiscal cost without weakening the preference. If instead it runs through the intensive margin, no roster expansion will help: the only lever is to shrink the \emph{scope} of the rule itself. Policy design hinges on the answer---for the procurement officers in Brazil's 5{,}570 municipalities implementing Law~14{,}133/2021, for the 130 countries with SME-preference regimes in place, and for development economists wrestling with the trade-off between competitive efficiency and distributional objectives in public spending.

Theory points both ways. \citet{bulow1996} show that, across a wide class of auction environments, an additional bidder is worth more to the auctioneer than any sophisticated mechanism---a headline result for the extensive margin. \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} formalize bid preferences in the procurement context and show that both margins are operative. Which dominates quantitatively is an empirical question, and outside the US the evidence for SME set-asides is essentially absent~\citep{nakabayashi2013, szucs2024, decarolis2024}.

This paper shows that the price effect is not a pure headcount effect and that an important within-auction bidding channel---the \emph{intensive margin}, how each participating firm bids---is the most plausible reading of what else is going on. Think of each auction as a bidding classroom: the SME-only rule does not merely empty some desks; it changes how each remaining student answers. Using phase-2 electronic-auction bid-distribution moments from 3.7~million procurement observations on Sao Paulo's BEC platform, I show that when Brazil's March~2018 reinterpretation extended the state's SME-only regime to medical supplies---the only previously exempt product group---the winning bid immediately moved about six percentage points closer to the buyer's reference price, an effect that persists after conditioning on exactly two, three, or five-plus firms. A \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition finds that six percent of the resulting 10--11\% price increase is explained by entry and winner composition; the remaining ninety-four percent is unexplained by these observable channels. Phase-2 bid moments---direct measurements of within-auction bidder behavior, not residuals from a mediation exercise---then document that the bid distribution itself changes when the restriction binds, pointing to the intensive margin as the most plausible interpretation of the residual. Pairing a discrete policy cutoff with within-auction bid-distribution data sidesteps the identification problems that have constrained cross-sectional studies of bid preferences~\citep{marion2007, krasnokutskaya2011, athey2013}.

The quasi-experiment I exploit has three features that sharpen identification. First, the SME set-aside in Sao Paulo operates through \emph{opt-out costs}: public buyer units (PBUs) that wish to run open tenders must justify the departure in a formal report to the audit courts, which is costly, scrutinized, and sanctionable. The rule effectively penalizes the efficient procurement choice, shifting the default from open to restricted tendering. Second, between August~2014 and February~2018, Group~65 (medical and hospital supplies) was singled out as the sole product group exempted from the state's SME-only rule, under a joint agreement between the state government and the audit court (TCE-SP) that medical supplies warranted open competition. Third, in March 2018 a legal opinion from a separate oversight agency (PGE-SP) reversed the exemption on isonomy grounds, extending opt-out costs to Group~65 along with every other product group. Because the reversal was driven by a legalistic reinterpretation rather than by any change in procurement outcomes for Group~65, the switch is plausibly exogenous.

The March-2018 event is, in modern language, a single-cohort staggered difference-in-differences~\citep{callaway2021,sun2021,goodmanbacon2021,borusyak2024}: Group~65 is the lone treated cohort, and the 76 other product groups---subject to the SME-only rule since the 2014 federal mandate---are never-treated controls within the sample window. Identification therefore comes from a clean treated-vs-never-treated comparison, free of the ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons that motivate the modern staggered-DiD literature. The Goodman-Bacon decomposition confirms the structure, and the~\citet{kim2019} difference-in-differences-in-reverse framework reformulates the same estimand under the complementary view that the 76 controls are ``always-treated.'' Both framings deliver the same coefficient and are numerically equivalent to the Sun-Abraham interaction-weighted estimator for a single-cohort design.

Imposing the SME-only rule on Group~65 raises procurement prices by 10--11\% in the 18-month window (13--14\% in the 6-month window, 7--10\% in IPCA-deflated real terms), reduces participating firms by 10--11\%, and cuts valid bids by 7--8\%. Winning firms under open tenders sit 6--14~km further from buyers, so the SME preference does deliver geographic proximity even as it raises fiscal cost. The mechanism shows up directly in the phase-2 bid moments: under open tenders the winning bid reaches about six percentage points deeper below the reference price, an effect that survives conditioning on exactly two, three, or five-plus firms. These bid-level moments are direct measurements of within-auction bidder behavior. Under the two-way fixed-effects specification, the bid-SD evidence is less clean than the bid-discount evidence (the sample-wide SD coefficient does not clearly tighten with firm count), so the paper rests its intensive-margin interpretation on the discount shift and the Gelbach residual rather than on SD compression. The \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition corroborates the interpretation: six percent of the price reduction runs through entry and winner composition, and ninety-four percent is unexplained by those channels, consistent with a within-auction bidding-behavior story.

Identification is supported by a battery of checks, most of them stronger for prices than for the secondary outcomes (firms and bids carry pre-trend contamination that neither placebo nor Sun-Abraham diagnostics can fully resolve; the paper therefore rests its mechanism reading primarily on prices). Three estimators from the staggered-DiD literature (CS2021, Sun-Abraham, Goodman-Bacon) deliver the same sign on all four outcomes; the item-level Sun-Abraham estimate is numerically equivalent to the headline DiD, while the group-month aggregations from CS2021 and Goodman-Bacon differ in magnitude as expected. One hundred percent of the Goodman-Bacon weight falls on the clean treated-vs-never-treated comparison: no ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons enter. A formal spillover test on the 76 control groups bounds the implied bias from non-SME firms redirecting into control markets at less than 1\% of the headline, preserving the lower-bound interpretation. Linking winners to RAIS (Brazil's matched employer-employee registry) leaves the coefficient invariant to firm-size, age, and two-digit CNAE controls, and the shift in winner composition is recoverable directly from a RAIS-validated SME flag. One caveat is structural: with a single switching unit, classical permutation inference has low power, and the randomization $p$-value is 0.342. The evidence for causality therefore rests on the consistency of effects across four outcomes and the convergence of multiple estimators rather than on that permutation. Placebo tests, alternative clustering, wild cluster bootstrap, winsorization, Lee bounds, quantile DiD, synthetic control, and causal forest analysis provide further corroboration in the Appendix and Online Appendix.

The static fiscal cost of the SME-only rule for Group~65 alone is R\$50--74 million (US\$14--21 million) over the 18-month window (R\$50 million real, R\$74 million nominal), or 7--11\% of the group's procurement value. The number is mechanical---a reduced-form price coefficient times pre-period procurement value---and ignores the completion, entry, and reference-price responses that a structural counterfactual would price. Two findings sharpen the policy implications. First, 92\% of that procurement value is concentrated in the top value quartile, so a mechanical value-threshold exemption recovers 90\% of the accounting cost while preserving the SME preference on 75\% of items by count. SMEs lose access to the top-quartile contracts under this reform---so it is not strictly Pareto-improving, and a full welfare analysis would require modeling the behavioral responses---but even in static terms the reform substantially relaxes the efficiency-equity trade-off that motivates SME-only rules. Second, CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531 within Group~65) bear more than twice the distortion of the non-pharma medical supplies: the CMED list-price cap does not shield procurement from the ME/EPP preference, consistent with contract prices lying below the cap and the restriction compressing competition within the residual slack. Two Brazilian regulatory regimes---SME procurement preferences and drug price controls---therefore interact perversely, raising public-health spending above what either instrument alone would deliver.

The paper makes four contributions to the literatures on set-asides, bid preferences, and public procurement efficiency.

\noindent\textbf{(i) Direct bid-distribution evidence on the intensive margin.} Prior work on SME preferences has largely inferred the mechanism from changes in the winning price or the number of bidders~\citep{marion2007, athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, nakabayashi2013}. Using phase-2 bid moments---the minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of the full bid distribution within each electronic auction---I show directly that surviving bidders submit more aggressive offers, with a tighter distribution, when the SME restriction does not bind. The finding qualifies as what~\citet{davis1971} called a \emph{False Correlation}: although entry and bid aggressiveness are theoretically linked, they appear quantitatively independent in this setting---94\% of the set-aside price effect is unexplained by entry and winner-SME composition, and the direct phase-2 evidence shows that bids shift even when firm count is held fixed. This bid-level evidence complements the auction-theoretic prediction of \citet{bulow1996} and \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} and reconciles the coexistence of large price effects with modest entry effects that the literature has often struggled to explain.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) A sharp counterfactual policy-design result.} Because 92\% of Group-65 procurement value concentrates in the top quartile, a mechanical value-threshold exemption---applying the SME-only rule only to items below approximately R\$7{,}600 (US\$2{,}180)---recovers 90\% of the accounting fiscal cost while preserving the SME preference on 75\% of items by count. The exercise is static accounting: it assumes within-quartile price effects are invariant to the reform, which would fail in equilibrium; and it ignores behavioral responses (completion, entry, reference pricing). It nonetheless identifies the lever---top-value contracts drive most of the distortion, so threshold-based exemption is the natural reform---in a form that the ongoing implementation of Law~14{,}133/2021, Brazil's new public procurement framework, could adopt as a starting point and refine with structural modeling.

\noindent\textbf{(iii) A perverse interaction between two Brazilian price-regulation regimes.} CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531 within Group~65) bear more than twice the price distortion of the non-pharma medical subsegment under the SME-only rule. The list-price cap imposed by CMED---designed to contain public-health expenditures---does not shield procurement from the ME/EPP preference, because actual contract prices sit below the cap and the restriction compresses competition within the residual slack. This interaction is new to the literature on procurement preferences~\citep{bosio2022, szucs2024, decarolis2024, colonnelli2022} and has direct implications for countries that combine SME preferences with pharmaceutical price controls.

\noindent\textbf{(iv) A fiscal-cost estimate from a large developing economy.} The paper delivers quasi-experimental estimates of SME set-aside costs in Sao Paulo (R\$50--74 million, or US\$14--21 million, over 18 months for a single product group), rare in the literature outside the United States and large firms in Europe~\citep{athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, bandiera2009, best2023}. The design---a single switching cohort against 76 never-treated controls---is a \emph{staggered-DiD reversal} rarely exploited in applied work~\citep{kim2019}, and the estimates are robust to the Callaway-Sant'Anna~(\citeyear{callaway2021}), Sun-Abraham~(\citeyear{sun2021}), and Goodman-Bacon~(\citeyear{goodmanbacon2021}) estimators, to a formal cross-group spillover test, and to winner-level controls for firm size, age, and sector extracted from Brazil's matched employer-employee registry (RAIS).
