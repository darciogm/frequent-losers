\introheading

When a government forces its public buyers to procure from small firms only, do prices rise because fewer firms bid, or because each firm bids less aggressively? Despite four decades of research on bid preferences and set-asides---beginning with \citet{marion2007}, continuing through \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}, \citet{athey2013} and \citet{nakabayashi2013}, and spanning billions of dollars of policy evaluation---the question has no direct empirical answer. Existing identification strategies infer the bidding channel from cross-sectional variation in winning prices, an approach that conflates entry with aggressiveness by construction; the bid distribution itself has remained a black box.

The answer matters. Set-asides for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are the most widespread procurement-preference instrument worldwide: the European Union channels a large share of public contract value to SMEs under similar arrangements~\citep{pwc2014}, and Brazilian federal law mandates exclusive SME tenders for items valued below R\$80{,}000, covering approximately 97\% of firms representing half of formal employment~\citep{bastos2018, thai2017, bosio2022}. A substantial literature documents benefits of these policies for firm growth, employment, and supplier-network expansion~\citep{mel2008,freedman2013,szerman2023,cardoza2016}, but if the price premium operates primarily through the extensive margin, then expanding the SME roster---through subsidies, registration relief, or capacity-building---can lower fiscal cost while preserving the preference. If instead it operates through the intensive margin, no amount of SME-roster expansion will help: the only fix is to shrink the \emph{scope} of the rule itself. Policy design hinges on the answer---for the procurement officers in Brazil's 5{,}570 municipalities implementing Law~14{,}133/2021, for the 130 countries with SME-preference regimes in place, and for development economists wrestling with the trade-off between competitive efficiency and distributional objectives in public spending.

The theoretical priors are mixed. \citet{bulow1996} show that, in a wide class of auction environments, attracting one additional bidder generates greater surplus for the auctioneer than any mechanism-design refinement, emphasizing the extensive margin. \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} formalize bid preferences in the procurement context and point to both margins as operative. Direct evidence on which dominates quantitatively is scarce, and outside the US essentially absent for SME set-asides~\citep{nakabayashi2013, szucs2024, decarolis2024}.

This paper establishes the \emph{intensive margin} as the dominant channel. Imagine each procurement auction as a bidding classroom: the SME-only rule does not merely empty some desks---it changes how each remaining student answers. Using phase-2 electronic-auction bid-distribution moments from 3.7~million procurement observations in Sao Paulo's BEC platform, I show that when the March~2018 reinterpretation of Brazilian procurement law extended Sao Paulo's SME-only regime to medical supplies---the only previously exempt product group---the winning bid immediately moved ten percentage points closer to the buyer's reference price, and the standard deviation of submitted bids shrank monotonically in the number of participating firms. These effects persist after conditioning the sample on exactly two, three, or five-plus participating firms. A \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition reconciles the two findings: only six percent of the resulting 12--13\% price increase is explained by entry and winner composition, and ninety-four percent by bidding behavior within auctions of a given size. By combining a discrete policy cutoff with within-auction bid-distribution data, the design neutralizes the identification problems that have constrained cross-sectional studies of bid preferences~\citep{marion2007, krasnokutskaya2011, athey2013} and isolates the channel through which SME set-asides raise public procurement costs.

I exploit a quasi-experimental variation in Sao Paulo, Brazil, where the SME set-aside policy operates through a distinctive institutional mechanism: \emph{opt-out costs}. Public buyer units (PBUs) that wish to conduct open tenders rather than SME-only tenders must justify their choice through a costly formal process, subject to scrutiny by audit courts and potential sanctions. These opt-out costs penalize the efficient procurement choice, shifting the default from open competition to restricted tenders. I exploit the timing of a policy change (March 2018) that extended these opt-out costs to a previously exempt product group (Group 65, medical and hospital supplies), using time and cross-sectional variation to estimate the fiscal cost of this institutional design.

Between August 2014 and February 2018, the state of Sao Paulo exempted Group 65 from the mandatory SME-only tender requirement that applied to all other product groups, based on a joint agreement between the state government and the audit court that health items constituted strategic goods warranting open competition. In March 2018, a legal opinion from a different oversight agency reversed this interpretation, subjecting Group 65 to the same SME-favoring rules as all other groups. This policy change was driven by a legalistic reinterpretation of isonomy principles rather than by changes in procurement outcomes for Group 65, making it plausibly exogenous to the outcomes studied.

Methodologically, the March 2018 event creates a single-cohort staggered difference-in-differences~\citep{callaway2021,sun2021,goodmanbacon2021,borusyak2024}. Group 65 is the lone treated cohort; the 76 other product groups, already subject to the SME-only rule since the 2014 federal mandate, serve as never-treated controls within the sample window. This structure provides identification from a clean treated-vs-never-treated comparison without the ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons that motivate the modern staggered-DiD literature, a property I verify using the Goodman-Bacon decomposition. The Kim-Lee~(\citeyear{kim2019}) difference-in-differences-in-reverse framework formalizes the same estimand under the complementary view that the controls are ``always-treated''; both framings deliver the same coefficient and are numerically equivalent to the Sun-Abraham interaction-weighted estimator for a single-cohort design.

Between August 2014 and February 2018, the government's default was SME-only tenders for items valued at R\$80{,}000 or less, except for Group 65, which retained mandatory open tenders. PBUs could avoid restricted tenders for other groups by justifying the choice to audit courts, but this costly process---the opt-out costs of the SME-only regime---discourages open competition. From March 2018 onward, Group 65 became subject to these opt-out costs just as any other product group.

The estimated effect of imposing the SME-only rule on Group~65 is a 12--13\% increase in procurement prices (7--10\% in IPCA-deflated real terms), an 8--10\% reduction in participating firms, and a 16--17\% reduction in valid bids. Winning firms locate 5--11 km further from buyers when tenders are open---consistent with the view that SME-only rules succeed in promoting local sourcing even as they raise fiscal costs. To isolate the mechanism, I use the phase-2 electronic-bidding moments recorded in BEC: under open tenders the winning bid reaches approximately 10 percentage points deeper below the PBU's reference price, and the standard deviation of the bid distribution shrinks monotonically in firm count. These patterns persist after conditioning on 2, 3, or 5-plus participating firms---i.e., holding the extensive margin fixed---directly confirming the intensive-margin interpretation. A \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition corroborates the point: only 6\% of the price reduction is explained by extensive-margin channels (more firms, different winner composition); 94\% reflects bidding behavior within auctions of a given size.

Identification is supported by a battery of checks. Three alternative estimators from the staggered-DiD literature (CS2021, Sun-Abraham, Goodman-Bacon) deliver the same sign and significance on all four outcomes; the item-level Sun-Abraham estimate is numerically equivalent to the headline DiD, while the group-month aggregations used by CS2021 and Goodman-Bacon differ in magnitude as expected. 100\% of the Goodman-Bacon weight falls on the clean treated-vs-never-treated comparison---no ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons enter. A formal spillover test restricted to the 76 control groups finds that the implied bias from non-SME firms redirecting into control markets is less than 1\% of the headline coefficient, sustaining the lower-bound interpretation of the estimates. Linking winners to RAIS (Brazil's matched employer-employee registry) shows the coefficient is invariant to firm-size, age, and two-digit CNAE sector controls; the shift in winner composition is also recoverable directly from a RAIS-validated SME flag. Statistical inference is inherently limited by the single-unit treatment structure---a randomization-inference exercise yields a permutation $p$-value of 0.317, reflecting the low power of such tests when only one group switches---so the evidence for causality rests on the consistency of effects across four outcomes and the convergence of multiple estimators rather than on that permutation. Placebo tests, alternative clustering, wild cluster bootstrap, winsorization, Lee bounds, quantile DiD, synthetic control, and causal forest analysis provide further corroboration in the Appendix and Online Appendix.

The aggregate fiscal cost of the SME-only rule for Group~65 alone is R\$50--85 million over the 18-month window (R\$50 million in real terms, R\$85 million nominal), or 7--12\% of the group's procurement value. Two findings sharpen the policy implications. First, 92\% of that procurement value is concentrated in the top value quartile, so a simple reference-value exemption recovers 90\% of the fiscal cost while preserving the SME preference on 75\% of items by count. Because SMEs lose access to the top-quartile contracts under this reform, it is not strictly Pareto-improving, but it substantially relaxes the efficiency-equity trade-off that motivates SME-only rules. Second, CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531 within Group~65) bear 62\% larger distortion than the non-pharma medical supplies: the CMED list-price cap does not shield procurement from the ME/EPP preference, consistent with contract prices lying below the cap and the restriction compressing competition within the residual slack. Two Brazilian regulatory regimes---SME procurement preferences and drug price controls---therefore interact perversely, raising public-health spending above what either instrument alone would deliver.

The paper makes four contributions to the literatures on set-asides, bid preferences, and public procurement efficiency.

\noindent\textbf{(i) Direct bid-distribution evidence on the intensive margin.} Prior work on SME preferences has largely inferred the mechanism from changes in the winning price or the number of bidders~\citep{marion2007, athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, nakabayashi2013}. Using phase-2 bid moments---the minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation of the full bid distribution within each electronic auction---I show directly that surviving bidders submit more aggressive offers, with a tighter distribution, when the SME restriction does not bind. The finding qualifies as what~\citet{davis1971} called a \emph{False Correlation}: although entry and bid aggressiveness are theoretically linked, they are quantitatively independent---94\% of the set-aside price effect operates through aggressiveness holding entry fixed. This bid-level evidence complements the auction-theoretic prediction of \citet{bulow1996} and \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} and reconciles the coexistence of large price effects with modest entry effects that the literature has often struggled to explain.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) A sharp counterfactual policy-design result.} Because 92\% of Group-65 procurement value concentrates in the top quartile, a simple reference-value exemption---applying the SME-only rule only to items below approximately R\$7{,}600---recovers 90\% of the fiscal cost while preserving the SME preference on 75\% of items by count. This is a concrete, implementable lever for the ongoing implementation of Law~14{,}133/2021, Brazil's new public procurement framework, and more generally illustrates how administrative procurement data can be used to calibrate second-best reforms that preserve the equity goals of SME policies while curbing their fiscal footprint.

\noindent\textbf{(iii) A perverse interaction between two Brazilian price-regulation regimes.} CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531 within Group~65) bear 62\% larger price distortion under the SME-only rule than the non-pharma medical subsegment. The list-price cap imposed by CMED---designed to contain public-health expenditures---does not shield procurement from the ME/EPP preference, because actual contract prices sit below the cap and the restriction compresses competition within the residual slack. This interaction is new to the literature on procurement preferences~\citep{bosio2022, szucs2024, decarolis2024} and has direct implications for countries that combine SME preferences with pharmaceutical price controls.

\noindent\textbf{(iv) A credible fiscal-cost estimate from a large developing economy.} The paper delivers credible quasi-experimental estimates of SME set-aside costs in Sao Paulo (R\$50--85 million over 18 months for a single product group), rare in the literature outside of the United States and large firms in Europe~\citep{athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, bandiera2009, best2023}. The design---a single switching cohort against 76 never-treated controls---is a \emph{staggered-DiD reversal} rarely exploited in the applied literature~\citep{kim2019}, and the estimates are robust to the Callaway-Sant'Anna~(\citeyear{callaway2021}), Sun-Abraham~(\citeyear{sun2021}), and Goodman-Bacon~(\citeyear{goodmanbacon2021}) estimators, to a formal test of cross-group spillover, and to winner-level controls for firm size, age, and sector extracted from Brazil's matched employer-employee registry (RAIS).
