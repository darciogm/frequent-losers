\introheading

Public procurement is increasingly used as an instrument of social and economic policy. Beyond securing goods and services at the best price, governments deploy procurement to promote small firms, encourage local sourcing, advance environmental sustainability, and pursue distributional objectives. SME set-asides are the most widespread of these instruments: in the European Union, SMEs account for nearly 99.8\% of all registered firms and receive a large share of public contract value~\citep{pwc2014}; in Brazil, where approximately 97\% of firms are SMEs representing half of formal employment~\citep{bastos2018}, federal law mandates exclusive SME tenders for all items valued below R\$80{,}000~\citep{thai2017, bosio2022}. A growing body of evidence documents the benefits of these policies---firm growth~\citep{mel2008,freedman2013}, employment~\citep{szerman2023}, supplier network expansion~\citep{cardoza2016}---though effectiveness depends on management quality~\citep{mckenzie2015} and capital provision~\citep{fafchamps2014}. What is largely missing from this literature is the other side of the ledger: credible evidence on what these policies \emph{cost} the public sector. Without cost estimates, policymakers cannot evaluate whether the efficiency-equity trade-off justifies the instrument.

There are strong theoretical reasons to expect these costs to be large. \citet{bulow1996} show that, in a wide class of auction environments, attracting one additional bidder generates greater surplus for the auctioneer than any mechanism design refinement, implying that policies restricting participation impose a first-order efficiency loss. \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} formalize this in the procurement context, showing that bid preferences reduce both the number of bidders (extensive margin) and the aggressiveness of their bids (intensive margin). Empirically, \citet{marion2007} estimates a 3.8\% cost increase from small business preferences in California highway procurement, and \citet{athey2013} document similar trade-offs in US timber auctions. But outside the US, credible estimates of set-aside costs are rare, and in developing economies---where SME policies are most prevalent---they are essentially absent~\citep{nakabayashi2013, szucs2024, decarolis2024}.

This paper provides such estimates.

I exploit a quasi-experimental variation in Sao Paulo, Brazil, where the SME set-aside policy operates through a distinctive institutional mechanism: \emph{opt-out costs}. Public buyer units (PBUs) that wish to conduct open tenders rather than SME-only tenders must justify their choice through a costly formal process, subject to scrutiny by audit courts and potential sanctions. These opt-out costs penalize the efficient procurement choice, shifting the default from open competition to restricted tenders. I exploit the timing of a policy change (March 2018) that extended these opt-out costs to a previously exempt product group (Group 65, medical and hospital supplies), using time and cross-sectional variation to estimate the fiscal cost of this institutional design.

Between August 2014 and February 2018, the state of Sao Paulo exempted Group 65 from the mandatory SME-only tender requirement that applied to all other product groups, based on a joint agreement between the state government and the audit court that health items constituted strategic goods warranting open competition. In March 2018, a legal opinion from a different oversight agency reversed this interpretation, subjecting Group 65 to the same SME-favoring rules as all other groups. This policy change was driven by a legalistic reinterpretation of isonomy principles rather than by changes in procurement outcomes for Group 65, making it plausibly exogenous to the outcomes studied.

Methodologically, the March 2018 event creates a single-cohort staggered difference-in-differences~\citep{callaway2021,sun2021,goodmanbacon2021,borusyak2024}. Group 65 is the lone treated cohort; the 76 other product groups, already subject to the SME-only rule since the 2014 federal mandate, serve as never-treated controls within the sample window. This structure provides identification from a clean treated-vs-never-treated comparison without the ``forbidden'' implicit comparisons that motivate the modern staggered-DiD literature, a property I verify using the Goodman-Bacon decomposition. The Kim-Lee~(\citeyear{kim2019}) difference-in-differences-in-reverse framework formalizes the same estimand under the complementary view that the controls are ``always-treated''; both framings deliver the same coefficient and are numerically equivalent to the Sun-Abraham interaction-weighted estimator for a single-cohort design.

Between August 2014 and February 2018, the government's default was SME-only tenders for items valued at R\$80{,}000 or less, except for Group 65, which retained mandatory open tenders. PBUs could avoid restricted tenders for other groups by justifying the choice to audit courts, but this costly process---the opt-out costs of the SME-only regime---discourages open competition. From March 2018 onward, Group 65 became subject to these opt-out costs just as any other product group.

The estimated effect of imposing the SME-only rule on Group~65 is a 12--13\% increase in procurement prices (7--10\% in IPCA-deflated real terms), an 8--10\% reduction in participating firms, and a 16--17\% reduction in valid bids. Winning firms locate 5--11 km further from buyers when tenders are open---consistent with the view that SME-only rules succeed in promoting local sourcing even as they raise fiscal costs. To isolate the mechanism, I use the phase-2 electronic-bidding moments recorded in BEC: under open tenders the winning bid reaches approximately 10 percentage points deeper below the PBU's reference price, and the standard deviation of the bid distribution shrinks monotonically in firm count. These patterns persist after conditioning on 2, 3, or 5-plus participating firms---i.e., holding the extensive margin fixed---directly confirming the intensive-margin interpretation. A \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition corroborates the point: only 6\% of the price reduction is explained by extensive-margin channels (more firms, different winner composition); 94\% reflects bidding behavior within auctions of a given size.

Identification is supported by a battery of checks. Three alternative estimators from the staggered-DiD literature (CS2021, Sun-Abraham, Goodman-Bacon) converge on the headline magnitude, with 100\% of the Goodman-Bacon weight placed on the clean never-treated comparison. A formal spillover test restricted to the 76 control groups finds that the implied bias from non-SME firms redirecting into control markets is less than 1\% of the headline coefficient, sustaining the lower-bound interpretation of the estimates. Linking winners to RAIS (Brazil's matched employer-employee registry) shows the coefficient is invariant to firm-size, age, and two-digit CNAE sector controls; the shift in winner composition is also recoverable directly from a RAIS-validated SME flag. Placebo tests, alternative clustering, wild cluster bootstrap, winsorization, Lee bounds, quantile DiD, synthetic control, and causal forest analysis provide further corroboration in the Appendix and Online Appendix.

The aggregate fiscal cost of the SME-only rule for Group~65 alone is R\$50--85 million over the 18-month window (R\$50 million in real terms, R\$85 million nominal), or 7--12\% of the group's procurement value. Two findings sharpen the policy implications. First, 92\% of that procurement value is concentrated in the top value quartile, so a simple reference-value exemption recovers 90\% of the fiscal cost while preserving the SME preference on 75\% of items by count---an approximately Pareto-improving reform relative to the uniform rule. Second, CMED-regulated pharmaceuticals (class 6531 within Group~65) bear 62\% larger distortion than the non-pharma medical supplies: the CMED list-price cap does not shield procurement from the ME/EPP preference, because actual contract prices sit below the cap and the restriction compresses competition within that slack. Two Brazilian regulatory regimes---SME procurement preferences and drug price controls---therefore interact perversely, raising public-health spending above what either instrument alone would deliver.

This paper contributes to two strands of the literature. It adds to the evidence on the costs of restricting competition in public procurement~\citep{bosio2022, szucs2024, decarolis2024, bandiera2009, best2023}, providing credible estimates from a large developing economy where SME preferences are especially prevalent, and documenting a direct link between price-cap regulation and procurement distortions. It also contributes to the literature on set-asides and bid preferences~\citep{athey2013, krasnokutskaya2011, marion2007} by (i) using phase-2 bid-distribution moments to provide direct evidence on the intensive margin of aggressiveness and (ii) showing how a simple value-threshold exemption can recover most of the fiscal cost of a uniform SME rule without diluting its primary distributional objectives. More broadly, the counterfactual policy-design exercise illustrates how administrative procurement data can be used to calibrate second-best reforms that preserve the equity goals of SME policies while curbing their fiscal footprint.
