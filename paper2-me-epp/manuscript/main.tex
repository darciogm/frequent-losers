\title{\textbf{The Price of Protecting Small Firms: Evidence from Public Procurement\tnoteref{tfoot}}}
\tnotetext[tfoot]{\emph{This version: April 2026 (First version: January 2021)}}
\author[INSPER]{Darcio Genicolo-Martins} \ead{darciogm1@insper.edu.br} 
\address[INSPER]{INSPER}

\begin{abstract}
Governments worldwide restrict public tenders to small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), yet the fiscal cost of this policy is virtually unknown. I exploit a natural experiment in Sao Paulo, Brazil, where the policy operates through \emph{opt-out costs}: public buyers that wish to conduct open tenders must justify their choice to audit courts, effectively penalizing the efficient procurement decision. Until March 2018, medical supplies were uniquely exempt from these costs. When a legal reinterpretation---unrelated to procurement outcomes---ended this exemption, it created a clean difference-in-differences in reverse (DiDiR) design. Open tenders yielded prices 12--13\% lower and attracted 19\% more firms, but a Gelbach decomposition reveals that increased participation explains only 6\% of the price reduction---most of the savings come from fiercer competition among bidders. The fiscal cost for one product group alone amounts to R\$84.5 million (${\sim}$US\$17 million) over 18 months; extrapolating to all state procurement yields ${\sim}$R\$310 million. These costs are concentrated among standardized goods; for specialized items, restrictions appear less distortionary. The one clear benefit: SME-only tenders successfully promote local sourcing.
\end{abstract}

\end{frontmatter}
