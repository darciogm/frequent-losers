\section{Introduction}

Brazil's federal SME statute reserves low-value public procurement for micro and small firms through a default that the buying unit must justify opting out of, subject to audit-court scrutiny. The rule binds ninety-seven percent of Brazilian firms representing half of formal employment and, in the state of S\~ao Paulo alone, redirects billions of reais each year. Its welfare cost has been studied as a reduced-form markup on winning prices; the structure behind the markup has not. Three questions bypass the reduced-form statement. First, how much of the price effect runs through each bidder bidding less aggressively, and how much through a shift in who shows up? Second, because the set-aside restricts the pool rather than the rules, how should one quantify the allocative inefficiency of assigning the contract to a possibly higher-cost SME? Third, once the marginal cost of public funds is priced, how large is the welfare bill? This paper answers all three in a single structural framework.

The setting is Brazil's BEC platform in S\~ao Paulo, which intermediates all standardized-goods procurement for the state. In March 2018 a legal opinion of the state prosecutor reversed a four-year exemption and extended the SME-only rule to medical supplies (Group~65 in the BEC catalog). The reversal was driven by an isonomy argument internal to the prosecutor's office and not by any change in procurement outcomes, so the shift is plausibly exogenous to the firm-level cost distributions. The same quasi-experiment powered the reduced-form estimates in an earlier version of this paper, which documented a 10--11\% winning-price increase and left open the structural interpretation. I build that structure here.

The model follows \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}: bidders draw private costs from type-specific distributions and compete in a Preg\~ao, the Brazilian variant of the English-reverse descending-clock auction. Losers' drop-out bids point-identify the type-specific cost distributions $F_c^k$ under the standard English-reverse interpretation \citep{haile2003}. Auction-level unobserved heterogeneity---items of the same CADMAT class differ in scale in ways the reference price does not fully absorb---is separated out via a Krasnokutskaya-style shrinkage step, reported by strata and cross-validated against a Turnbull \citep{turnbull1976} nonparametric MLE that treats the winner as left-censored at the second-lowest loser bid. The policy change is mapped as a pool restriction in the \citet{atheyseira2011} sense: the SME-only rule truncates the entering bidder set, and the resulting Bayes-Nash equilibrium prices can be simulated under three counterfactuals that vary both composition and cost-distribution inputs.

The central decomposition is a standard one. Let $S_1$ be the observed open regime with both types competing, $S_2$ the SME-only counterfactual at the pre-period SME pool (intensive margin only, no entry response), and $S_3$ the SME-only counterfactual at the post-period pool (full policy effect with endogenous entry). Then the price effect $p_{S_3} - p_{S_1}$ decomposes into an intensive component $p_{S_2} - p_{S_1}$ and an entry component $p_{S_3} - p_{S_2}$. Both enter the estimation symmetrically; neither is forced to be the residual.

Three findings emerge. First, the intensive margin dominates: roughly seventy-five percent of the price effect is already present before endogenous entry clears, the remaining twenty-five comes from SMEs adjusting their participation. Point estimates place $p_{S_3} - p_{S_1}$ at 23\% of the reference price in non-pharma classes and 33\% in pharma; a cluster bootstrap at the auction level (B=500) puts the 95\% confidence intervals at [19\%, 29\%] and [25\%, 36\%]. A Turnbull NPMLE robustness check ratifies these numbers within five percent across both classes. Second, the welfare cost is about twice to four times earlier reduced-form estimates. With the marginal cost of public funds set at $\lambda = 0.30$---the Ballard-Shoven-Whalley benchmark value---the welfare loss runs to 28\% of procurement value in non-pharma (95\% CI 21--35\%) and 46\% in pharma (95\% CI 35--56\%). Roughly two-thirds of that loss is allocative---the extra production cost of routing contracts to bidders who are not the lowest-cost in the universal pool---and one-third is tax-distortion on the government's higher outlay. Third, a counterfactual 10\% price preference for SMEs---under which SME bids are discounted 10\% to select winners but paid at face value---achieves comparable SME win-rate gains at essentially zero price cost. The welfare weight on SME surplus required to prefer full set-asides over the 10\% preference rule is 2.4 to 3.5, implausibly large by Brazilian social-transfer standards where the implicit weight on cash transfers to the formal sector's lower tail is typically one-and-a-quarter to one-and-a-half.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it quantifies both margins of an SME set-aside in a single estimated model, closing a gap that dates to \citet{marion2007} and \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}. Most prior work on auction preferences infers the channel from winning-price variation alone, a design that conflates entry with bidding by construction. The structural approach here separates them directly. Second, it delivers a welfare decomposition that makes tax-distortion explicit---the reduced-form literature collapses the distortion into the transfer and tends to understate it. The 28--46\% range is large relative to prior estimates in the procurement literature and closer to the magnitudes reported in \citet{krasnokutskaya2011} for California highway contracts than to the 10--15\% benchmarks from state-level US procurement \citep{marion2007,athey2013}. Third, it documents a Pareto-improvement candidate: the 10\% price preference. The result is within-model and subject to the standard caveats of first-price versus second-price equivalence \citep{maskinriley2000} and single-market extrapolation, but the magnitude and direction are robust.
