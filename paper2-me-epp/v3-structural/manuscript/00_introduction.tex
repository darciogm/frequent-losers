\section{Introduction}

When a government reserves most of its low-value procurement for small firms and makes opting out of the reservation a scrutinized administrative act, what does that do to the price per contract, and what does it do to welfare? The reduced-form answer---somewhere between ten and fifteen percent on winning prices---has been in hand for a decade \citep{marion2007,krasnokutskaya2011,athey2013,nakabayashi2013}. The structural answer has not. Because a set-aside restricts the pool of admissible bidders rather than the rules they bid under, the price markup decomposes into a within-auction response (each remaining bidder bids less aggressively) and a between-auction response (different firms show up). A full welfare accounting further needs to separate the allocative waste of routing contracts to a non-minimum-cost winner from the tax distortion of financing the government's higher outlay. Neither split is available from price regressions alone. This paper builds the structure and delivers the splits.

The setting is S\~ao Paulo state's BEC platform, the centralized electronic reverse auction through which the state procures standardized goods. On March 1, 2018, a legal opinion from the state prosecutor's office (PGE-SP) reversed a four-year administrative exception and extended the SME-only regime to medical supplies (Group 65). The decision was internal to PGE-SP and defended on isonomy grounds; there is no evidence it responded to prices, competition, or supplier complaints within Group 65. An earlier version of this paper used the same cutoff to estimate, by difference-in-differences, that winning prices rose 10--11 percent on Group 65 items relative to 76 never-treated product groups. Those numbers are the reduced form. What they do not reveal---because no reduced form can---is whether the markup ran mostly through bidders or mostly through bidding.

To answer that, I specify an asymmetric independent-private-values model of the Preg\~ao format. Bidders draw costs from type-specific distributions $F_c^{\text{SME}}$ and $F_c^{\neg}$; they compete under a descending-clock English-reverse auction in which losers exit at their cost and the last remaining firm supplies the item. Losers' drop-out prices then point-identify $F_c^k$ under the standard \citet{haile2003} interpretation, and winners are treated as left-censored at the second-lowest exit via a Turnbull NPMLE that serves as a robustness check on the baseline all-bidders estimator. Two items within the same CADMAT class can differ in scale in ways the reference price does not fully absorb, so I separate out an auction-level unobserved heterogeneity component $a_t$ following \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}'s shrinkage procedure; the resulting cost primitives are UH-clean. Entry is handled in the \citet{atheyseira2011} spirit: observed pre- and post-period bidder counts per auction are taken as equilibrium arrival rates, and counterfactual prices are simulated under the restricted pool.

Three findings emerge.

\noindent\textbf{(i) The current rule is Pareto-inefficient.} A counterfactual 10 percent price preference---where SME bids are scored with a 10 percent discount for the purpose of selecting the winner, but the government pays the actual bid (not the discounted one)---delivers essentially zero price cost ($p - p_{S_1} = +1.5$ percent in non-pharma, $-0.4$ percent in pharma), while the SME win-rate gain is of the same order as under the full set-aside. The welfare weight on SME surplus required to rationalize the set-aside over the preference rule is 2.4--3.0; the implicit weight on Brazilian cash-transfer programs, by contrast, runs 1.2--1.5. Under any plausible distributional aggregator the current regime is welfare-dominated by an instrument the existing legal framework already permits.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) Welfare losses are two to four times the reduced-form markup.} Pricing allocative waste ($c_{(1)}^{S_3} - c_{(1)}^{S_1}$) and the marginal cost of public funds ($\lambda \cdot (p_{S_3} - p_{S_1})$, with $\lambda = 0.30$ after \citet{ballard1985}) delivers a welfare cost of 28 percent of procurement value in non-pharma (95\% CI 21--35) and 46 percent in pharma (95\% CI 35--56). The gap to the v1 figure of 11.8 percent has two mechanical sources: UH-clean cost distributions have heavier right tails than the losers-only ECDF that earlier work used, which amplifies the allocative wedge; and making tax distortion explicit adds seven to nine percentage points that reduced-form price regressions leave implicit in the transfer bucket.

\noindent\textbf{(iii) Within the total price effect, the intensive margin dominates.} Let $p_{S_1}$ denote the simulated winning price under the pre-period open regime, $p_{S_2}$ the SME-only counterfactual holding the SME pool at its pre-period size, and $p_{S_3}$ the SME-only equilibrium with endogenous entry. In non-pharma Group-65 items, $p_{S_3} - p_{S_1} = +0.24$ of the reference price, of which 76 percent operates through $p_{S_2} - p_{S_1}$ (each bidder bids less aggressively under the restricted pool) and 24 percent through $p_{S_3} - p_{S_2}$ (SMEs double their participation, partially but not fully offsetting the intensive shift). In pharma the total is $+0.31$ and the intensive share is 75 percent. A cluster bootstrap at the auction level places the 95 percent CIs at $[0.19, 0.29]$ and $[0.25, 0.36]$; a Turnbull NPMLE ratifies within five percent.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it identifies a policy instrument---the 10 percent price preference---that within the model Pareto-improves on the status-quo set-aside for any SME welfare weight below 2.4. This is the finding that carries through to policy. The magnitude depends on a Vickrey-equivalent framing that I discuss and qualify; the direction is robust to Turnbull corroboration, to bootstrap inference on the welfare ingredients, and to the sensitivity of the MCPF parameter. Second, it makes the welfare arithmetic complete by pricing allocative loss and tax distortion separately, delivering a total two to four times the reduced-form benchmark and tracing the departure to two methodological choices (UH-clean cost distributions and explicit MCPF) rather than to anything about the sample. Third, it delivers a within-auction structural decomposition of an SME preference in a developing-country procurement market, closing a gap that dates to \citet{marion2007} and has survived the subsequent auction-preferences literature largely because the price-only data used in that literature conflates entry with bidding by construction. The decomposition here rests on Preg\~ao drop-out identification (in the \citet{haile2003} tradition) and Convite first-price GPV inversion \citep{gpv2000} as a cross-modality consistency check, with auction-level heterogeneity corrected via \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}.

A practical example fixes the magnitudes. On February 6, 2018, one month before the policy change, a S\~ao Paulo public buyer procured fifty thousand units of a CMED-regulated generic (furosemide 40 mg, item code 110639) through an open Preg\~ao. Ten firms bid, the final price sat roughly twelve percent below the reference. Eight months into the post-period, a comparable purchase by the same buyer attracted three SMEs, and the winning price cleared just above reference. The gap is the 24 percentage points the model attributes on average to the pharma case; the intensive half of it shows up not as fewer firms but as each of the three doing something different than the ten had done. The welfare math in Section~\ref{sec:welfare} prices what that difference costs.
