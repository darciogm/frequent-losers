\section{Welfare}\label{sec:welfare}

The headline of this section is a number and a displacement from an earlier one. The welfare cost of the set-aside is 28.7 percent of procurement value in non-pharma and 45.5 percent in pharma---two to four times the 11.8 percent reduced-form estimate of the predecessor paper. The displacement has two mechanical sources that will be isolated below: the losers-only cost identification used in earlier work understates the allocative wedge, and the reduced form does not price tax distortion at all. The 28--46 percent range is the v3 number; it is robust to the bootstrap confidence interval, to the MCPF calibration, to the Turnbull NPMLE, and to the sample-filter grid reported in Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

The price effect is one piece of that welfare cost, and one of three. Two others matter and are routinely left out of reduced-form accountings. \emph{Allocative waste} arises because the set-aside routes the contract to an SME winner whose cost may exceed the lowest cost in the full pool; the extra production cost is lost to society, not a transfer. \emph{Tax-distortion loss} arises because the additional outlay that the government makes is financed from distortionary taxation, so each marginal real costs $(1+\lambda)$ in welfare shadow terms. The transfer from government to SMEs---$\Delta_{\text{gov}}$ minus the allocative wedge---is zero-sum across agents in a social-planner ledger and is not destruction. This section isolates the two destruction pieces.

Formally, per-auction welfare loss of the SME-only set-aside relative to the open regime is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:welfare}
\mathrm{Loss} \;=\; \underbrace{c_{(1)}^{S_3} - c_{(1)}^{S_1}}_{\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \cdot (p_{S_3} - p_{S_1})}_{\mathrm{MCPF\ distortion}}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the allocative wedge. It measures by how much the winner's production cost is higher in the restricted regime than in the open regime---the resource loss from assigning the contract to a non-minimum-cost supplier. The second term is the tax-distortion loss on the government's extra outlay $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = p_{S_3} - p_{S_1}$, scaled by the marginal cost of public funds $\lambda$. The natural normalization is the share of procurement value the loss represents, $\mathrm{Loss} / p_{S_1}$, and I report it that way.

\subsection{Point estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:v3_welfare} gives the decomposition at $\lambda = 0.30$, the \citet{ballard1985} benchmark value. In non-pharma, $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = 0.242$ of the reference price, of which $\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}} = 0.150$ is allocative waste and the remaining $0.091$ is an implicit transfer to SME bidders. MCPF distortion adds $0.30 \times 0.242 = 0.073$. The total welfare loss is $\mathbf{0.223}$, or \textbf{28.7 percent of $p_{S_1}$}.

In pharma the corresponding numbers are larger. $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = 0.308$; $\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}} = 0.206$; implicit transfer $0.102$; MCPF distortion $0.092$; total welfare loss $\mathbf{0.298}$, or \textbf{45.5 percent of $p_{S_1}$}. Allocative waste alone is two-thirds of the total; MCPF distortion is about one-third. In both classes, the implicit transfer captures roughly 30--35 percent of the government's extra outlay; the rest is destruction.

\subsection{Confidence intervals}

Cluster bootstrap with B=500 replicates (Table~\ref{tab:v3_welfare_ci}) delivers 95 percent CIs on $\mathrm{Loss}/p_{S_1}$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Class      & Point  & 95\% CI           \\
\midrule
Non-pharma & 28.7\% & $[21.3\%, 35.3\%]$ \\
Pharma     & 45.5\% & $[35.1\%, 56.2\%]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The 14 and 21 percentage-point CI widths are substantive but leave no ambiguity about where the estimates sit relative to the reduced-form predecessor's 11.8 percent. The 95 percent lower bound in pharma is 35.1---still three times the v2 figure. The two-to-four-times departure is a structural feature of the estimation, not an artifact of the confidence set.

\subsection{Sensitivity to $\lambda$}

For $\lambda \in \{0.20, 0.30, 0.40\}$---the range that brackets the benchmark 0.30 value of \citet{ballard1985} and its subsequent refinements for emerging economies---the welfare loss in non-pharma runs 25.6, 28.7, and 31.8 percent. In pharma, the same grid gives 40.8, 45.5, and 50.2 percent. Each 0.10 increment in $\lambda$ adds roughly 3 percentage points in non-pharma and 5 in pharma. The qualitative result is robust across the range.

\subsection{Why v3 is two-to-four times v2}

Two mechanical features drive the departure from the v2 figure of 11.8 percent.

First, the UH-clean cost distribution has a heavier right tail than the losers-only ECDF that v2 used. Losers-only systematically excludes the lowest draw per auction---the winner's cost---so the tail it does observe is compressed in the left and thin at the right. The UH-clean all-bidders ECDF includes a winner proxy $c_{(2)}$ and, after the shrinkage, a right tail that reflects the full support of the bidder-specific primitive. $\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}}$ is sensitive to that tail because the set-aside forces the winner to come from the SME distribution only, and the SME tail is correspondingly more influential.

Second, v2 did not price tax distortion. $\lambda \cdot \Delta_{\text{gov}}$ was implicit in the ``transfer'' bucket. The v3 decomposition separates it out and prices it at $\lambda = 0.30$; this alone adds seven percentage points to non-pharma and nine to pharma.

The v3 numbers align more closely with \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}'s California-highway welfare estimates (20--30 percent cost of a 10 percent preference rule) than with the smaller US state-procurement figures in \citet{marion2007} and \citet{athey2013}. The difference between the US and Brazilian magnitudes plausibly reflects that the Brazilian rule is a full set-aside---truncating the pool entirely---rather than a preference, and full truncation generates a larger allocative wedge regardless of how $\lambda$ is priced.

\subsection{Scaling to the furosemida vignette}

The pair of purchases introduced in Section~\ref{sec:data}---fifty thousand units of furosemida 40\,mg, CADMAT class 6531, one month before and eight months after the cutoff---makes the magnitudes tangible. Applying the pharma-class point estimates to that single purchase: relative to what the unit would have cost under the pre-period open regime, the government pays on average $+0.31$ of the reference price more under the set-aside---in this purchase, a $+15{,}500 \, p^{\mathrm{ref}}$ larger outlay across the fifty thousand units. Of that amount, roughly $10{,}500 \, p^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is allocative waste---the extra production cost of routing the contract to an SME winner whose cost is higher than the lowest cost in the unrestricted pool---and roughly $4{,}500 \, p^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is tax distortion at $\lambda = 0.30$. The remaining $4{,}500 \, p^{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a transfer from the government to the SME winner. At the pharma-class-median reference price of R\$2.96, the allocative waste alone is roughly R\$31{,}000 on this single purchase. A purchase of this exact type takes place several times a year on BEC; Group~65 as a whole comprises hundreds of thousands of such items. The per-auction number scales; the fiscal consequence at the group level is substantial, but the welfare consequence is larger still.
