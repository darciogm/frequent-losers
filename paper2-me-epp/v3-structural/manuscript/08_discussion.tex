\section{Discussion: Policy Design}\label{sec:discussion}

The model delivers a quantifiable trade-off between two instruments that a procurement authority can deploy to protect small firms: a full set-aside (V0) and a price preference (V3). The two are not typically juxtaposed in the empirical literature; the US research has focused on the price-preference side \citep{marion2007,krasnokutskaya2011,athey2013}, while the Brazilian literature has focused on the set-aside side \citep{cabral2017,szerman2023}. My structural framework can compare them within a single market.

\subsection{Welfare weight implicit in the current regime}

Let $w^{\text{SME}}$ denote the relative social weight on surplus accruing to SMEs. The government is indifferent between V0 (full set-aside) and V3 (10\% preference) when the expected welfare difference equals the expected SME-surplus difference times $w^{\text{SME}}$:
\begin{equation}
  w^{\text{SME}}_\star = \frac{\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3)}
                                {\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3)}.
\end{equation}
With my estimates, $\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3) \approx 0.22$ (non-pharma) or $0.30$ (pharma) in reference units, and $\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3) \approx 0.09$ or $0.10$ respectively. The implied indifference weight is \textbf{$w^{\text{SME}}_\star \approx 2.4$ in non-pharma and $3.0$ in pharma}. For the current regime to be welfare-optimal, the government would need to value R\$1 of SME surplus at R\$2.40 to R\$3.00 of general welfare. Brazilian cash-transfer programs (Bolsa Fam\'ilia, Aux\'ilio Brasil) are typically justified with implicit weights in the 1.2 to 1.5 range; the set-aside preference rule, in contrast, would require weights roughly twice as high. I read this as prima facie evidence that the full set-aside is a suboptimal instrument relative to the price preference under any plausible welfare aggregator.

\subsection{Implementation margins}

Two caveats qualify the headline. First, the 10\% price preference is a Vickrey-equivalent calculation; a first-price sealed-bid preference would involve strategic bidding adjustments that my model does not solve for \citep{krasnokutskaya2011,maskinriley2000}. The direction of the bias would typically be smaller realized price gains (bidders internalize the preference), reinforcing rather than weakening the Pareto-improvement claim. Second, a preference rule is more complex to implement than a set-aside and may be more susceptible to disputes at the audit-court level. In practice, a 10\% preference rule (or the \citet{denes1997} subcontracting-mandate variant) has been implemented in multiple US states and the federal SBA framework without significant administrative friction. The transplant to Brazilian state procurement is not unprecedented---the federal procurement law (Law 14{,}133/2021) already contemplates price-preference mechanisms for some goods.

\subsection{Non-pharma vs.\ pharma}

The welfare cost of the set-aside is 60\% larger in pharma than in non-pharma (46\% vs.\ 28\% of procurement value). Two forces contribute. First, pharma has heavier unobserved heterogeneity (intraclass correlation 0.62 vs.\ 0.47), so the UH-clean $F_c$ has a heavier right tail once the shrinkage is applied; this amplifies the allocative wedge. Second, pharma SMEs Post have a more dispersed cost distribution than their pre-policy counterparts---the KS test $D = 0.072$ in pharma Preg\~ao rejects invariance, consistent with the new entrants being a selected tail of stragglers. The SME-only rule in pharma is therefore disproportionately costly and the case for reform disproportionately strong.

A possible objection is that the BEC pharma sample is contaminated by the CMED price regulation (price-regulated drugs): CMED-regulated items sit under a binding ceiling, so the set-aside's price effect might be compressed against the cap. Section~\ref{sec:results} addresses this indirectly: the pharma-narrow classes (CMED-regulated) show a $p_{S_1}$ of 0.66 of reference, well below the cap, and a Post-period price of 1.00---still under the cap. The set-aside raises prices within the residual slack below the CMED ceiling, consistent with the regulation binding but not fully muting the effect. This is the ``regulatory interaction'' that prior v1 analysis flagged at the reduced-form level; the structural version here makes it welfare-quantitative.

\subsection{Extensions not attempted}

The model abstracts from two features that matter for the full welfare accounting. First, procurement quantity $Q$ is treated as inelastic to price; in practice, demand may adjust if prices rise and the budget constraint tightens. Second, the set-aside's dynamic effects---whether protected SMEs accumulate capacity, scale up, or exit---are outside the static equilibrium framework here \citep{szerman2023,cabral2017}. Both extensions would typically strengthen the case against the full set-aside (quantity adjustment introduces deadweight loss beyond allocative and MCPF channels; dynamic SME capacity effects are empirically small in the Brazilian literature), but the magnitudes would require a dedicated follow-up paper.
