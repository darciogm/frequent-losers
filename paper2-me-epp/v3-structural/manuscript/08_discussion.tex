\section{Discussion: Policy Design}\label{sec:discussion}

The model puts a number on a trade-off that procurement authorities in Brazil are already making implicitly. Law 14{,}133/2021---the federal procurement framework adopted in 2021 and now being rolled out across the 5{,}570 Brazilian municipalities---contemplates both full SME set-asides and price preferences as tools for small-firm protection. The choice between the two instruments is currently decided at the administrative level, with the set-aside being the default for low-value procurement and the preference left to discretionary use. The estimates here indicate that the defaults should invert.

\subsection{The welfare weight implicit in the current regime}

Consider a social planner aggregating producer and consumer surplus with a weight $w^{\text{SME}}$ on SME producer surplus. The planner is indifferent between the full set-aside (V0) and the 10 percent price preference (V3) when the welfare-loss differential equals the SME-surplus differential, times the weight:
\begin{equation}
w^{\text{SME}}_\star
\;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3)}
           {\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3)}.
\end{equation}
With the point estimates of Section~\ref{sec:welfare} and the SME-surplus implicit in the V0--V3 comparison ($\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3) \approx 0.22$ in non-pharma and $0.30$ in pharma; $\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3) \approx 0.09$ and $0.10$), the indifference weight is
\[
w^{\text{SME}}_\star \approx 2.4 \text{ (non-pharma)}, \qquad 3.0 \text{ (pharma)}.
\]
For the full set-aside to be welfare-optimal under these estimates, the planner would have to value one real of SME surplus at between 2.4 and 3.0 reais of general welfare.

This is a large number. Brazilian cash-transfer programs (Bolsa Fam\'{\i}lia and Aux\'{\i}lio Brasil) have been defended under implicit weights in the 1.2--1.5 range---the shadow price of a real transferred to the lower tail of the income distribution, relative to a real in general tax revenue. The SMEs receiving the set-aside benefit are medium-sized formal-sector firms, not individuals in the lower tail; the standard welfare argument for concentrating fiscal resources on them is weaker than for cash transfers, not stronger. A weight of 2.4--3.0 sits above the weight implicit in Brazilian social policy for the population groups with the most established claim to such support. The set-aside is, on these numbers, Pareto-inefficient for any plausible welfare aggregator. I read the gap from 2.4--3.0 to the 1.2--1.5 benchmark as prima facie evidence that Brazil's procurement-protection regime is under-delivering relative to the 10 percent price preference.

\subsection{Implementation considerations}

Two features qualify the main result. First, V3 is computed in a Vickrey-equivalent framework. In a first-price sealed-bid auction with the same preference rule, bidder equilibrium behavior would adjust---SMEs would bid more aggressively anticipating the preference, non-SMEs would bid less aggressively anticipating the lost share---in ways characterized by \citet{maskinriley2000}. The adjustment reduces the realized gain to SMEs relative to the Vickrey benchmark, and increases the realized welfare saving to the government. The direction strengthens the Pareto-improvement claim; the magnitude depends on the strategic adjustment, which this paper does not solve.

Second, a preference rule is procedurally more complex to implement than a set-aside. The buyer must score bids twice (actual, and actual scaled by $1 - 0.10$ for SMEs), and the winner-selection rule must be disclosed and contestable. In the US and in the SBA framework the preference rule has been operational since the 1970s without significant administrative friction. The same is true for the \citet{denes1997} subcontracting mandate, a variant that achieves similar SME protection via a different mechanism. Brazilian state procurement has already experimented with preference rules in several narrow product categories---the scale-up to Group-65 medical supplies, and beyond, is a procedural rather than a conceptual move.

\subsection{The entry response is the government's partial insurance}

A subsidiary finding, visible only once entry is endogenized, is that the SME entry response is doing heavy lifting for the public purse. V2 in Table~\ref{tab:v3_apv}---the counterfactual that imposes the full set-aside but holds the SME pool at its pre-period size---delivers a price effect 162 percent of V0's in non-pharma and 170 percent in pharma. That is, if SMEs had not doubled their participation rate after the policy, the price rise would have been 60--70 percent larger than what is actually observed.

The policy is therefore self-correcting at the extensive margin, but only partially. SMEs draw in because the protected segment makes entry profitable, and their entry depresses the second-order statistic below what a fixed-pool counterfactual would deliver. Three readings follow. First, welfare cost estimates that freeze the SME pool at pre-policy levels---the standard reduced-form device in the cross-sectional literature---systematically overstate the policy's realized cost. The v1 reduced-form estimate of 11.8 percent DWL partly reflected this bias on top of the UH-clean and MCPF biases already discussed in Section~\ref{sec:welfare}. Second, the entry response is itself a policy lever: rules that make SME entry cheaper (subsidized registration, capacity-building programs) would deepen the pool, shift $p_{S_3}$ down further, and narrow the gap between V0 and V3 without changing the set-aside itself. Third, the entry margin is slow---the window-sensitivity analysis in Section~\ref{sec:robustness} shows intensive share dropping from 77 percent at 6 months to 68 percent at 18 months, meaning the extensive response takes roughly a year to equilibrate. A short-horizon welfare evaluation would miss the insurance and overstate the cost; the 18-month baseline here is the steady-state number.

The reading is that the set-aside is less costly than it first appears, but only because the market is doing work the rule itself does not contemplate. A well-designed preference instrument---V3---would capture the same SME-protection value without requiring the market to absorb 60 percent of a larger price shock in order to neutralize it.

\subsection{Non-pharma versus pharma}

The welfare cost of the set-aside is 60 percent larger in pharma than in non-pharma---46 percent of procurement value versus 28 percent. Two structural forces contribute, and both bear on the question of what kind of good is most poorly served by a full set-aside.

First, auction-level heterogeneity is larger in pharma (intraclass correlation 0.62 versus 0.47). The CADMAT pharma classes host items of very different scale and registration burden---a box of twenty tablets of a generic antihypertensive and a cold-chain vial of a brand-name biologic sit in the same broader class. The reference-price normalization absorbs the predictable scale, but not the item-by-item heterogeneity in ANVISA compliance cost, storage specification, or expiration management. Once the rule forces the winner to come from the SME distribution alone, the pharma winner's expected production cost is higher because the SME right tail---truncated from above by the set-aside's requirement---draws from a distribution with a heavier upper reach.

Second, the SME pool in pharma is thinner in absolute terms ($N^{\text{SME}}_{\text{Pre}} = 0.44$ versus $0.86$ in non-pharma). The equilibrium price under SME-only, $p_{S_2}$, is pulled further from the open-regime $p_{S_1}$ when the SME pool is thin, because the second-order statistic is drawn from a thinner underlying distribution. This is an intensive-margin effect amplified by baseline thinness.

Both forces identify a property of the good, not of the buyer: pharma is costly to set aside because pharma has heterogeneous quality primitives and a thin SME supply base. By implication, the case for relaxation scales \emph{with the heterogeneity of the good}, not with the level of procurement value alone. High-heterogeneity goods---pharmaceuticals, specialized medical equipment, engineering services---are systematically worse candidates for full set-asides than homogeneous low-value goods (office supplies, cleaning products, basic stationery). The structural framework here makes that argument quantitatively: the welfare cost is a direct function of $\rho^k$, not a uniform feature of the rule itself.

Two Brazilian regulatory regimes (the CMED price ceiling and the SME set-aside) interact to produce the pharma magnitudes. The set-aside raises prices within the residual slack below the CMED cap, and the allocative cost is larger than it would be under open competition at the cap. The price ceiling does not shield the welfare account from the set-aside; the two policies compound perversely.

\subsection{Extensions the paper does not attempt}

The model treats procurement quantity $Q$ as inelastic and the set-aside as static. A full welfare account would price two more margins: the quantity response of the buyer to higher prices (typical in procurement with a fixed budget), and the dynamic capacity-accumulation effects for SMEs protected by the rule \citep{szerman2023,cabral2017}. Both extensions would typically strengthen the case against the full set-aside---quantity adjustment introduces additional deadweight beyond the static channels, and the dynamic SME capacity effects documented in Brazilian data are empirically small and short-lived. A full dynamic-general-equilibrium treatment of the SME-only rule is a paper of its own.
