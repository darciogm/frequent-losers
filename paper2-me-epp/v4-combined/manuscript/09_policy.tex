\section{A Pareto-Improvement Counterfactual}\label{sec:pareto}

The 10--11 percent reduced-form price rise and the 29--45 percent welfare loss raise an obvious question: is there a cheaper rule that achieves the same SME-protection objective? Section~\ref{sec:welfare} supplied the answer arithmetically; this section develops it structurally.

A 10 percent price preference---of the kind the United States Small Business Administration has operated since the 1970s and that \citet{denes1997} documents in historical US federal procurement---works by scoring SME bids at a discount for purposes of winner selection while having the government pay the actual bid. Non-SMEs remain admissible and keep competing; SMEs get protection only to the extent that their bids fall within 10 percent of the non-SME minimum. Within the model's Bayes-Nash equilibrium, I simulate the preference rule on the same UH-clean cost distributions that anchor the set-aside decomposition. The result:
\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{V}_3 - \mathrm{V}_1 \;=\; +0.015 \text{ (non-pharma)}, \qquad -0.004 \text{ (pharma)},
\end{equation*}
versus $\mathrm{V}_0 - \mathrm{V}_1 = +0.245$ and $+0.308$ respectively under the full set-aside. The price cost to the government is essentially zero. The SME win-rate gain is of the same order as under the full set-aside---SMEs are selected as the preferred bidder in a comparable share of auctions---but the payment rule does not pull the equilibrium price up with them. Table~\ref{tab:v3_apv} reports the full comparison across four policy variants; Figure~\ref{fig:v3_welfare_weight} summarizes the welfare-weight implications.

\subsection{Welfare-weight identity}

The comparison formalizes through a standard welfare-weight identity. Let $w^{\text{SME}}$ denote the social weight on SME producer surplus. A planner is indifferent between V0 (full set-aside) and V3 (price preference) when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:welfare_weight}
w^{\text{SME}}_\star \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3)}{\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3)}.
\end{equation}
With the point estimates of Section~\ref{sec:welfare} and the SME-surplus differentials implicit in V0--V3 ($\mathrm{Loss}(V_0) - \mathrm{Loss}(V_3) \approx 0.22$ in non-pharma and $0.30$ in pharma; $\mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_0) - \mathrm{SMESurplus}(V_3) \approx 0.09$ and $0.10$), the indifference weight is
\[
w^{\text{SME}}_\star \;\approx\; 2.4 \text{ (non-pharma)}, \qquad 3.0 \text{ (pharma)}.
\]
For the full set-aside to be welfare-optimal, the planner would need to value one real of SME producer surplus at 2.4 to 3.0 reais of general welfare. Brazilian cash-transfer programs (Bolsa Fam\'{\i}lia, Aux\'{\i}lio Brasil) have been defended under implicit weights in the 1.2--1.5 range---the shadow price of a real transferred to households in the lower tail of the income distribution, relative to a real in general tax revenue. The SMEs receiving set-aside benefits are medium-sized formal-sector firms, not households in the lower tail; the standard welfare argument for concentrating fiscal resources on them is \emph{weaker} than for cash transfers, not stronger. The implicit weight required to rationalize the current rule over the preference thus sits well above the weight Brazilian social policy places on population groups with the most established claim to such support. On these numbers the set-aside is welfare-inferior to the preference rule under any plausible distributional aggregator.

\subsection{Two implementation caveats}

The V3 counterfactual is Vickrey-equivalent. In a first-price sealed-bid auction with the same preference rule, equilibrium bidding would adjust in ways \citet{maskinriley2000} characterize: SMEs bidding more aggressively anticipating the preference, non-SMEs less aggressively anticipating the lost share. The direction of the adjustment typically reduces SME rents and increases the government's realized savings, so the Pareto-improvement claim carries through in sign even though the exact surplus split depends on the strategic response the paper does not solve. I flag this caveat explicitly; the preference-rule finding is directional rather than pinned to the Vickrey magnitude.

A preference rule is also procedurally more complex than a set-aside: the buyer must score bids twice and disclose the winner-selection rule. In the US SBA framework the rule has been operational for decades without significant friction. Brazilian procurement law already contemplates price preferences for several categories. The scale-up to Group-65 medical supplies would be a procedural rather than a conceptual move.

\subsection{When does the ranking become sensitive?}

Table~\ref{tab:v3_strict_invariance} in the appendix reports the strict-primitive-invariance benchmark, in which the post-period SME cost distribution is replaced by its pre-period counterpart (rather than being treated as an equilibrium-selection object). In non-pharma the preference rule continues to dominate. In pharma the implied welfare weight falls to 0.7 and the ranking reverses: under this stricter reading, a utilitarian planner ($w=1$) would prefer the set-aside to the preference. I read the bifurcation as informative about the mechanism rather than as fragility. In thin, heterogeneous markets the identity of the induced entrants is first-order for equilibrium price formation; changing the assumption about who the Post-period SME is changes the conclusion. In thick, standardized markets the same assumption is numerically immaterial.

The policy implication is therefore conditional rather than universal. For commonplace goods with thicker SME pools, preference rules Pareto-dominate set-asides. For regulated goods with thinner protected supply and more heterogeneity, the comparison requires additional modeling of the entrant pool. The paper's contribution is to locate where the policy question becomes structural and to show that, where it does, it is the entrant-mix question specifically that drives the sensitivity.
