\section{Welfare}\label{sec:welfare}

The welfare consequences of the set-aside are class-heterogeneous from the start. Under the main equilibrium-selection specification, the welfare cost is 28.7 percent of procurement value in non-pharma and 45.5 percent in pharma at $\lambda = 0.30$. The price effect is therefore only the beginning of the welfare account. What matters for policy is how bidder exclusion interacts with allocative waste, tax distortion, market thickness, and product heterogeneity. The same structure that delivered the decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:results} also explains why the welfare comparison is stable in one class and model-sensitive in the other.

The price effect is one piece of that welfare cost, and one of three. Two others matter and are routinely left out of reduced-form accountings. \emph{Allocative waste} arises because the set-aside routes the contract to an SME winner whose cost may exceed the lowest cost in the full pool; the extra production cost is lost to society, not a transfer. \emph{Tax-distortion loss} arises because the additional outlay that the government makes is financed from distortionary taxation, so each marginal real costs $(1+\lambda)$ in welfare shadow terms. The transfer from government to SMEs---$\Delta_{\text{gov}}$ minus the allocative wedge---is zero-sum in a social-planner ledger and is not destruction. This section isolates the two destruction pieces.

Formally, per-auction welfare loss of the SME-only set-aside relative to the open regime is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:welfare}
\mathrm{Loss} \;=\; \underbrace{c_{(1)}^{S_3} - c_{(1)}^{S_1}}_{\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\lambda \cdot (p_{S_3} - p_{S_1})}_{\mathrm{MCPF\ distortion}}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the allocative wedge. It measures by how much the winner's production cost is higher in the restricted regime than in the open regime. The second term is the tax-distortion loss on the government's extra outlay $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = p_{S_3} - p_{S_1}$, scaled by the marginal cost of public funds $\lambda$. The natural normalization is $\mathrm{Loss} / p_{S_1}$, and I report it that way.

\subsection{Point estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:v3_welfare} gives the decomposition at $\lambda = 0.30$, the \citet{ballard1985} benchmark. In non-pharma, $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = 0.242$ of the reference price, of which $\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}} = 0.150$ is allocative waste and the remaining $0.092$ is an implicit transfer to SME bidders. MCPF distortion adds $0.30 \times 0.242 = 0.072$. Total welfare loss is $0.223$, or 28.7 percent of $p_{S_1}$.

In pharma, the same accounting is larger on every margin. $\Delta_{\text{gov}} = 0.308$; $\mathrm{DWL}_{\text{alloc}} = 0.205$; implicit transfer $0.103$; MCPF distortion $0.092$; total welfare loss $0.298$, or 45.5 percent of $p_{S_1}$. The allocative component dominates in both classes, but especially in pharma, where a thin SME pool and higher goods heterogeneity make bidder exclusion more likely to route the contract to a high-cost protected winner.

\subsection{Entry as partial insurance in welfare units}

Section~\ref{sec:results} showed that the observed SME entry response is the policy's partial insurance on the price side. The same logic carries over to welfare. Table~\ref{tab:v3_apv} reports that the no-entry counterfactual V2 is 161.7 percent of the realized V0 price effect in non-pharma and 169.7 percent in pharma. Because both terms in equation~\eqref{eq:welfare} load on that price margin, a fixed-pool welfare calculation would be about 60--70 percent larger than the realized one. Endogenous entry does not overturn the ranking, but it materially reduces the fiscal and welfare burden of bidder exclusion.

This is the correct way to reconcile the two findings. The intensive margin remains the dominant channel. Entry matters because it softens the intensive shock ex post, not because it is the main mechanism through which the policy works. The market does part of the adjustment for the government, but only after the rule has created a potentially large distortion.

\subsection{Sensitivity to \texorpdfstring{$\lambda$}{lambda}}

The MCPF parameter $\lambda$ is the analytical currency of public-finance welfare analysis, and its calibration is the one free parameter the headline estimates depend on. The \citet{ballard1985} benchmark of $\lambda = 0.30$ was derived on 1970s U.S.\ tax data; the consensus range for modern economies is approximately $\lambda \in [0.15, 0.45]$. Table~\ref{tab:v3_welfare} reports the decomposition across this full interval. In non-pharma, the welfare cost runs 24.0, 25.6, 28.7, 31.8, and 33.3 percent of $p_{S_1}$ at $\lambda \in \{0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.45\}$; in pharma, the same grid gives 38.4, 40.8, 45.5, 50.2, and 52.5 percent. Each 0.10 increment in $\lambda$ adds about 3 percentage points in non-pharma and about 5 in pharma.

Two features of the sensitivity matter for the policy reading. First, even at the lower bound $\lambda = 0.15$, the welfare cost of the set-aside is 24 percent in non-pharma and 38 percent in pharma---more than double the reduced-form price effect. The qualitative ranking against the 10 percent price preference counterfactual of Section~\ref{sec:pareto} is therefore robust under any plausible MCPF calibration, not just the benchmark $\lambda = 0.30$. Second, the Brazilian tax base weights indirect and payroll taxation more heavily than the U.S.\ base on which Ballard-Shoven-Whalley calibrated; if that translates into a higher Brazilian $\lambda$, as several Brazilian public-finance studies have suggested, the estimates in the upper half of the grid are the more policy-relevant ones. The class ranking does not move across the entire sensitivity range: pharma welfare cost is strictly larger than non-pharma at every $\lambda$.

\subsection{Why the structural number exceeds a fiscal-cost calibration}

Two mechanical features separate the structural welfare cost from a naive fiscal-cost calibration. First, the UH-clean cost distribution has a heavier right tail than a losers-only ECDF would imply. Losers-only systematically excludes the lowest draw per auction, compressing the support in precisely the place where the allocative wedge is most sensitive. Second, a purely fiscal calibration---reduced-form price coefficient times pre-period procurement value---does not price tax distortion. Equation~\eqref{eq:welfare} makes that term explicit and adds about seven percentage points in non-pharma and nine in pharma at $\lambda = 0.30$.

The strict-invariance benchmark delivers 30.0 percent in non-pharma and 39.2 percent in pharma (Table~\ref{tab:v3_strict_invariance}). The pharma decline relative to the main specification is exactly the footprint of equilibrium selection: when the protected regime is allowed to draw in a different SME pool, the welfare burden rises because the selected entrant mix is part of the policy response. That difference is substantive evidence on mechanism, not a sign that the decomposition itself is fragile.

\subsection{Distributional incidence of the SME transfer}

The welfare loss above sums two destruction terms (DWL$_{\text{alloc}}$ and MCPF distortion) and excludes the implicit transfer from the government to SME bidders. That transfer is not destruction in a planner's ledger, but \emph{who} receives it is itself a public-finance question: the political economy of the set-aside hinges on whether the protected segment is broadly distributed across the SME population or is concentrated in a small group of suppliers. Table~\ref{tab:v3_incidence} reports the realized incidence of the rule's post-policy transfer across SME suppliers in Group~65.

Three patterns emerge. First, the transfer is highly concentrated: the Gini coefficient across the 488 distinct SME firms that won at least one Group-65 contract in the 18-month post-policy window is 0.78---comparable to Brazilian wage inequality. The top 1 percent of beneficiaries (5 firms) capture 19.3 percent of the realized SME revenue; the top 10 percent capture 67.5 percent; and the top 25 percent capture 86.4 percent. Mean revenue per SME winner is R\$221{,}000 over the window; the median is R\$47{,}000---a five-fold gap that is itself a measure of the right-skewed distribution. Second, the transfer accrues primarily to micro-employer firms: 72 percent of the realized revenue goes to firms with 0--4 formal employees in the RAIS registry, a further 25 percent to firms with 5--19 employees, and only about 3 percent to firms with 20 or more employees. The set-aside is therefore reaching small SMEs in headcount terms, but its largest beneficiaries within that population are themselves a thin layer of relatively higher-revenue micro firms. Third, the transfer is geographically local: 82 percent of the SME revenue goes to firms based in S\~ao Paulo state, with 60 percent located in SP-interior municipalities outside the capital. The rule does deliver geographic proximity in a way the set-aside's stated objectives anticipate, but the proximity is concentrated in interior firms rather than in the state's least-developed areas.

The incidence pattern affects the welfare-weight calibration of Section~\ref{sec:pareto}. If a planner valued one real of SME producer surplus at the same shadow price across all SMEs, the high concentration of the transfer would not affect the welfare ranking; but if the planner placed greater weight on broadly-distributed gains than on concentrated ones, the implicit weight required to prefer the set-aside over the price preference would rise further still. The transfer is concentrated; the welfare cost is destruction.

\subsection{Scaling to the furosemida vignette}

The pair of purchases introduced in Section~\ref{sec:data}---fifty thousand units of furosemida 40\,mg, CADMAT class 6531, one month before and eight months after the cutoff---makes the magnitudes tangible. Applying the pharma-class point estimates to that single purchase: relative to what the unit would have cost under the pre-period open regime, the government pays on average $+0.31$ of the reference price more under the set-aside. Of that amount, roughly two thirds is allocative waste and roughly one third is tax distortion at $\lambda = 0.30$. The remaining piece is a transfer from the government to the SME winner. The per-auction number scales; the group-level fiscal consequence is substantial, and the welfare consequence is larger still.
