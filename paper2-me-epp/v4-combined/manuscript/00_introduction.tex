\section{Introduction}

On February 6, 2018, a S\~ao Paulo public buyer procured fifty thousand units of furosemide 40\,mg---a generic antihypertensive, item 110639 in the BEC catalog---through an open electronic reverse auction. Ten firms bid, and the clearing price settled roughly twelve percent below the buyer's reference. Eight months later, after a legal opinion of the state prosecutor had reclassified medical supplies as subject to the state's SME-only procurement rule, a comparable purchase by the same buyer attracted three small firms, and the winning unit price cleared just above the reference. The price gap between the two purchases is about fourteen percent of the reference price. It is neither an outlier nor a coincidence; it is the average direction of more than a hundred thousand pharma item-transactions over a matched eighteen-month window. This paper quantifies that gap, decomposes it, and asks whether the rule that produced it is worth its cost.

Set-asides for small and medium-sized enterprises are the most widely deployed procurement-preference instrument in the world. European Union surveys document them in more than one hundred jurisdictions; the United States Small Business Administration has operated them since the 1950s; Brazilian federal law converted the option into a default in 2014 and extended opt-out costs in 2018 \citep{bastos2018, thai2017, bosio2022, loader2013, loader2015}. The empirical literature has documented that the rules raise procurement prices, typically by ten to fifteen percent \citep{marion2007, krasnokutskaya2011, athey2013, nakabayashi2013}. What the literature has not been able to settle is \emph{why}. When a rule restricts who can bid, two margins move simultaneously: the set of admissible firms, and the competitive pressure each remaining firm faces. The price-only data that powers most of the existing evidence cannot separate them.

The distinction is not academic. If the markup runs mostly through the extensive margin, expanding the SME roster---through subsidized registration, capacity-building programs, or looser eligibility thresholds---attenuates the policy's fiscal cost without weakening its distributive intent. If instead the markup runs mostly through the intensive margin, no roster expansion will help; the only lever is a different mechanism altogether. The choice between rules matters for the thousands of Brazilian municipalities currently implementing Law~14{,}133/2021, for the hundred-plus jurisdictions running preference regimes elsewhere, and for any buyer balancing competitive pressure against distributive policy.

This paper answers the question using a natural experiment in S\~ao Paulo state's Bolsa Eletr\^onica de Compras (BEC), Brazil's largest state-level electronic procurement platform. Between August~2014 and February~2018, medical supplies were the only product group exempted from the state's SME-only rule, under a joint interpretation of the state government and the audit court that treated them as strategic goods. In November~2017 a separate oversight agency, the Procuradoria-Geral do Estado (PGE-SP), issued a legal opinion reversing the exemption on isonomy grounds. Effective March~2018, the rule applied to medical supplies on the same terms as every other product group. The reversal was a legalistic reinterpretation internal to PGE-SP; there is no evidence it responded to price levels, competition patterns, or supplier complaints specific to medical supplies. The timing was bureaucratic, and the switch was plausibly exogenous to the procurement outcomes studied.

The paper exploits this quasi-experiment in two layers that speak to each other. A reduced-form difference-in-differences against the 76 product groups that were already under the SME-only rule delivers the causal headline: winning prices on medical supplies rose 10--11 percent after March~2018 (7--10 percent in real, IPCA-deflated terms), the number of participating firms fell 10--11 percent, the number of valid bids fell 7--8 percent, and the winning-firm-to-buyer distance rose six to fourteen kilometers. A \citet{gelbach2016} decomposition shows that entry and winner-composition channels, while individually non-trivial, offset each other and explain approximately five percent of the price effect; the remaining ninety-five percent is unaccounted for by observable channels and flows through direct within-auction bid-distribution moments that change when the rule binds. This is evidence that the markup runs primarily through how each bidder bids, not through who bids.

The structural model gives that interpretation teeth. I specify an asymmetric independent-private-values auction of the Preg\~ao (descending-clock English-reverse) format, in which each bidder's type $k \in \{\mathrm{SME}, \neg\}$ draws a private cost of supply from a type-specific distribution $F_c^k$. Because Preg\~ao is a descending-clock auction, losers' drop-out bids point-identify their costs under the standard \citet{haile2003} interpretation. Two items within the same CADMAT class can differ in ANVISA registration burden or production scale in ways the reference-price normalization cannot fully absorb, so I recover the cost primitives after shrinking out an auction-level unobserved heterogeneity component \`a la \citet{krasnokutskaya2011}. Entry is treated in the \citet{atheyseira2011} spirit. The model's counterfactual engine delivers three findings that anchor the paper.

\noindent\textbf{(i) The intensive margin dominates.} A Bayes-Nash equilibrium decomposition places 75--85 percent of the simulated set-aside price effect in the within-auction bidding shift across both pharmaceutical and non-pharmaceutical classes and across three cost-distribution estimators (all-bidders, Turnbull left-censoring, and losers-only). The between-auction composition shift contributes the remainder. The 10--11 percent reduced-form price rise, translated through the counterfactual, becomes +0.24 of the reference price in non-pharma and +0.34 in pharma, with bootstrap 95 percent intervals of $[0.19, 0.29]$ and $[0.25, 0.36]$. The conclusion is quantitatively consistent with the reduced-form Gelbach residual and sharpens it.

\noindent\textbf{(ii) Entry acts as the government's partial insurance.} Holding the SME pool at its pre-policy size while imposing the restriction---a pure intensive-margin counterfactual---delivers a price effect 162 percent of the observed effect in non-pharma and 170 percent in pharma. The induced SME entry response therefore absorbs 60--70 percent of what would otherwise be a larger latent shock. This is a finding the reduced-form DiD cannot produce, and it repurposes the extensive margin as a dampener rather than a source of the markup. It also disciplines welfare arithmetic: a fixed-pool calculation---the standard reduced-form shortcut---would materially overstate the policy's realized cost.

\noindent\textbf{(iii) The welfare ranking of set-asides versus a price preference is goods-characteristic-dependent.} Pricing allocative waste ($c_{(1)}^{S_3} - c_{(1)}^{S_1}$) and the marginal cost of public funds ($\lambda \cdot (p_{S_3} - p_{S_1})$, with $\lambda = 0.30$ after \citet{ballard1985}) places the welfare cost of the set-aside at 28.7 percent of procurement value in non-pharma (95 percent CI 21--35) and 45.5 percent in pharma (95 percent CI 35--56). A counterfactual 10 percent SME price preference delivers comparable SME win-rate gains at essentially zero price cost in thick, standardized markets. The implicit social welfare weight required to prefer the current rule over the preference exceeds three in pharma and 2.4 in non-pharma, above the 1.2--1.5 range typical of Brazilian cash-transfer programs. In thinner, more heterogeneous markets (pharma under a strict-invariance benchmark) the comparison becomes model-sensitive, which is itself a finding about the boundary where bidder-exclusion policies stop delivering proportional returns.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it delivers the first developing-country reduced-form evidence on an SME set-aside that is paired with a structural decomposition of the effect into margins, using a legally-triggered natural experiment and bid-distribution moments that the prior literature did not have access to. Second, it shows that welfare accounting for bidder-exclusion rules must include endogenous entry as a self-insurance mechanism; omitting it inflates welfare loss estimates by two-thirds. Third, it identifies a specific policy instrument---a 10 percent price preference---that Pareto-dominates the current Brazilian set-aside rule in the market class where a preference rule is administratively straightforward, and it maps the market conditions under which this dominance does and does not hold.

Five features of the design sharpen the identification. The rule change was triggered by a legalistic reinterpretation rather than by market outcomes. The treated cohort is a single product group against 76 never-treated controls, so the staggered difference-in-differences literature's concerns about forbidden implicit comparisons do not apply and the Goodman-Bacon decomposition places 100 percent of weight on a clean treated-vs-never-treated contrast \citep{goodmanbacon2021, kim2019}. A formal cross-group spillover test bounds non-SME displacement at less than one percent of the headline. Linking winners to Brazil's matched employer-employee registry (RAIS) delivers winner-level controls for firm size, age, and sector, all of which leave the coefficient invariant. And the Preg\~ao drop-out identification permits point recovery of the cost distribution, bypassing the inversion problem that constrains reduced-form studies of first-price auctions.
